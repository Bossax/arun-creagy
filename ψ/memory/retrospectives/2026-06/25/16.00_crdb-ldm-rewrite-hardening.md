## AI Diary

This session was an important reminder that a draft can fail even when it is logically coherent if it is not evidentially sharp enough for the human’s threshold. The human did not merely ask for stylistic refinement; the complaint was epistemic. The prose named PDNA without unpacking it, referred to standards without distinguishing them, and described DDPM constraints without pushing the implications hard enough. That meant the problem was not only weak writing but also insufficient analytical decomposition before writing. Once that became clear, the correct move was to stop treating the task as simple revision and instead break it into controlled extraction layers. That choice improved the output because each later sentence inherited a verified evidence packet rather than a memory of one.

[🟧 AGENT DECISION] I made the wrong choice earlier by allowing the first drafting pass to proceed before building paragraph-level source-to-claim controls, which produced generic prose and forced a more expensive rewrite cycle.

What I am taking from this is that for evidence-sensitive report writing, “draft first, sharpen later” is often the wrong order. When the human’s quality test depends on named frameworks, institutional nuance, and design traceability, the draft must be preceded by extraction architecture. The strongest part of this recovery was not the final rewrite itself, but the discipline of building separate artifacts for PDNA, standards, interoperability, DDPM context, and paragraph claim matrices. That process restored trust because it made the eventual prose auditable. It also clarified a broader writing principle: in complex institutional reports, depth is not produced by making paragraphs longer; it is produced by increasing the precision of evidence-to-claim linkage.

## Honest Feedback

Three friction points stood out. First, the earlier drafting pass created avoidable rework because the analytical staging was incomplete. The writing was serviceable on the surface, but not strong enough under adversarial review. That means the cost of moving into prose too early is high when the section must withstand line-by-line scrutiny. Second, mode selection drift created unnecessary confusion. The work bounced through architect, orchestrator, code, and report-writer contexts, and at one point even triggered an appropriate human reaction because the visible mode did not match the intellectual job being done. The content survived that drift, but the coordination overhead was real. Third, `/rrr` execution on Windows still carries command-fragility tax. Even straightforward repo/session detection required multiple command adjustments, and the missing session log meant the timeline had to remain partially degraded. None of these broke the substantive work, but they added procedural roughness that should be reduced in future sessions.

## Lessons Learned

- For sections that must compare named standards and institutional workflows, build evidence packets before writing prose; otherwise the draft will sound generic even when conceptually correct.
- If a human critique says the text lacks depth, treat that as a sign to redesign the extraction architecture, not just to expand the wording.
- Paragraph-level source-to-claim matrices are the most reliable anti-generic control for complex Thai report drafting.
- PDNA-like frameworks should be introduced through full naming, origin, objective, phase logic, and data structure before being used as design justification.
- When a workflow truly spans multiple specialties, [`orchestrator`](orchestrator:1) is the right governing mode even if the final act is prose drafting.
