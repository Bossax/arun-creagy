## AI Diary

This session had a genuine correction moment, and it's worth being honest about it rather than filing it away as routine. When Boss dropped inline `%%comment%%` annotations into the worklist file instead of just telling me in chat, I read them and immediately saw the pattern: two separate rows, same underlying mistake — I'd been treating "this indicator doesn't cover the whole activity by itself" as sufficient grounds to cut it, when the actual right question was "does anything better exist to replace it, or does cutting just delete information with nothing gained." That's not a subtle rubric-interpretation disagreement; it's a category error I made independently across two different subagent batches, which suggests it wasn't a fluke in one agent's reasoning but something closer to a bias in how I'd framed the review task for both of them upstream. I went and checked a third row on my own initiative before Boss had to point it out again, which felt like the right instinct — once you know your own failure mode, you should go looking for it rather than waiting to be told row by row. I also want to note I was glad the worklist was structured as a table with per-row reasoning rather than a prose summary, because that's exactly what made the flaw checkable and fixable in place rather than requiring a full redo. The token-efficiency plan (delegating bulk review to Sonnet subagents, reserving Perplexity for genuinely contested rows) held up fine in practice — no row needed escalation, which in hindsight makes sense given how much of the actual review turned out to be structural (crosswalk gaps, scope-inference errors) rather than factual-plausibility questions that search is suited for.

## Honest Feedback

Three friction points worth naming. First, my initial framing of "single indicator must cover full activity scope" wasn't something either subagent flagged as a concern during their own review — both batches independently reproduced pieces of it, which means the flaw was baked into my task prompt to them, not something that emerged from their individual judgment. I should have stress-tested the review criteria against a multi-habitat/multi-ecosystem activity case before delegating, rather than discovering the gap only after Boss read the output closely. Second, the crosswalk-gap finding (3 of 5 "use a different M&E indicator" verdicts had literally no alternative in the pool) only surfaced because Boss explicitly asked "which indicator?" — my first pass had used a phrase ("use a different M&E indicator") that sounded actionable but wasn't actually checked against the real candidate pool at the time I wrote it. That's a precision gap I should catch myself: don't write a specific-sounding recommendation without having actually verified a specific alternative exists. Third, working inside a single long session with heavy multi-step delegation (two background subagents, several rounds of workbook querying, plan-mode) means context is doing a lot of work to keep the crosswalk logic, the rubric weights, and the specific row IDs all consistent — a good discipline going forward would be to keep re-deriving key facts (like "does an alternative actually exist in the pool") from the source data rather than from memory of an earlier claim in the same conversation, exactly the kind of check that caught the coral/OHI/wetland-cooperation flaw.

## Lessons Learned

- When judging whether an indicator "fits" a multi-habitat or multi-ecosystem activity, judge it as a candidate member of a needed *indicator set*, not as something that must singlehandedly cover the whole activity scope. Partial coverage is not grounds to cut unless something better exists to replace it.
- Before writing "use a different M&E indicator" (or any specific-sounding recommendation), verify a concrete alternative actually exists in the relevant candidate pool — don't let a plausible-sounding phrase substitute for a checked fact.
- A reviewer's inline comments directly in a working file are a strong signal worth explicitly scanning the whole file for after any external edit is flagged — the same failure pattern can recur elsewhere in a document even if the reviewer only annotated two of the affected spots.
