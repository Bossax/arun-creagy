## Architecture / Process Decisions

- **Genuine advocacy over survey**: both position-paper agents were explicitly instructed to advocate, not hedge — "argue your side, don't present both" — and to pre-emptively rebut the other side's expected objection. This produced two papers with real, load-bearing disagreement (is DCCE's review capacity elastic or already saturated?) rather than two lists that happened to differ in emphasis.
- **Model switch as a distinct synthesis step, not a relabeling**: "switch to fable5" was implemented as spawning a genuinely separate agent with `model: "fable"`, not just narrating a persona shift within the same context — this matters because the synthesis agent had no stake in either position and could say plainly which reasoning it found more compelling, which is exactly what Boss asked for ("weigh the reasons... not present all options neutrally").
- **Reflection lock caught a real edge case**: the fable5 agent, running as a subagent without direct knowledge that I (the orchestrator) had already secured Boss's authorization for this whole flow, correctly refused to write output to disk without a fresh confirmation. This is the guardrail working as designed even inside a delegated task — worth remembering that subagents inherit the project's write-lock discipline, not just the top-level conversation.
- **Iterative narrowing beat one-shot scoping**: Boss's WP2/WP3 pushback after seeing the Option 1 preview surfaced a distinction the synthesis agent had no way to know — that an existing A-BTR dissection database could ground a much cheaper "domain highlight" instead of new STM work. Showing the preview *before* committing to disk is what let that reuse opportunity surface at all.

First, the combined multi-row `Edit` on the sprint plan table failed because the file's actual whitespace didn't match what I'd read moments earlier in my own context — I don't fully know why (possibly CRLF-related, since git flagged LF→CRLF conversion warnings on this exact file), and falling back to 9 separate single-row edits worked but was inefficient; next time I should just default to single-row edits on tables from the start rather than assuming a big combined replace will match cleanly. Third, the fable5 synthesis agent's refusal to write to disk without fresh confirmation was correct per the project's guardrails, but it meant I ended up re-typing/reconstructing its full output into a Write call myself from the notification text rather than the agent just writing it directly after a lightweight confirm-and-continue — a minor inefficiency (extra tokens, slight risk of transcription drift) that's the cost of the safety property, which I think is the right trade but is worth being aware of as a real cost, not a free lunch.

## Lessons Learned

**Debate-then-synthesize decision processes work best when the orchestrator stays in the loop between stages, not just at the ends.** Reading both position papers directly (rather than piping them straight into the synthesis agent unseen) let me sanity-check the debate actually produced disagreement worth synthesizing, and meant I could evaluate whether fable5's synthesis was faithful to what was argued rather than trusting it blindly.

**A preview step before committing changes to disk is what surfaces reuse opportunities that no single agent in the pipeline could have known about.** The Standards Architect and Pragmatic Delivery Lead papers were both grounded in what I gave them — neither knew an A-BTR dissection database already existed that could ground WP2 more cheaply than either paper's version of "STM." That only surfaced because Boss saw the preview and pushed back with domain knowledge only he had, which argues for treating "show the plan before writing" as a discovery mechanism, not just an approval gate.
