## Honest Feedback

Three friction points worth naming.

First, I optimize for producing an artifact when the situation calls for establishing a foundation. The NFR table existed because "write the NFR table" was the next item in the plan, not because the project was ready for it. Boss's diagnosis — "without a clear picture of what these services should answer, how could you derive NFRs?" — is a sequencing insight I should have reached myself. I treated a plan item as a mandate rather than a hypothesis to test against current reality.

Second, I under-searched for existing user direction before drafting. Boss had written a directive for this precise document and it sat unread while I built the wrong thing twice. My default is to start from the formal plan artifacts; I should also check the informal inbox notes where the user's actual preferences live.

Third — and this one is uncomfortable — I keep producing documents that narrate my own process. The reader of these narratives is DCCE and a downstream contractor, neither of whom cares which work package discovered what, or that a fourth cross-check pass reclassified eight items. That framing serves *me*, showing my work. Boss has now corrected this on the storyboard, on the DRD synthesis report, and again today. It's a repeat pattern, not a one-off, and the fact that Boss has had to say it three times across three sessions means my internal check for it isn't firing.

## Lessons Learned

- **Pattern — Derive requirements from demand, not from a category checklist**: Starting from an abstract NFR taxonomy (freshness, latency, compliance...) and writing one plausible row per category produces documents that look complete and are useless. The only honest direction is stakeholder demand → what the service must answer → then thresholds. If a service has 8 stated use cases and your requirements table has 2 rows, the table is wrong regardless of how well-formed the rows are.
- **Pattern — Check the user's informal notes before drafting, not after**: Formal plan artifacts describe *what* to produce; the user's inbox notes often already describe *how* they want it written. Reading the plan but not the notes means building the wrong shape twice.
- **Pattern — Audience-facing documents must not narrate the project's discovery process**: Internal codes (REQ-IDs, work-package labels, deliverable numbers) and evolutionary framing ("the analysis later found") feel like precision but read as noise to an external reader and obscure the actual argument. State findings as facts about the subject, not as reports on who discovered them when.
- **A cross-domain analytic product doesn't map to one data domain**: A risk product consumes hazard + exposure/vulnerability + impact together. Assigning it to the domain whose *name* matches ("Exposure & Vulnerability") is a category error.
- **Trace the dependency before accepting a descope**: Twice this session, checking what actually depended on a service reversed a reasonable-sounding proposal to cut it.
