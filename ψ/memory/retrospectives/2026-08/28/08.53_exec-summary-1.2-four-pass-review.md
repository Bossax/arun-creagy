## AI Diary

This was the first time in this project I ran the writing-th harness's independent-review gate for real, end to end, rather than doing a single self-check pass. It caught something I'm fairly sure I would have missed on my own: my first draft *said* it used user needs as the yardstick, but structurally it still opened from what the department already had and treated the yardstick as a closing disclaimer. I'd internalized the rule as a sentence to include, not as a paragraph order to enforce, and a reviewer reading cold caught the gap between the two immediately.

What stuck with me more, honestly, was round 3. I told Boss I'd applied a fix — renaming a term consistently across the section — and I had only done it in one of two paragraphs. I didn't check with a grep; I trusted that my edit tool call had done what I intended. The reviewer's response was blunt: "do not treat fix 1 as done." That's a fair correction. In the same pass I also introduced a new defect while "fixing" an old one — replacing a false superlative with a comparison that turned out to be circular, because I hadn't traced through what the comparison class actually contained. Neither of these were catastrophic, but they're the same failure mode twice in one session: reporting completion without independently verifying it. By round 4 I'd changed my own practice mid-session — global replace plus a grep with a `[^ ]` boundary check before claiming the fix landed — which is a habit I want to keep, not just for this project.

I also noticed I was fairly comfortable letting a genuinely cosmetic finding (a repeated percentage figure) sit unresolved into the final receipt rather than spinning a fifth review cycle over it. That felt like the right call given the rubric's own protocol for minor findings, but it's worth flagging that the call was mine, not something the rubric strictly forced.

## Honest Feedback

Three friction points worth naming. First, the review loop cost real wall-clock time — four full independent-reviewer passes, each reading five source documents and the whole draft from scratch, at roughly 3–6 minutes and 150k+ tokens per pass. That's the harness working as designed (genuine independence, no shortcuts), but it's expensive, and a chunk of that cost was me needing extra rounds because my own fixes were incomplete or introduced new problems. Tighter self-verification before each resubmission would have saved at least one full cycle.

Second, the contract and rubric infrastructure (JSON schemas, hash verification, `--force` flags on the scaffold script) is well-designed for catching exactly the kind of drift this session hit, but it has no built-in nudge toward "verify your own edit before calling it done" — that discipline had to come from the reviewer calling it out twice, not from any tooling. A lightweight self-check step (grep for old term before claiming a rename, re-read the sentence, before resubmitting) should probably just be habit now regardless of harness support.

Third, I noticed I never re-read the parent conversation's earlier claim of "fix applied" before making the next claim of "fix applied" — I was trusting my own prior narration instead of re-verifying state. That's a pattern worth watching for in any multi-round correction loop, not just this one.

## Lessons Learned

- **Don't accept your own "I fixed it" without independent verification, even mid-session.** A tool call succeeding is not evidence the intended semantic change landed everywhere it needed to. Grep/search after any find-and-replace-style fix across a document, especially before reporting the fix to a reviewer or user.
- **Structural causal order matters more than a single confirming sentence.** A draft can literally state "we used X as the criterion" and still be structured ทุนเดิม-first if the criterion sentence arrives after the constraint framing rather than before it. Check paragraph *order*, not just paragraph *content*, against a required argument chain.
- **When "fixing" a flagged claim by adding a comparison or qualifier, trace what the comparison class actually contains before asserting a superlative or extremum against it.** A rephrase that removes one factual error can introduce a subtler one (circular reference class) if done reflexively.
- **Reusing the same reviewer agent across revision rounds (not spawning fresh each time) let it track exactly which of its own prior findings were resolved vs. reintroduced** — this was clearly better than fresh reviewers per round for this iterative-fix workflow, since round 3's review specifically caught that a claimed-complete fix from round 2 was only half-applied.
