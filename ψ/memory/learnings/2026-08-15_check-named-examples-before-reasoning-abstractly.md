# Lesson: Check named examples before reasoning about them abstractly

**Date**: 2026-08-15
**Source session**: WP4 non-catalog deliverable narratives (DEL-1, DEL-11)

## The pattern

When a question names a specific example inside an abstract or philosophical framing ("does X need Y — like this Z"), the instinct is to answer the abstract question and treat the named example as illustrative, not literal. That instinct can be wrong. The named example may be a real, uninspected artifact already sitting in the project's own corpus.

## What happened

Boss asked whether website-content products need business narratives on top of D-043's 8 services, using "Thailand climatology dashboard" as an example. I answered the abstract question twice — first with an ungrounded assumption (later corrected: dashboards are pure aggregation, inheriting justification from Service 6, which turned out not even to be in this project's build scope), then with reasoning grounded in a web search about dashboard justification in general. Only when asked directly to check WP4 did I discover the "example" was DEL-1, a real, specified, partially-blocked DRD deliverable with zero justification anywhere in the project.

Two turns of abstract reasoning were spent before the concrete check happened — a check that immediately produced the actual answer (a real gap, not a thought experiment).

## Why it matters

Abstract reasoning can be entirely sound and still answer the wrong question, if the "hypothetical" turns out to be real. The cost isn't just wasted turns — it's that the genuinely interesting finding (a real, currently-unjustified deliverable) sat undiscovered while the conversation stayed at the philosophical level.

## How to apply

On any question that names a specific system, page, product, component, or artifact — even inside a clearly exploratory or "I wonder if" framing — do a quick existence check against the project corpus (grep, targeted read) before answering in general terms. If the check is cheap (as it was here — one grep), do it before answering, not after being asked to.
