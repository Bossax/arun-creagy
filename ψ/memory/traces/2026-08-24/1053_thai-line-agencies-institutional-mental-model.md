## Strengthened Institutional Model

### 1. Capacity is mandate-bounded rather than freely composable

Thai agencies often possess real expertise, datasets, models, staff, and operational reach. That capacity belongs to a legal and administrative mandate, however. It cannot be assumed to transfer into a cross-government platform merely because another agency knows it exists. The practical unit of capacity is therefore not “government as a whole” but the agency, division, system owner, and sometimes individual focal point that controls a specific asset.

### 2. Data possession, data access, and data trust are separate conditions

Oracle memory repeatedly corrects the assumption that an inventory entry means an accessible integration endpoint. An agency may possess data but lack authority to release it, may release only aggregated products, may rely on undocumented internal models, or may be uncomfortable exposing analytical methods and quality limitations. Cross-agency data work is therefore partly a negotiation over risk, reputation, sovereignty, and accountability.

### 3. Coordination occurs across peer mandates, not down one chain of command

DCCE, line agencies, governors, and local authorities occupy different chains of authority. A focal point can communicate and submit material but may not bind the parent agency. A workshop attendee can explain feasibility but may not authorize sharing or commit budget. A governor's territorial coordination role is not the same as an LAO's service jurisdiction, and neither substitutes for a line ministry's technical mandate.

### 4. Agencies adopt through administrative fit

Cross-cutting frameworks become usable when translated into the agency's existing services, departmental responsibilities, reporting cycles, and budget lines. Academic or system-wide categories can be analytically sound yet institutionally homeless. Adoption depends on answering: Which unit owns this? Which routine carries it? Which budget supports it? What existing obligation makes it recur?

### 5. Parallel system building is a rational response to weak shared authority

When no central actor can settle ownership or enforce shared methods, agencies tend to build end-to-end capabilities inside their own boundary. DPT's overlap and DDPM/DCCE pipeline issues fit this pattern. The result can be duplicated models and databases, but the behavior is not simply irrational siloing: internal control is often the safest way for an agency to meet its own mandate when inter-agency dependencies are uncertain.

### 6. Technical and domain authority are often institutionally separated

Inside DCCE, Adaptation owns policy meaning while a Mitigation-hosted database group owns infrastructure. Similar separations likely recur across government. A platform team can operate systems without authority to define domain truth; domain experts can define truth without controlling implementation. Governance must explicitly allocate decision rights across this boundary.

### 7. Transparency is politically and professionally costly

Sharing raw data, models, or analysis can expose gaps, inconsistent methods, weak documentation, or contested jurisdiction. Requests for transparency therefore create institutional risk for the source agency. Trust cannot be designed as a purely technical permission setting; it must include attribution, limits on interpretation, quality declarations, and protection of the source agency's decision rights.

### 8. Local implementation follows service responsibility and budget geography

Local Thai officials respond to categories recognizable in daily service delivery—public works, drainage, waste, health, land use, and budgets. National analytical taxonomies do not automatically map onto these structures. Spatial boundaries also differ: administrative geography, line-agency operating areas, governor-led provincial coordination, and LAO jurisdiction are not interchangeable.

### 9. Existing routines are stronger than new parallel obligations

The synthesis report's conclusion is reinforced: cooperation becomes durable when attached to an existing planning, budget, performance, statistical, or reporting routine. Voluntary focal-point coordination can start a cycle but is fragile as a permanent operating mechanism.
