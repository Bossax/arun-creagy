New Glasgow is a small town in northern Nova Scotia, Canada, with around 10,000 residents. Though it is 20 km inland, it is shaped by a tidal river system and exposed to many of the same climate dynamics that affect coastal regions, including post-tropical storm systems, high winds, prolonged power outages and river-based flooding influenced by downstream tidal conditions.

In recent years, these climate impacts have become more immediate. Storm-related disruptions, wind-driven outages and [emerging heat risks](https://www.c40knowledgehub.org/s/article/How-to-adapt-your-city-to-extreme-heat) have begun to affect municipal services and residents more frequently than in the past. At the same time, New Glasgow faces a familiar challenge for many small municipalities: many potential climate actions, but limited capacity to pursue them all.

This has required the town to be selective and pragmatic. Rather than treating climate action as a collection of standalone projects, it has adopted an integrated approach, focused on how climate risks intersect with municipal services and community well-being. This transition has also been supported internally by way of a growing organisational focus on equity, enabling a better understanding of how social conditions shape vulnerability across the community.

This case study, written by New Glasgow’s Environment and Sustainability Lead, Rohan Mishra, explores how this shift has shaped local decision-making, and offers some practical reflections for other small and mid-sized municipalities facing similar constraints.

## What’s the situation in New Glasgow?

Like many small municipalities, New Glasgow operates with limited internal capacity. Climate considerations are addressed alongside day-to-day service delivery, capital planning and community development, rather than by a large, dedicated team. Consequently, the question was not what could be done, but what mattered most for municipal services and residents.

To understand the best course of action, in 2024, New Glasgow applied a [climate risk lens](https://www.c40knowledgehub.org/s/guide-navigation?guideRecordId=a3t1Q0000007lEWQAY&guideArticleRecordId=a3s1Q000001iahxQAA) to assess the performance of municipal services. This examined near-, medium- and longer-term risks to service delivery across areas such as transportation, stormwater management, community facilities and emergency services, using a portfolio-level approach to assess both the likelihood of risks and their consequences.

The primary value of this work was not technical detail, but clarity. The assessment provided a shared basis for decision-making across departments and helped distinguish between risks that required immediate attention and those that did not ‒ an essential discipline in a small town.

**The assessment found that though core emergency response services were generally capable of responding to many expected climate events, this operational strength did not translate into equal protection for all residents.**

In particular, the screening highlighted social and equity-related vulnerability during certain climate events, especially in instances of prolonged heat, extended power outages and situations requiring temporary sheltering. While municipal services could respond effectively, some residents faced disproportionately higher risk due to factors such as housing conditions, income, age and access to cooling or transportation. This finding challenged a common assumption in municipal emergency management, that a successful operational response equates to community-wide resilience. It showed that a town could respond effectively to an event and still leave some residents exposed to harm.

[Heat risk](https://www.c40knowledgehub.org/s/article/How-to-adapt-your-city-to-extreme-heat) was especially instructive. While historically less prominent in local planning, the screening highlighted heat ‒ particularly when combined with power outages ‒ as an emerging risk for residents without access to cooling or with mobility and health constraints. These vulnerabilities were not always visible in traditional service metrics, but became clear when climate hazards were examined alongside social conditions. This understanding also built on earlier work undertaken with the Pictou County Regional Emergency Management Organization, including the development of a Heat Alert Response System, which identified vulnerable populations and coordinated responses such as cooling centres and public communication during extreme heat events.

This insight reframed how resilience was understood by the town council. It showed that resilience could not be measured solely by infrastructure performance or response capacity. It reframed [equity as a material component of climate risk](https://www.c40knowledgehub.org/s/guide-navigation?guideRecordId=a3t1Q0000007lEWQAY&guideArticleRecordId=a3s1Q000001iaiBQAQ), rather than a parallel or downstream concern. This shift in understanding has influenced how the council approaches adaptation, directing attention towards actions that could reduce vulnerability and improve access to safe spaces during climate events.## What action did New Glasgow take based on these findings?

As climate impacts became more layered in New Glasgow, it became clear that existing planning and decision tools were no longer enough on their own. The emerging challenges were not isolated. Heat events overlapped with power outages. Infrastructure stress intersected with social vulnerability. These were not problems that fit neatly within a single department or programme.

To respond, New Glasgow has increasingly started to assess climate actions for their combined effects on climate risk, equity, service delivery and the town’s long-term capacity. For a small town with limited resources, this has helped focus efforts on actions that address multiple challenges at once, rather than relying on tools designed for simpler conditions.

The identification of equity as a material climate risk, for example, prompted the council to reconsider how residents access support during climate events. Rather than expanding traditional emergency response capacity, it started to focus on how existing community assets could be used to reduce vulnerability in periods of stress.

**One example is the [Community Resilience and Comfort Hubs](https://c40.my.salesforce.com/sfc/p/36000001Enhz/a/Vo000002vkY1/di8Y2KAclDizoSkzatmdOT8fb_sHbJhy6clpfTjQvb4) that have been embedded in existing community facilities.** The hubs provide accessible, safe spaces during events such as prolonged heat, extended power outages and situations requiring temporary sheltering. To understand where hubs were most needed, the council used interactive mapping to identify areas of the town with higher social vulnerability, where residents were at greater risk of climate impacts. The approach emphasises distribution over centralisation, recognising that proximity and accessibility are critical in a small town where not all residents have reliable transportation. Importantly, this approach did not require new construction or large capital projects. Adapting facilities already in place in neighbourhoods decreased the climate risk inequity experienced in the town, while working within existing municipal responsibilities and capacity.

The Resilience hubs are just one example of the shift in how New Glasgow understands climate equity and adaptation locally. Community facilities are no longer viewed solely as sites of service delivery, but as part of the town’s social infrastructure, which could play a direct role in protecting residents during climate events.

## What can other small municipalities learn from New Glasgow’s experience?

New Glasgow’s experience is not a template. It does, however, offer several practical lessons that may be useful to other small and mid-sized municipalities working with limited capacity in changing climate conditions.

**Starting with a structured understanding of risk can help narrow priorities.** For small municipalities, clarity about what matters most is often more valuable than a long list of potential actions. [Applying a climate risk lens](https://www.c40knowledgehub.org/s/guide-navigation?guideRecordId=a3t1Q0000007lEWQAY&guideArticleRecordId=a3s1Q000001iahxQAA) to assess the resilience of different municipal services can help to build consensus on where to focus efforts.

**Climate resilience should be understood as both a technical and a social issue.** Strong infrastructure and emergency response systems do not guarantee equitable outcomes, particularly during events such as extreme heat or extended power outages. Though New Glasgow had strong response services, some of its most vulnerable residents still suffered acute risk to climate emergencies. How equity across your city affects access to technical responses is as important to consider as improving the services in and of themselves.

**Existing community assets can play a meaningful role in adaptation.** Using facilities already in pace in neighbourhoods allowed New Glasgow to strengthen resilience at low cost, without relying on large capital projects or new infrastructure. There are almost certainly assets that could be used to the same end in your city.

Together, these lessons reflect an approach grounded in risk, equity and practicality. For small municipalities facing similar pressures, this way of working may offer a useful starting point.
