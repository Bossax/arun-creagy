# 1
Q: what is the risk framework of IPCC
A: **The concept of risk** is a key aspect of how the IPCC assesses and communicates to decision-makers about the potential for adverse consequences for human or ecological systems, recognizing the diversity of values and objectives associated with those systems. In the context of climate change, risks can arise from both the potential impacts of climate change and the human responses to it.

To better support risk assessment, **a common risk framework has been implemented across all three IPCC Working Groups** in the Sixth Assessment Report (AR6). Working Group I (WGI) contributes to this common risk framing by providing relevant physical climate information tailored to regions and stakeholders.

The key components of the WGI risk framework include:

- **Climatic Impact-Drivers (CIDs):** CIDs are physical climate system conditions (such as means, events, and extremes) that affect an element of society or ecosystems. Depending on a system's tolerance, changes in CIDs can be detrimental, beneficial, or neutral. In the context of climate change risk assessment, detrimental CIDs are evaluated as "hazards".
- **Low-Likelihood, High-Impact Outcomes:** A comprehensive risk assessment must consider physical climate changes that have a low or poorly known probability of occurring, but which would have very large, severe impacts on society and ecosystems. Examples of these include ice-sheet collapse, abrupt ocean circulation changes, massive forest dieback, and certain compound extreme events.
- **Physical Climate Storylines:** To communicate climate information and address deep uncertainties, the IPCC utilizes physical climate storylines. These storylines describe plausible trajectories of weather and climate conditions—especially those related to high levels of risk or low-likelihood outcomes—allowing users to explore uncertainties and explicitly embed climate changes into their specific risk landscapes and decision-making contexts.

# 2
Q: what are the risk elements/determinants and their definitions from WGII chapter 1
A: In the context of climate change impacts, **risk** is defined as the potential for adverse consequences for human or ecological systems. This risk is driven by the dynamic interactions among three primary determinants: **hazard**, **exposure**, and **vulnerability**. These elements interact dynamically and can change across space and time in response to socioeconomic developments and decision-making.

The definitions of these specific risk elements are as follows:

- **Vulnerability** is defined as **the propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected**. It is a broad concept that encompasses elements such as a system's sensitivity or susceptibility to harm, as well as a lack of capacity to cope and adapt to climate changes.
- **Exposure** refers to **the presence of people; livelihoods; species or ecosystems; environmental functions, services, and resources; infrastructure; or economic, social, or cultural assets in places and settings that could be adversely affected**.
- **Hazards** refer to the **changing climatic conditions** (also referred to as Climatic Impact Drivers) that impact natural and human systems. Hazards are driven by current and expected future changes in the climate, including altered climate variability and shifts in the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events.

# 3
Q: how does response have to do with risk
A: In the context of climate change, societal responses—such as adaptation, mitigation, and sustainable development—are fundamentally intertwined with risk in two main ways: responses are designed to **reduce existing risks**, but they can also **generate new risks**.

**1. Responses for Risk Reduction** The primary goal of climate responses is to manage and reduce the risks posed by a changing climate. Different types of responses target different elements of risk (hazard, exposure, and vulnerability):

- **Mitigation** (such as reducing greenhouse gas emissions) aims to limit atmospheric warming, which directly reduces climate-related **hazards**.
- **Adaptation** and **sustainable development** actions generally aim to reduce the **exposure** and **vulnerability** of human and natural systems to those hazards.

**2. Risks Generated by Responses (Response Risk)** A major advancement in the IPCC's assessment of climate risk is the recognition of **response risk**, which acknowledges that human responses to climate change can themselves create novel hazards or unexpected consequences. This type of risk arises from:

- **Failure or Trade-offs:** Responses may fail to achieve their intended objectives, or they may create negative side effects and opportunity costs for other societal goals.
- **Maladaptation:** This is a specific form of response risk where adaptation actions inadvertently lead to adverse effects, such as increasing greenhouse gas emissions, locking in long-term vulnerabilities, or diminishing the welfare of certain populations either now or in the future.

Because these systems are complex, the interactions between climate risks and human responses can cascade and compound across different sectors and regions. For instance, a response designed to protect one community might inadvertently shift the risk to another community, or a short-term solution might limit the adaptation choices available to future generations. Therefore, evaluating risk now requires looking not only at the impacts of a changing climate, but also at the potential dangers introduced by our attempts to manage it.

# 4
Q: explain how response shapes future risk determinants
A: Human responses to climate change, including mitigation, adaptation, and sustainable development, dynamically interact with and modulate the future determinants of risk—hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. These responses shape future risk determinants in two primary ways: by intentionally reducing them, or by inadvertently increasing or generating new risks.

**Reducing Future Risk Determinants** The primary goal of climate responses is to lower future risks by altering one or more of its determinants. Mitigation efforts, such as greenhouse gas emissions reductions and carbon removal, directly reduce climate forcing, which in turn reduces the frequency and intensity of future climate **hazards**. Meanwhile, adaptation and sustainable development efforts are primarily designed to decrease the **exposure** and **vulnerability** of human and natural systems to these hazards.

**Generating New Risks and Complexities (Response Risk)** Conversely, responses can inadvertently increase future risk determinants, a concept known as "response risk". This occurs when responses fail to achieve their intended objectives, create negative side effects, or result in trade-offs with other societal goals.

- **Novel Hazards and Path Dependencies:** Human responses can create entirely new hazards or lead to unexpected opportunity costs and path dependencies. For example, poorly designed and costly infrastructure can create "lock-in" effects that limit the options future generations have to adapt, effectively cementing their future vulnerability and exposure.
- **Maladaptation:** This occurs when an adaptation action inadvertently leads to adverse effects, such as increasing greenhouse gas emissions (thereby increasing future **hazards**) or directly increasing the **vulnerability** of certain populations.

Ultimately, the determinants of risk vary and change across space and time directly in response to these complex societal decisions and socioeconomic developments. Because these interactions can cascade and compound across different sectors and regions, evaluating future risk requires looking not only at the changing climate but also at the systemic impacts of our responses to it.

# 5
Q: what are key risks?
A: **Key risks** are defined as **potentially severe climate risks that merit society’s full attention on both a global and regional scale**, which help inform judgements about dangerous human interference with the climate system. Because the elements of climate risk are dynamic and compounding, these key risks take on particular significance when they interact with the cultures, values, ethics, identities, and knowledge systems of the communities they affect.

The evaluation of key risks relies on expert judgement across multiple lines of evidence and is heavily shaped by societal values. The specific criteria used to evaluate the importance of a key risk include:

- The **magnitude of the adverse consequences**, paying special attention to outcomes that could trigger cascading effects, cross critical thresholds, or cause irreversible damage.
- The **likelihood and timing** of the risk.
- The **ability of a system to respond** or adapt to the risk.

The IPCC identifies several **representative key risks** that are currently emerging across different sectors and regions. These include:

- Risks to **coastal socio-ecological systems**, as well as **terrestrial and ocean ecosystems**.
- Risks to **critical infrastructure, networks, and services**.
- Risks to **living standards, human health, and cultural heritage**, reflecting a growing emphasis on the human dimensions of climate change.
- Risks to **food and water security**.
- Risks to **peace and migration**.

Because no single metric can capture the diverse consequences climate change poses to human life, the economy, and the environment, the IPCC groups these key risks into five overarching domains called **"reasons for concern" (RFCs)**. These five domains are:

1. **RFC1: Unique and threatened systems**, focusing on highly vulnerable ecosystems with low adaptive capacity, such as coral reefs and Arctic sea ice.
2. **RFC2: Extreme weather events**, including the increasing frequency of heatwaves, droughts, heavy rain, coastal flooding, and wildfires.
3. **RFC3: Distributional impacts**, which highlights the climate justice component of risk—specifically the "unfairness factor" where populations that historically contributed the least to global emissions often face disproportionate vulnerability.
4. **RFC4: Aggregate impacts**, looking at the comprehensive, global-scale damages of climate change.
5. **RFC5: Large-scale singular events**, encompassing non-linear tipping points, such as major ecosystem regime shifts or the collapse of ice sheets.

Ultimately, the assessment of these key risks conveys an increasing urgency for action, demonstrating that climate impacts are highly visible today and pose severe threats to sustainable development, poverty reduction, and global human security.

# 6
Q: Difference between Climatic impact-drivers and key risks
A: **Climatic impact-drivers (CIDs)** refer specifically to the physical conditions of the climate system, such as climatic means, events, and extremes, that affect society or ecosystems. The defining characteristic of CIDs is that they provide a **value-neutral characterization** of climate changes. Depending on the specific tolerance of an affected system, CIDs can have detrimental, beneficial, neutral, or a mixture of outcomes. The IPCC uses the term CID to describe physical changes without pre-judging whether they will inherently lead to negative impacts.

**Key risks**, on the other hand, are defined specifically by the **potential for adverse consequences** to human or ecological systems, such as negative impacts on lives, livelihoods, health, ecosystems, and economic or cultural assets. While CIDs describe the physical climate conditions alone, **risks result from the dynamic interactions between climate-related hazards, the exposure of the affected system, and its vulnerability**.

In terms of how they connect, CIDs can inform the "hazard" component of key risks. A CID only contributes to climate risk if the physical change acts as a hazard that leads to detrimental or adverse outcomes for an exposed and vulnerable system. In short, **CIDs describe the objective physical changes happening in the climate (which can be good, bad, or neutral), whereas risks describe the potential for harm** resulting from those changes.

# 7
Q: how are CIDs linked to impacts?
A: Based on the sources, Climatic impact-drivers (CIDs) are linked to impacts through the specific vulnerabilities, exposures, and tolerances of the human or ecological systems they affect.

Here is how they are connected:

- **Value-Neutral Starting Point:** CIDs are simply the physical conditions of the climate system, such as weather events, extremes, or changing climate means. Because they are value-neutral, CIDs do not automatically equate to negative impacts; instead, **their impacts depend entirely on the specific tolerance and context of the affected system**.
- **Detrimental, Beneficial, or Neutral Impacts:** Depending on the system they interact with, changes in CIDs can result in outcomes that are detrimental, beneficial, neutral, or a mixture of each.
- **Connection to Risk and Hazards:** When a CID crosses a threshold that leads to adverse consequences for society or an ecosystem, it acts as a "hazard". Impacts are the actual, realized consequences of these risks, resulting directly from the dynamic interaction between the CID (acting as a hazard), how exposed the system is, and how vulnerable or adaptable it is.
- **Interacting Drivers:** Multiple CIDs can also interact to mediate impacts. For example, drivers for climate-related risks to food security might include negative CIDs like drought and temperature extremes, but these can simultaneously be mediated by other CIDs, such as increased CO2 fertilization, which might help increase crop yields.

In short, CIDs describe the physical change in the climate, while impacts are the final consequences (whether adverse or beneficial) that occur when those physical changes interact with exposed and vulnerable systems.

Q: what is cascading risk or impact?
A: **Cascading risks or impacts** refer to situations where one climate-related event or impact triggers another in a chain reaction. They are a fundamental component of the complex nature of climate risk, illustrating how climate impacts and human responses interact dynamically across and within different regions, sectors, and systems.

Understanding cascading risks involves recognizing several key characteristics:

- **Triggering chain reactions:** A cascade specifically occurs when an initial hazard or impact acts as a catalyst, triggering subsequent vulnerabilities, exposures, or impacts in a linked sequence.
- **Non-linear and compounding interactions:** Because cascading risks unfold in complex systems with multiple stressors occurring simultaneously, the total risk is rarely just a simple addition of individual, independent risks. Instead, these interactions can compound, creating feedback loops, non-linear responses, and unexpected surprises.
- **System-wide consequences:** Cascading risks can ripple out far beyond the initial location or sector of the original hazard. Therefore, accurately understanding and evaluating these risks requires a "systems approach" rather than looking at individual impacts in isolation.

Because of their potential to cause interconnected and severe damage, the potential for an outcome to trigger cascading effects is one of the primary criteria the IPCC uses to identify and evaluate **key risks**—which are the severe climate threats that merit society's full attention on global and regional scales.
