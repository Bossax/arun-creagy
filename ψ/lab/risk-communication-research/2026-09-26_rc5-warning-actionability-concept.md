# RC5 research concept — making warnings people can act on

## 1. Why this note exists

RC5 is the root cause that sits closest to the public. Even a correct forecast fails if the warning that carries it tells people the weather, not what will happen to them or what to do. During the current heavy rain in eastern and central Thailand, this is the gap that social media weather pages fill, often with exaggerated or wrong content that people still trust more than the official bulletin.

This note sets out what the research says about why official warnings fail to move people, what has worked elsewhere, and what a fix in Thailand would need. It is meant as the design basis for a better warning message and for the agency capacity behind it. RC1 (the impact-forecasting chain) decides *what* the warning can say. RC5 decides whether people understand it, believe it, and act on it.

## 2. The problem as it shows up in Thailand

*This section is our reading of the situation. It is not sourced from the research papers.*

Official warnings in Thailand tend to arrive as formal bulletins. They give rainfall in millimetres, a percentage of area affected, and a list of provinces. They rarely say which streets will flood, when, or what a household should do first. They are written in formal government Thai and often published as PDF files or text posts.

People in flood-prone areas want an answer to a simpler question. Will my house flood tonight, and should I move the car now? Social media weather pages answer that question directly, with maps, arrows, and urgent language. They are often wrong, but they speak to the question people actually have. The official channel does not.

## 3. What the research says about bureaucratic warnings

Across the six papers, official warning systems share a recognisable style. Five features come up again and again.

**They assume information alone drives action.** Abunyewah et al. call this the *information deficit model*. It assumes people fail to prepare because they lack knowledge, so more information will fix it. The paper cites several studies showing this assumption "does not hold entirely." Awareness does not reliably turn into preparation.

**They describe the hazard, not the impact.** Dash's study of India's warning system separates "what the weather will be" from "what the weather will do." Official warnings focus on the first. They use fixed thresholds, colour codes, generic impact lines such as "damage to weak houses," and broad advice such as "suspend fishing." They are not linked to local data on who and what is exposed.

**They move top-down and use technical language.** Perera et al. describe a chain in which a technical agency warns national authorities, who pass it to regional, district, and local offices, with the media and police told last. Messages use millimetres and probabilities, in the formal national language rather than local dialects. They go out to everyone at once rather than to specific communities.

**The technical side is funded and the communication side is not.** Perera et al. and the Nordic participatory framework paper both note that early warning systems are mostly designed and run by engineers and atmospheric scientists. Monitoring and forecasting get the investment. Dissemination, preparedness, and response get far less.

**The media reinforces the same frame.** Parida et al.'s study of Cyclone Amphan coverage found that government officials were the main source in 63% of articles and experts in only 12%. Coverage was 85% neutral in tone, described the cyclone as a "natural" event, and focused on short-term relief.

The common thread across all five is that the official duty ends once the message is sent. Perera et al. quote interviews in Pakistan where officials considered their job done once they passed the warning to the next level down, without knowing whether it reached people in time.

## 4. Why these warnings fail: the three RC5 gaps

The study identified seven gaps in total. Three of them are about the warning message itself, which is the scope of RC5.

### Gap 1. People do not understand or trust the message

Messages full of technical figures and formal language do not reach people with lower literacy or who speak a different language at home. Perera et al. give the example of women in Punjabi-speaking communities in Pakistan who could not follow TV warnings given in millimetres of rain, percent probability of flooding, and formal Urdu. Even when people understand a warning, doubts about its credibility lead many to ignore it.

### Gap 2. The warning gives the hazard but not the consequences

Dash's fieldwork in Puri district, Odisha, shows this clearly. During Cyclone Jawad in 2021, the national forecast was accurate on track, rainfall, and general damage. It did not warn of the crop losses that hit farmers hardest. People said they wanted to know how long power and water would be cut and how much of their crop was at risk. The paper calls this a gap in how forecasters and citizens understand the word "impact."

The content half of this gap sits in RC1, since the warning can only state consequences that someone has calculated. The message half sits here, since even good impact information can be written in a way people cannot use.

### Gap 3. The message raises fear but not confidence to act

Abunyewah et al. explain why accurate warnings still fail to move people. People act when two things happen together. They judge the threat as serious and personal (threat appraisal), and they believe they can do something useful about it (coping appraisal, meaning confidence in their own ability to act and belief that the action will work).

Official warnings mostly deliver the first and skip the second. The result is what we can call "fear without efficacy." People feel alarmed but powerless, which leads to fatalism or to ignoring the advice.

The paper adds two more reasons. A single message for everyone persuades only some people. Drawing on Cultural Theory, it names four groups with different views of risk and authority. Hierarchists trust experts, Egalitarians distrust them, Individualists resist anything that limits their freedom, and Fatalists believe nothing can be done. A standard expert bulletin mainly reaches the first group. And when people are unsure, they turn to neighbours and community members first. Without two-way dialogue, doubts stay unresolved.

### The other four gaps

| Gap | Where it is handled |
|---|---|
| Conflicting warnings from several sources | RC2 (one public face) |
| Warnings not physically reaching people (power cuts, no signal, remote areas) | RC6 and operations |
| Technical side funded, communication side not | RC1 and agency capacity (also the basis for solution A below) |
| Media framing disasters as "natural" and relief-focused | RC4 (track record and accountability) |

## 5. What has worked elsewhere

These cases are the evidence base for the two proposed solutions that follow.

**Cuba.** Perera et al. report that the Cuban Institute of Meteorology regained public trust through three changes. It improved forecast accuracy, replaced TV presenters with trained meteorologists to deliver warnings, and communicated emergency plans and agency roles more openly to the public. The source links the regained trust to warnings coming from people with visible expertise, backed by forecasts that proved more accurate.

**Bangladesh.** The paper "Should I stay or should I go now" credits the Cyclone Preparedness Programme with cutting cyclone deaths from about 300,000 (Bhola, 1970) to about 3,000 (Sidr, 2007) and 26 (Amphan, 2020). The programme is a joint venture of the government and the Bangladesh Red Crescent Society. Around 56,000 volunteers go house to house, run drills, and use material in local languages. Its motto is "bridging the last meter," not just the last mile.

The same paper describes a second finding. Many people knew what to do before a cyclone but did not act, out of fear of being judged by their neighbours for doing something unusual. A national TV programme showed whole communities taking action together to make evacuation normal. 47% of viewers took action after watching.

**Puri district, India.** Dash found that residents already carry out their own "informal impact analysis." They combine official warnings with what they see locally (wind direction, sky colour, tides, how close they live to Chilika Lake) and with experience of how hazards combine, such as trees falling because heavy rain has first loosened their roots. The paper's point is that the ability to turn hazard into consequence already exists in communities. It is simply not used by the official system. The recommended fix is to link the district's own hazard and vulnerability analysis to the national forecast through shared work between the weather agency, the agriculture department, and the disaster management authority.

**What transfers to Thailand.** Credible expert voices delivering the warning (Cuba), a trusted local network that carries the message the last meter (Bangladesh), messages that make protective action feel normal (Bangladesh), and warnings built with local knowledge of consequences (Puri).

## 6. Proposed solution A — a shift in how government sees communication

The first change is a change of mindset. Communication needs to move from being seen as broadcasting, where the bulletin is passed down the chain and the job is done once it is sent, to being seen as a high-value task with its own body of knowledge. It is the step that decides whether a forecast saves lives.

**What the research supports.** The information deficit model, the "job done once passed down" attitude documented in Pakistan, systems designed and run almost entirely by technical staff, and the chronic underfunding of dissemination and preparedness all point the same way. Perera et al. put it directly, writing that "the production of technically sound early warnings is meaningless if it does not translate into emergency response action."

**Boss's observation (unsourced, flagged by Boss as personal and possibly biased).** In Thai agencies, turning technical information into public communication often falls to junior officers or to technical and academic staff without communication training. More recently, agencies have come to rely on AI-generated images, infographics, and articles for this work. Boss reads this as the same mindset in a new form, one that treats communication as something anyone can produce, rather than as a specialist task. None of the six papers discuss AI-generated content, so this point would need its own evidence if it is taken further.

**What the shift would look like in practice.** Communication gets a clear mandate, its own budget line, and a defined place inside the warning procedure. It becomes part of how a warning is made, not a step added after the forecast is finished.

## 7. Proposed solution B — specialist roles inside the agencies

The research names five roles or structures that close the communication gap. Each is backed by a source and, where available, a case.

| Role or structure | What it does | Source and case |
|---|---|---|
| Meteorologist-communicators | Trained forecasters deliver warnings on air themselves, instead of general presenters | Perera et al., Cuba |
| Media-trained scientists | Experts who are willing and able to talk to journalists and explain risk in public | Parida et al. (Amphan). A senior journalist said "it is very difficult to find a good scientist who is willing to talk to us... that is where policies suffer." The paper suggests building media training into scientific education. |
| Risk-communication and persuasion specialists | Staff with social-science training who design messages that build confidence to act and reach different audiences | Abunyewah et al. |
| A mandated communication and dissemination unit | A formal unit responsible for translating, localising, and targeting warnings, with standing equal to the forecasting unit | Perera et al. |
| Trusted intermediary networks | Volunteers, civil society groups, and local leaders who carry warnings the last meter and answer questions | Bangladesh CPP (about 56,000 volunteers); the same paper also cites Oxfam working through faith-based and traditional leaders in Fiji and the Solomon Islands |

Solution A is what makes solution B last. If communication is still seen as a low-value task, new posts will be filled by whoever is available, and the old pattern will continue under new job titles.

## 8. Research questions for Thailand

On agency capacity:
- Who in TMD, DDPM, and DCCE writes the public warning text today, and what training do they have?
- Does any of these agencies have a communication post with a real mandate and budget, or is the work spread across technical staff?
- How much of current public communication material is produced with AI tools, and who reviews it?

On the message:
- What consequence information do residents in the affected eastern and central provinces actually need in a flood warning?
- Which audiences do official warnings reach, and which do they miss?
- What makes people act on a warning, and what makes them wait?
- Can the message be redesigned now, using today's hazard forecasts, without waiting for the RC1 impact chain to be in place?

## 9. Possible research approach

These are options for discussion, not a finished design.

- **A review of recent warnings.** Collect TMD and DDPM warnings from the current rainfall event and assess them against the three RC5 gaps (clarity and credibility, consequence information, confidence to act).
- **Focus groups in affected provinces**, modelled on Dash's Puri study, to learn what people needed from the warning and how they decided what to do.
- **Message testing.** Draft redesigned warnings for a few real scenarios and test them with residents against the current format.
- **Interviews inside agencies** to map who produces public warnings, with what skills, and under what procedure.

Solutions A and B would fill the first gap. The second is a question of timing that the working group would need to decide.

## 11. Links to other root causes

- **RC1.** The impact-forecasting chain supplies the consequence information that a good message needs. RC1 also faces the same capacity problem on the modelling side, since a small team is being asked to carry work that needs several disciplines.
- **RC2.** One public face for warnings would stop the message being split across agencies.
- **RC3.** Dash notes that a shared warning process needs agreed rules on accountability for false alarms and over-warning. That speaks directly to the lopsided penalties in RC3.
- **RC6.** Two-way dialogue is part of the persuasion mechanism in gap 3. Without a listening channel, doubts stay unresolved.

## 12. Limits of the evidence

- The notebook holds six papers, mostly on South Asia (India, Bangladesh, Pakistan), plus Nordic and general reviews. None is about Thailand.
- None covers social media weather pages or influencers, which are central to the Thai situation.
- None covers AI-generated public communication.
- Several findings rest on a single case (Cuba) or a small field study (Puri covers a few villages in one block, as the author notes).
- New sourcing is needed for Thai evidence, for the influencer dynamic, and for the AI point in section 6.
