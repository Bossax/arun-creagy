# The psychology of differential reaction to warnings

Beyond the four Cultural Theory worldview groups already noted in earlier RC5 work (Hierarchist, Egalitarian, Individualist, Fatalist), the research names five further factors that explain why the *same* warning message produces different reactions in different people.

## 1. Direct vs. indirect disaster experience

People who have personally lived through a disaster show a reliable positive correlation between that experience and both risk perception and preparedness intention. People without direct experience form their risk perception entirely from what they hear, read, or are told by media, experts, and authorities — and for this group, risk perception does not rise automatically. It rises only if the communication source is persuasive and credible. This means the same warning can move a disaster survivor and do nothing for a first-timer, not because of the content, but because of the audience's prior experience and how they weigh the source.

## 2. Type of hazard knowledge and education pathway

Understanding a hazard is not one thing — the literature separates knowledge of a hazard's **genesis** (why it happens), its **exposure mechanisms** (how it reaches you), and its **adjustment or mitigation avenues** (what can be done about it). People can be strong on one and weak on the others. Education pathways matter too: school-based education is identified as the strongest basis for building this knowledge, ahead of self-, family-, or community-education. People with little or no hazard knowledge maintain low risk perception and take minimal protective action, regardless of how the warning is worded.

**A caution the source itself raises**: several cited studies (Ballantyne et al., Paton) found that awareness creation alone does *not* reliably translate into preparedness. Knowledge is necessary but not sufficient — which is why the next two factors matter as much as knowledge does.

## 3. Threat appraisal and coping appraisal, acting jointly

This is the mechanism already used elsewhere in this research (the "fear without efficacy" finding), now stated in full. Protective behavior depends on two separate appraisals happening *together*:

- **Threat appraisal**: the person's judgment of their own vulnerability (susceptibility) and the hazard's severity (magnitude of consequences).
- **Coping appraisal**: the person's confidence in their own ability to carry out the recommended action (**self-efficacy**), and their belief that the action will actually work (**response efficacy**).

A message that delivers threat appraisal without coping appraisal — describing how bad the hazard is without building confidence that a specific action helps — produces a "negative decision outcome": the person concludes their coping strategies are ineffective, or that the hazard is too destructive to bother adapting to. A message that builds both produces a "positive decision outcome": belief that preparation adds real value.

## 4. Critical awareness

Separate from risk perception (the *judgment* of likelihood and consequence), **critical awareness** is simply how often a person actively thinks and talks about a hazard in ordinary daily life. This has been independently confirmed as a motivational factor for preparedness — it functions as a kind of background rehearsal that keeps the hazard live in someone's mind between events, making them more receptive when a real warning arrives.

## 5. Source credibility is evaluated before message content

Receivers do not process a warning's content in a vacuum. They first assess the trustworthiness, authority, and consistency of *whoever is issuing it*. If the source is judged unpersuasive or untrustworthy, the content is discounted regardless of its accuracy — which is the mechanism behind why the same accurate warning can be trusted from one source and dismissed from another (directly relevant to the trust-inversion pattern between TMD and social media pages documented elsewhere in this project).

## 6. Communities are heterogeneous — peer validation matters

Because people interpret information differently, no single message serves an entire community. When someone is uncertain about a warning or the coping strategy it recommends, the *first* point of contact is not the issuing authority — it is other community members. Only if peers cannot resolve the doubt does the person escalate to civic agencies for clarification. This means a "negative decision outcome" can typically only be reversed through community participation and dialogue, not by repeating or amplifying the original one-way broadcast. Trust is described as the variable that mediates between this community empowerment process and the eventual intention to prepare.

## Why this matters for RC5

These six factors are the reason a single, uniform warning message structurally cannot work for an entire population. They set up the audience-segmentation framework (see the companion atomic note on message and channel design) as the direct, practical response to this psychology — segmentation isn't a stylistic preference, it's what the appraisal and worldview research requires.
