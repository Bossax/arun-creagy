# Impact forecasting as a shared chain, not a single-team task

## What changed from the root-cause note

The root-cause note named RC1 as "nobody owns turning the forecast into local impact." That is no longer accurate. TMD has a plan to move from meteorological forecasts to hazard maps and then to impact forecasts, and its climate center is tasked with impact-based forecasting. The NFCS action plan also gives warning thresholds (4.2.1.1) and impact-based forecasting (4.2.1.2) to TMD.

A better name for RC1 is **"Impact forecasting is assigned to one small team, not shared across the chain."** The gap is no longer about ownership on paper. It is about capacity, and about how the work is divided.

## Why the load is too big for one team

The TMD climate center has around 10–15 staff. It already carries climate projections, research on climate events like ENSO, and other climate data work. Impact forecasting would add the integration of:

- hydrological data and forecasts from HII and RID
- real-time satellite data from GISTDA
- hydrological, hydrodynamic, and hydraulic modelling

A flood impact forecast is mostly hydrology and exposure. Meteorology is only its first link. If one small team carries the whole chain, it has to rebuild skills other agencies already have, and it still depends on their data.

## The chain, link by link

| Link | What it produces | Who already has the capability |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Rain forecast | Where and how much rain, and when | TMD |
| 2. Runoff and river forecast | River levels and flows | HII, RID, ONWR (National Water Command Center) |
| 3. Inundation modelling | Where water spreads, how deep | HII, RID |
| 4. Real-time observation | Flood extent from satellite | GISTDA |
| 5. Exposure and vulnerability | People, assets, and risk areas in the flood's path | DCCE, DPT, NSO, local government |
| 6. Warning and action | Public alert and response orders | DDPM, NDWC, provincial governors |

TMD owns link 1. The other links sit with agencies that already have the models, data, or legal mandate.

## A possible division of work

- **TMD** owns the rain forecast and sets the warning thresholds together with the hydrology agencies.
- **HII, RID, and ONWR** own the river and inundation forecast. ONWR coordinates water situations by law and runs the National Water Command Center, so it may be the natural lead for this part (still to check).
- **GISTDA** supplies real-time flood extent to check and correct the forecast during an event.
- **DCCE** supplies the exposure and vulnerability layer and could build a library of impact scenarios ahead of each season (see below).
- **DDPM and the NDWC** turn the impact forecast into public warnings and action orders.

Someone still has to own the joining step, so that each link's output reaches the next on time. That role could stay with TMD's team, which would then coordinate the chain rather than model all of it.

## DCCE's possible role: a scenario library built before the season

DCCE may be a poor fit for real-time forecasting. It works on planning timescales, has no 24/7 operations, and does no hydrological modelling. Adding it to the live chain could make warnings slower.

It could instead work one step earlier. Before each rainy season, DCCE would combine its exposure data with inundation runs from HII and RID to build a library of pre-computed scenarios. Each scenario would say "if rain of this pattern hits this basin, these sub-districts, people, and assets are affected."

During an event, TMD's team would match the forecast to the closest ready-made scenario instead of modelling the impact live. That cuts the real-time workload of the small team and uses what DCCE already holds (exposure data, risk-area maps, and its mandate for a national climate information hub).

This idea has not been tested with DCCE.

## Using the NFCS to allocate the work

The NFCS is the right place to negotiate who does which link. The draft action plan already includes a mandate matrix and joint work plan (5.1.1.2). The working group, with TMD as chair and TMD and DCCE as joint secretariat, brings the relevant agencies into one room.

There is a limit on what it can decide. The working group sits under the climate policy committee (กนภ.). Warning operations fall under the NDPMC and the Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Act 2007. Water management falls under ONWR's structure. So the NFCS can broker and write down the division of work, but making it binding on line agencies probably needs one of these.

- The joint TMD–DDPM warning procedures (SOPs), extended to the hydrology agencies
- A decision by the NDPMC
- A formal agreement between the agencies, such as a data-sharing and roles MoU

## How this changes the suggested order in the root-cause note

Step 1 moves from "name one owner" to **"use the NFCS mandate matrix to divide the impact forecasting chain across agencies, and back it with a joint procedure that makes it binding."** RC5 (warnings are hard to act on) is a separate, message-design cause and does not need this solved first, but a redesigned warning gets better content to work with once this shared chain is in place.

## A concrete example: operational flood-map forecasting as a priority use case

This is a preliminary idea and needs more research before it goes anywhere near a pitch or a proposal.

Impact-based early warning for flood-prone metropolitan areas could be picked as the lead use case for addressing RC1, because it lines up with three pressures agency directors are already feeling, rather than asking them to care about a new one.

- **Urgency.** Directors are being blamed now, this rainy season, not in some future scenario.
- **Compliance.** It is not optional. The draft Climate Change Act (Art. 158/163) and the NFCS action plan already name TMD and DCCE as responsible for this work (4.2.1.1, 4.2.1.2).
- **Budget.** It cuts both ways. Failing to deliver risks losing a budget line, since it looks like incompetence after a bad season. Naming the gap correctly can also justify a new budget request, the same "database vacancy as roadmap" logic already noted elsewhere in this project, where an unbuilt capability becomes a costed ask instead of an admission of failure.

Framed this way, the entry point is not "help us communicate better." It is closer to "help us not get blamed, not get flagged non-compliant, and not lose our budget line, by fixing the chain that produces the flood map." That is a director-level problem, which may make it a stronger opening than the trust/communication angle explored elsewhere in this research.

**Why this example is a live illustration of RC1.** Producing one operational flood map for a metro area on a given day is a production line with several stations, each run by a different agency, using the chain in the table above (TMD rain forecast, HII/RID/ONWR hydrology, GISTDA satellite check, DCCE exposure overlay, DDPM/NDWC warning issue). Right now there is no:

- single accountable owner who signs off the finished map as the official one
- service-level agreement between stations, for example by what time TMD's forecast must land for HII to run hydrology in time for a same-day warning
- standing budget line that funds the integration work itself, separate from each agency's own mandate
- decision rule for when two agencies' models disagree, such as TMD's rain scenario against HII's river model against GISTDA's real-time satellite read

**Where this must be cleared.** Any institutional design for this chain, whether a lead-agency model, a joint operations center, or a data-hub-with-SLAs, has to be negotiated and cleared through the NFCS working group structure (TMD chair, TMD and DCCE co-secretariat), since that is the only forum that currently has standing to bring these agencies together on this exact chain. A forum is not yet an operating model. Turning it into one, meaning who does what by when and who is accountable when the map is wrong or late, is separate work.

## Open questions

- How many staff does the TMD climate center actually assign to impact forecasting, and what does its current plan cover?
- Does ONWR see flood impact forecasting as part of its role, and how does the National Water Command Center work with TMD today?
- What inundation models do HII and RID run operationally, and could their outputs feed a scenario library?
- Would DCCE take on a scenario-library role, and does its risk-area data have the detail needed (sub-district or finer)?
- Which route would make the division binding (SOP, NDPMC decision, or MoU), and who would push for it?
