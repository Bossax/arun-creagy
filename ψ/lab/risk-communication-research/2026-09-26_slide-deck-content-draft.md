## 2. The current flooding event

Since 22 September 2026, heavy rain has affected central and eastern Thailand. As of 26 September, more than 84,000 people across 21 provinces have been affected, concentrated in the central region. In Bangkok, accumulated rainfall in major canals exceeded 20 centimetres, flooding roads, disrupting traffic, and forcing evacuations. The Office of the National Water Resources warned eight eastern provinces of flooding and flash floods for the period 22–27 September; Sa Kaeo province recorded the country's highest rainfall total, 211 millimetres. The Thai Meteorological Department has forecast that heavy rain will continue, citing a low-pressure system over the central region combined with a strengthening southwest monsoon. This is the live event against which the rest of this briefing should be read.

*Source: Nation Thailand; wire reports (AP/Reuters), 26 September 2026.*

---

## 3. How public trust has shifted away from the official warning

Thailand's official flood warnings, issued through the Thai Meteorological Department (TMD), are widely perceived as slow, hedged, and difficult to act on. This perception is reinforced by TMD's own institutional position: because it is judged on both false alarms and missed events, its internal procedures favor caution, and warnings go through a hierarchical sign-off before release. Into this gap, unofficial social media weather pages have stepped in. These pages face no institutional accountability for being wrong, and their communication is often deliberately dramatic and immediate. Because people tend to remember one dramatic correct prediction and forget many incorrect ones, these pages have accumulated more day-to-day public trust than the government agency actually responsible for the forecast, even though no page publishes a real record of its accuracy. The result is a trust structure that has inverted: the actor penalized most heavily for being wrong is now trusted the least, while the actor who faces no penalty at all is trusted the most.

---

## 4. The public blames the wrong agency

When a warning fails or arrives late, public criticism lands on TMD. But TMD's institutional role is to forecast the hazard — it does not, by law, issue the public warning or order an evacuation. That authority sits with the Department of Disaster Prevention and Mitigation (DDPM), through the National Disaster Warning Centre (NDWC), which issues the warning; the response itself is then ordered by the provincial governor. Because the actual translation of "how much rain will fall" into "what will happen in your neighborhood, and what you should do" is not clearly owned by any single agency, this is exactly the gap unofficial social media sources have filled.

**Diagram (three boxes, connected in sequence, with a callout):** TMD forecasts the hazard → DDPM / NDWC legally issues the public warning → the provincial governor orders the response. Callout pointing at the first box: "The public blames TMD" — even though TMD does not own the warning or the response.

---

## 5. What each actor in this system is optimizing for

**TMD.** TMD's institutional incentive is scientific accuracy. Because it is penalized for both false alarms and misses, it prioritizes procedural caution over speed, which produces the slow, hedged warnings described above.

**DDPM.** DDPM's incentive is a visible, well-resourced response once an event occurs. It faces little direct penalty for a warning that was late or unclear, since the public associates warnings with TMD rather than with DDPM.

**The public.** Residents want early, specific, local certainty — will my house flood, and when should I act. Because preparing on a false alarm costs little but being caught unprepared costs a great deal, they are drawn toward whichever source warns earliest and most vividly.

**The social-media influencer.** An unofficial forecaster's incentive is reach and audience growth. Because these accounts face no institutional consequence for a wrong or exaggerated warning, a single dramatic correct call outweighs, in public memory, many incorrect ones.

*(DCCE is deliberately not in this list — it has no role in live disaster response; its relevance is to RC1's data/planning side, covered later.)*

---

## 6. Where these incentives collide

**1. Blame and legal mandate do not line up.** TMD, DDPM, and provincial authorities can each accurately say the failure sits somewhere else in the chain, so no single agency is compelled to fix it.

**2. No agency owns the step between hazard and consequence.** TMD holds the meteorological forecast and DDPM holds the dissemination channels — but joining these into a specific, risk-based local warning is not formally anyone's job.

**3. The penalty structure is lopsided.** TMD is penalized for both false alarms and misses; unofficial forecasters are penalized for neither. Caution costs the agency public trust, while boldness costs the unofficial forecaster nothing.

**4. No one publishes a record of accuracy.** Because neither TMD nor the unofficial pages publish how often their warnings were actually correct, public trust is shaped by memory and emotion rather than by any comparable track record.

## 7. What root cause analysis is, and why we use it here

**What it is.** Root cause analysis traces a recurring problem back to the small number of underlying structural conditions that keep producing it, instead of treating each bad event as a one-off to be individually explained away. A symptom (a late warning, a wrong evacuation call) is not itself the thing to fix — it is evidence of a structural cause that will keep generating the same symptom until the cause itself changes.

**Why here.** The same complaints reappear every rainy season — warnings seen as late, blame falling on TMD, social media filling the gap. Explaining any single bad warning would only re-explain that one event. This analysis instead asked what structural conditions make every season look like this, so the fix targets the conditions themselves rather than repeating an after-the-fact defense of one forecast.

**The naming convention.** This analysis found six distinct, separable root causes — not one vague problem — each nameable and independently explainable, though they also feed each other in reinforcing loops (shown shortly). Each is labeled **RC** (Root Cause) plus a number, purely as shorthand so the same cause can be referenced consistently across every slide that follows.

## 8. RC1 and RC2, explained in full

**RC1 — Impact forecasting overloads one small team.** Turning a rainfall forecast into a statement of local consequence — which streets will flood, how long power will be out, which crops are at risk — requires combining meteorological forecasting with hydrology, real-time satellite observation, and exposure data. TMD's climate center, the unit formally tasked with this work, has roughly 10 to 15 staff, who are also responsible for climate projections and climate-event research such as ENSO. This is a substantially larger task than the team assigned to it, and it is currently carried by that one team rather than divided across the agencies that already hold the relevant data. *Research lens: multi-agency data governance, interagency coordination, and technical workforce planning.*

**RC2 — Blame lands where the mandate isn't.** The public holds TMD responsible for warnings, but the legal authority to issue a public warning sits with DDPM's National Disaster Warning Centre, and the authority to order a response sits with the provincial governor. Because each agency's account of the failure is individually accurate, no single agency is institutionally compelled to resolve the mismatch, and the public continues to blame the agency whose name it recognizes rather than the one legally responsible. *Research lens: blame-avoidance theory in public administration, and how disaster governance structures diffuse accountability across agencies.*

---

## 9. RC3 and RC4, explained in full

**RC3 — Penalties are lopsided.** TMD faces real institutional cost for both false alarms (public and business complaints, reputational damage) and misses (blame after a disaster). Unofficial social-media forecasters face neither cost. Because TMD's procedures are built around avoiding false alarms specifically, warnings are issued more cautiously and later than they might otherwise be, which itself widens the space unofficial sources fill. *Research lens: incentive design under asymmetric loss and organizational risk-aversion in bureaucracies — the same dynamic behind the well-documented "cry wolf" effect.*

**RC4 — No public track record.** Neither TMD nor the social-media pages that compete with it publish a record of how accurate their past warnings actually were. Without this, public trust is shaped by memory rather than evidence — a single dramatic correct call is remembered for years, while many incorrect informal warnings, and the agency's actual, more consistent accuracy, both go unrecorded and forgotten. *Research lens: forecast verification science, and how publishing performance data can repair institutional trust.*

---

## 10. RC5 and RC6, explained in full

**RC5 — Warnings are hard to act on.** Official warnings are typically expressed in millimetres of rainfall, percentages of area affected, and lists of provinces, in formal government language. They rarely state what will actually happen to a specific household or what action to take and by when. Research on early-warning systems elsewhere identifies this as a general pattern: warnings describe the hazard rather than its consequences, use technical language that excludes less literate or non-native-language readers, and raise alarm without building the recipient's confidence that a specific action will help. *Research lens: risk-communication message design, and the psychology of protective action.*

**RC6 — Communication runs one way.** Agencies issue warnings but have no routine channel for hearing what people on the ground are asking, what rumors are circulating, or what actually happened after an event. Without this feedback, agencies cannot correct misinformation while it is spreading, and they gain no ground-truth information that could improve future warning thresholds. *Research lens: crisis informatics, and participatory, two-way risk-communication systems.*

## 11. How Loop A and Loop B keep the problem stuck

**Loop A — the caution spiral (a real cyclic diagram: 3 boxes, arrows looping back to the first).**
Box 1: Lopsided penalties (RC3) lead agencies to issue cautious, hedged warnings.
Box 2: These warnings feel late or vague, so public trust falls, and each error is amplified in public perception.
Box 3: Rising scrutiny increases the institutional cost of being wrong, pushing agencies toward even greater caution.
Arrow loops back from Box 3 to Box 1.
*Takeaway: this closes back on itself, which is the loop the team feels as "no matter what we do, caution gets punished."*

**Loop B — the vacuum loop (a real cyclic diagram: 4 boxes, arrows looping back to the first).**
Box 1: An under-resourced impact-forecasting capability (RC1) and a message format that is hard to act on (RC5) leave an information gap official warnings don't fill.
Box 2: Social-media pages fill that gap and gain audience share.
Box 3: Their tendency to over-warn produces warning fatigue, so the public responds more slowly to all warnings — official and unofficial alike.
Box 4: Slower response means bigger losses when a real event occurs; those losses are blamed on the official agency, trust falls, and the gap widens again.
Arrow loops back from Box 4 to Box 1.
*Takeaway: this is the loop that explains why social media keeps winning even when it's wrong more often.*

## 12. How Loop C and Loop D keep the problem stuck

**Loop C — the blame loop (a real cyclic diagram: 3 boxes, arrows looping back to the first).**
Box 1: Blame and legal mandate don't line up (RC2).
Box 2: No single agency is compelled to take ownership of fixing the underlying problem, so the impact-forecasting chain (RC1) stays undivided and under-resourced.
Box 3: The same failure recurs at the next event, and blame lands on TMD again.
Arrow loops back from Box 3 to Box 1.
*Takeaway: because no single agency owns the fix, this loop repeats at every event without resolution.*

**Loop D — the memory loop (a real cyclic diagram: 3 boxes, arrows looping back to the first).**
Box 1: No one publishes a record of forecast accuracy (RC4).
Box 2: Public trust is shaped by memory rather than evidence — an unofficial source's dramatic correct calls and an agency's occasional misses are retained in roughly equal, distorted weight.
Box 3: The current trust imbalance persists even in a period when the official agency has actually been more accurate than the unofficial sources.
Arrow loops back from Box 3 to Box 1.
*Takeaway: without a published track record, this loop holds the current trust reversal in place indefinitely.*

## 13. Which root causes can be addressed without waiting for trust to improve first

Because trust sits inside every one of the four loops above, it cannot be the starting point for a fix. Five of the six root causes, however, can be started as a decision or a design change, without needing public trust to improve first; only one depends on the others being addressed first.

| Root cause | Start without trust? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| RC1 | Yes | A staffing and division-of-labor decision. Starting it shrinks the gap in Loop B and begins to address part of RC2. |
| RC5 | Yes | A message-design choice that can start immediately, on today's forecasts. It also shrinks the gap in Loop B. |
| RC4 | Yes | A measurement and publication choice. It softens RC3 over time and breaks Loop D. |
| RC6 | Yes | A listening capability that can be built now. It feeds RC4 with ground-truth information and further shrinks the gap in Loop B. |
| RC2 | Partly | Needs an agreed procedure between TMD and DDPM — two institutions must agree, though public trust does not need to have already recovered. Fixing it breaks Loop C. |
| RC3 | No | The one root cause that eases only as the other five are addressed, since it depends on agreed thresholds and a visible track record already existing. Fixing it ends the caution spiral in Loop A. |

This is why the recommended order of attack, given at the end of this briefing, starts with RC1 and RC5 rather than with the trust problem itself.

## 14. What the National Framework for Climate Services is

The National Framework for Climate Services (NFCS) is a World Meteorological Organization–aligned governance structure that Thailand is building to coordinate climate services across agencies, sectors, and levels of government, for the human settlements and human security sector in particular. Governance of the Thai NFCS sits with a working group chaired by TMD, with TMD and the Department of Climate Change and Environment (DCCE) serving jointly as secretariat, reporting up through Thailand's national climate change policy structure.

The NFCS action plan, currently in draft, organizes its work into five goals, following the WMO's own framework:

1. **Observation and Monitoring (O&M)** — the physical sensor and data-collection networks.
2. **Climate Services Information System (CSIS)** — data governance and the systems that turn raw climate data into usable information.
3. **Research, Modelling and Prediction (RMP)** — hazard modelling and risk assessment.
4. **User Interface Platform (UIP)** — how warnings and services actually reach and are understood by the public.
5. **Governance and Capacity Development (CD)** — the working group's own institutional structure, staffing, and budget.

The activity codes referenced on the next slide are drawn from this draft plan — for example "CS-M1-A3," where the first part names the goal ("CS" for the Climate Services Information System goal, the second goal in the list above; other goals use their own prefixes, such as "RMP" or "UIP"), "M1" names the measure within that goal, and "A3" names the specific activity.

---

## 15. How the draft NFCS action plan already addresses these root causes — and where it doesn't yet

**RC1.** The draft plan already assigns much of this work: under the Climate Services Information System goal, one activity names DCCE as the lead agency for producing risk and impact data (activity CS-M1-A3), and under Research, Modelling and Prediction, one activity assigns TMD, HII, RID, and the Office of National Water Resources jointly to build water hazard models (activity RMP-M2-A1). What is still missing is a binding procedure — a standard operating procedure, a National Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Committee decision, or a memorandum of understanding — that would make this division of labor enforceable, and a clear rule for what happens when TMD's, the hydrology agencies', and GISTDA's satellite data disagree.

**RC2.** The plan's governance activities establish the NFCS working group's own structure and its national-to-local coordination mechanism (activities CD-M1-A1 and CD-M1-A2), and a separate activity calls for warning-to-action protocols between agencies (UIP-M2-A4). What is still missing is any activity that names a single public-facing spokesperson for a live warning.

**RC3.** One activity calls for agreed, impact-based warning thresholds and triggers (UIP-M2-A2), which would convert an individual official's judgment call into a protocol everyone has already signed off on. This activity, however, is scheduled for 2028–2029, meaning nothing in the current draft addresses this problem for this year's or next year's rainy season.

**RC4.** Several activities touch verification — an impact-based forecast system with built-in performance verification (UIP-M2-A3), a model and data quality-certification framework (RMP-M5-A2), and the NFCS's own monitoring and reporting system (CD-M1-A3). All three, however, are internal or policy-facing; none publishes a scorecard the public could use to compare TMD's accuracy against unofficial social-media sources.

**RC5.** Multiple activities under the User Interface Platform goal are directly relevant — impact-based thresholds (UIP-M2-A2), multi-channel dissemination including community leaders (UIP-M3-A1), an activity named specifically for risk communication capacity (UIP-M3-A3), and user co-design of services (UIP-M4-A1). None of these, however, sets an actual message-format standard, and none begins before 2028.

**RC6.** One activity calls for gathering user feedback and co-designing services (UIP-M4-A1), and another tracks the reach of warnings (UIP-M3-A2). Both, however, describe a slow, multi-year service-review cycle rather than the real-time listening function — monitoring rumors and questions during an actual live event — that this root cause requires.

## 16. RC1 in detail — the forecasting chain and who could own each link

Producing a single, usable flood warning for a specific area requires five linked steps: a rainfall forecast, produced by TMD; river and flood modelling, which depends on hydrological data currently held by the Hydro-Informatics Institute (HII), the Royal Irrigation Department (RID), and the Office of National Water Resources (ONWR); real-time satellite observation of actual flood extent, held by GISTDA; translation of the hazard into exposure and likely impact, which depends on the population and asset data DCCE holds; and finally the issuing of the warning and any evacuation order, which is DDPM's and the provincial governor's legal responsibility.

Today, TMD's small climate center is asked to carry most of this chain itself. A more workable division would have TMD continue to lead and coordinate the rainfall forecast and set thresholds jointly with the hydrology agencies; HII, RID, and ONWR — with ONWR as the natural lead, given its National Water Command Center — own the river and flood modelling; GISTDA supply real-time observation to check the forecast during an actual event; DCCE own the exposure and impact translation; and DDPM and the NDWC own the warning and response. Under this division, TMD's team shifts from modelling the entire chain itself to coordinating a chain that other agencies' own data and expertise actually run.

**Diagram: five boxes in sequence** — Rain forecast (TMD) → Hydrology & river modelling (HII, RID, ONWR) → Real-time satellite (GISTDA) → Exposure & impact (DCCE) → Warning & action (DDPM, governors).

## 17. RC1 in detail — DCCE's possible role, and the limit of what the NFCS can decide alone

DCCE is not well suited to real-time forecasting: it operates on a planning timescale, has no 24-hour operational capability, and does not run hydrological models. A better-fitting role would have DCCE build a library of pre-computed impact scenarios before each rainy season — for example, "if a rainfall pattern of this type hits this river basin, these sub-districts, populations, and assets are affected" — by combining its own exposure data with inundation modelling already run by HII and RID. During an actual event, TMD's team would then match the live forecast to the closest pre-built scenario, rather than modelling the impact from scratch under time pressure. This idea has not yet been tested with DCCE and would need to be.

Any formal division of labor along these lines needs to be negotiated through the NFCS working group, since it is the only existing forum that brings TMD, DCCE, and the relevant line agencies together specifically on this issue; the working group's mandate matrix and joint work plan (activities under CD-M1) are the natural vehicle. However, the working group sits under Thailand's national climate change policy committee, while actual warning operations sit under the National Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Committee, so a division agreed within the NFCS will likely need to be formalized through a joint standard operating procedure, a Committee decision, or a memorandum of understanding before it is actually binding on the agencies involved.

## 18. RC5, theory first — the psychology of why the same warning lands differently

Before naming any gap or solution, the psychology behind risk communication has to be understood on its own terms — because it is precisely this framework that Thai agencies currently lack.

- **Threat and coping appraisal, together.** People act only when they judge the threat as real *and* believe a specific action will help. Threat without efficacy produces paralysis, not preparedness.
- **Four worldview groups.** Hierarchists trust experts; Egalitarians distrust central authority; Individualists fear losing autonomy; Fatalists believe risk can't be managed. One message reaches only one group.
- **Direct vs. indirect experience.** Disaster survivors raise their own risk perception automatically. Everyone else depends entirely on whether the source is seen as persuasive and credible.
- **Credibility is checked first.** People assess *who* is speaking before they process *what* is said. An accurate warning from a distrusted source is still discounted.
- **Communities are heterogeneous.** When uncertain, people ask neighbors first, civic agencies second. A one-way broadcast cannot resolve doubt the way peer dialogue can.
- **Knowledge alone isn't enough.** Awareness campaigns raise knowledge but, on their own, do not reliably raise preparedness. Motivation has to be built separately.

## 19. RC5, theory first — the audience-segmentation framework

This is how to analyze an audience, given the psychology above — a framework, not yet a solution.

**Four steps to design a channel:** (1) Define the exact action needed. (2) Segment the population by ability to receive, understand, and act. (3) Match each segment to the channel it actually uses. (4) Identify the messenger each segment already trusts.

**The fear-without-efficacy fix, worked example.**
- Bureaucratic: *"Severe flooding expected; evacuation mandatory."*
- Efficacy-built: *"Severe flooding will reach 1 meter by 4 PM. Moving to the shelter at [location] via [route] takes 15 minutes and keeps your family and documents dry and safe."*

**Framing tailored to each worldview:**

| Worldview | Framing that reaches them |
|---|---|
| Hierarchist | Official data, institutional rules, expert consensus |
| Egalitarian | Collective wellbeing, mutual aid, shared responsibility |
| Individualist | Personal assets, livelihood, individual choice — not top-down mandates |
| Fatalist | Simple, achievable micro-actions with direct peer support |

Also: leverage social norms (the Bangladesh TV example, 47% acted after seeing evacuation normalized), communicate uncertainty transparently, and route through trusted intermediaries where official trust is already low.

*Source: ψ/lab/risk-communication-research/2026-09-27_atomic-message-channel-design-principles.md*

## 20. RC5 — the gap this theory reveals

Because agencies lack this psychological and audience-segmentation perspective, they do not treat communication as a specialist discipline. Instead they default to a hierarchical, one-way broadcast: a bulletin is drafted centrally and passed down the chain, with the job assigned to entry-level or administrative staff rather than trained communicators — because nobody with authority sees the gap this leaves.

The three specific symptoms already identified in this research are downstream of exactly this missing framework:

- **Comprehension & credibility** — no segmentation means one register for everyone: technical, formal, and excluding whoever it wasn't written for.
- **Hazard vs. impact specificity** — no audience analysis means no one asks what a specific resident actually needs to know to act.
- **Motivation & persuasion** — no coping-appraisal design means threat is stated without ever building confidence to act ("fear without efficacy").

*(This slide's causal framing — government lacks the framework → doesn't value communication as a discipline → defaults to hierarchical broadcasting — is Boss's own diagnostic reasoning, built on the atomic psychology note and section 6 of the RC5 concept note, not a claim taken directly from a single research paper.)*

## 21. RC5, Solution A — a shift in how government sees communication

The pattern documented in the research, and in Thailand today, is that communication is treated as broadcasting rather than expertise: a bulletin is passed down the chain, and the job is considered done once it is sent — reinforced by early-warning systems designed and led almost entirely by engineers and meteorologists, with no one in the room who holds the audience-segmentation framework just described. A technically sound warning is meaningless if it does not translate into action.

**The shift:** treat public communication as a specialist discipline with its own budget, its own mandate, and a defined place inside the warning procedure — not a task handed to whoever is free.

*Flagged as an unsourced, personal observation: in Thai agencies this task often falls to junior staff without communication training, and increasingly to AI-generated images and text — possibly the same pattern in a new form.*

---

## 22. RC5, Solution B — hire the trained specialists the shift requires

Once communication is valued as a discipline, the staffing consequence is concrete. Five roles close the gap:

| Role | Evidence and case |
|---|---|
| Meteorologist-communicators | Trained forecasters deliver warnings on air themselves — Cuba |
| Media-trained scientists | Experts willing and able to engage journalists — Cyclone Amphan study |
| Risk-comm / persuasion specialists | Social-science staff designing for confidence-to-act, not just facts |
| A mandated dissemination unit | Standing equal to the forecasting unit, not an afterthought |
| Trusted intermediary networks | Volunteers and CSOs carrying the message the last meter — Bangladesh CPP |

None of these roles will stick unless Solution A's mentality shift happens first.

---

## 23. RC5 — the bigger picture: system redesign

Solutions A and B fix who does the work and how it is valued. But the early warning system itself is still architected as a one-way broadcast chain — a forecast passed downward, with no return path for what people on the ground actually see, need, or ask. Closing RC5 fully means rethinking that architecture: not just staffing the current system better, but redesigning it toward two-way, participatory communication between agencies and the public it serves.

The Nordic participatory early warning system is a concrete, already-piloted example of what that redesign looks like in practice.

---

## 24. RC5 — closing case study: the Nordic participatory EWMS

Classic systems run expert-to-expert, one way. The Nordic pEWMS adds citizen participation across all four functions: participatory risk knowledge (adaptive, cross-cultural learning cycles), a national data hub blending official data with citizen photos and observations, a two-way web interface that pushes forecasts down and pulls local observations up into the actual models, and a response layer that co-designs action plans from a shared community risk picture.

- **Finland**: 17,412 citizen observations from 7,810 users in 2 months via the existing FMI weather app.
- **Iceland**: photo portal went fully operational during real flooding in Oct 2016, reaching remote areas with no static sensors.
- **Cross-European modelling**: crowdsourced data, even imprecise, measurably improved flood-forecast accuracy when assimilated into models.

**Honest limits:** no agency has yet wired citizen data into actual warning triggers; whether participation changes behavior remains an open research question; Nordic citizens culturally expect only authorities to monitor hazards, which itself limited uptake there. Thailand may differ — organic citizen flood-reporting on social media already happens here at scale, unlike the low starting base in the Nordic pilots.

**How it integrates, not replaces:** citizen tools are built into the agency's existing app, not a parallel system; citizen data is one more input assimilated into the agency's own models; a tiered interface keeps simplified alerts for the public while agency experts retain the raw feeds and decision authority.

*Source: ψ/lab/risk-communication-research/2026-09-27_atomic-nordic-pEWMS-case.md*

## 25. Where this suggests starting

Because five of the six root causes do not require public trust to improve before they can be started, the recommended order is: begin with RC1 (dividing the impact-forecasting chain across the agencies that already hold the relevant data) and RC5 (redesigning the warning message itself, which can start immediately on today's forecasts) together; follow with RC4 (publishing a simple, public record of forecast accuracy) and RC6 (building a real-time listening function during live events); then RC2 (agreeing who is the one public face during a warning, through the TMD–DDPM procedure work); and only then RC3, the lopsided penalty structure, which realistically only eases once agreed thresholds and a visible accuracy record are already in place.

**Diagram: six boxes in sequence** — RC1 → RC5 → RC4 → RC6 → RC2 → RC3, with RC3 visually marked as the one that eases last rather than being actively started.
