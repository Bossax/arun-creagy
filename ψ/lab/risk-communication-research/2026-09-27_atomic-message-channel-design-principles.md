# Message and channel design principles

These are the concrete design rules that follow directly from the psychology in the companion note. None of this is generic communication advice — each rule closes a specific mechanism identified in the research.

## 1. Fix "fear without efficacy" with a template, not just an instruction

Official warnings typically deliver threat appraisal (hazard severity) without coping appraisal (confidence the recipient can act, and that the action works). The fix is structural: pair every threat statement with a specific, doable action and a reason to believe it works.

Worked example from the source, contrasting the two forms:

- **Bureaucratic form**: *"Severe flooding expected; evacuation mandatory."*
- **Efficacy-built form**: *"Severe flooding will reach 1 meter by 4 PM. Moving to the community shelter at [location] via [route] takes 15 minutes and will keep your family and essential documents dry and safe."*

The second form names the timing, the destination, the effort required, and the specific benefit — it answers "can I do this" and "will it help" in the same sentence that states the threat.

## 2. Tailor framing to the four Cultural Theory worldview groups

A single message framing persuades only one of four groups. The research gives the specific angle for each:

| Worldview | What they believe | Framing that reaches them |
|---|---|---|
| **Hierarchist** | High trust in expert authority | Official scientific data, institutional rules, structural guidelines, expert consensus |
| **Egalitarian** | Skeptical of central authority | Collective community wellbeing, peer-led action, mutual aid, shared responsibility |
| **Individualist** | Fears loss of personal autonomy/freedom | Protecting personal assets, livelihood continuity, individual choice and options — not top-down mandates |
| **Fatalist** | Believes risk cannot be managed | Simple, highly achievable micro-actions, paired with direct peer support to counter helplessness |

A warning built for Hierarchists (heavy on expert authority) will specifically fail to persuade Egalitarians and Individualists, who are, respectively, skeptical of that authority and resistant to being told what to do by it.

## 3. Leverage social norms — the "fitting in" mechanism

Even when people understand a warning and know what to do, many don't act because they fear being judged by neighbors for "trying something new." This is a social-conformity barrier, not a knowledge or trust barrier, and it needs a different fix: role-modeling collective action so the behavior looks normal and shared. The Bangladesh case (see the mental-models and RC5 concept notes) demonstrated this directly — a national TV program showing whole communities evacuating together moved 47% of viewers who watched it to actually prepare.

## 4. Communicate uncertainty transparently — a second, independent lever on trust

Distinct from publishing a track record (which addresses RC4 after the fact), this is about how a *live* warning is worded: explaining what is known and what is not known, and the basis for the advisory, prevents the false-alarm perception that erodes credibility over time. Countries that handled COVID-19 communication most effectively were noted for combining high transparency with high empathy; low government trust was independently associated with worse pandemic outcomes. Trust-building is explicitly framed as a long-term investment governments have to make *before* a crisis, not during one.

## 5. The 4-step audience-segmentation process for channel design

This is the practical framework for designing who gets which message through which channel, given the psychology above:

1. **Define the action.** Specify exactly what protective action is required — not "be prepared," but the literal action (evacuate to X, move the car, shelter in place).
2. **Segment the population.** Identify how different groups vary in their physical ability to receive, comprehend, and act on a warning (literacy, language, mobility, device access).
3. **Match channels to each segment.** Select the transmission channels (TV, radio, mobile app, megaphone, door-to-door) based on each segment's actual media consumption patterns — not a single channel for everyone.
4. **Identify the trusted messenger for each segment.** Map which specific communicator holds immediate credibility for each audience — some sources need no track record (already trusted), others require a long-term investment to build credibility.

## 6. Trusted intermediaries to "bridge the last meter"

Where government trust is already low, or administrative reach is limited, warnings should route through intermediaries the audience already trusts: civil society organizations, faith-based leaders, traditional community leaders, or local figures. The Bangladesh Cyclone Preparedness Programme's ~56,000-volunteer network is the concrete instance of this. A second example: in Fiji and the Solomon Islands, Oxfam fact-checked and adapted official pandemic messaging through established local and faith-based networks, filling a role the Ministry of Health could not fill directly.

## Why this is "theory," not yet "the solution"

This note describes *how to analyze an audience and design for it* — a framework. It becomes actionable only once an institution decides to value this as a discipline (Solution A) and staffs for it (Solution B). Presented before those solutions, it establishes what a government lacking this framework is actually missing, which is the basis for the RC5 gap diagnosis.
