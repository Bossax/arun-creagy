# DCCE's implicit M&E model: project-level vs national-policy blended

## Question
Does DCCE's current M&E framework operate on a project-level logic (Monitoring and Evaluation cleanly separated, distinct functions and owners) or a national-policy logic (blended, where progress reports double as informal evaluation)?

## Evidence
The notebook explicitly warns that the traditional project-level M&E split (OECD, 2002) "does not necessarily apply in the same way to national policy M&E arrangements" — some countries blend M&E into one joint report (Austria), others fully separate them via independent expert bodies (UK, Ireland). [[q2.1]] already established the generic distinction between Monitoring and Evaluation in the literature; this note asks which model DCCE has actually, even implicitly, adopted.

Round 1 returned three distinct organizational patterns for how a blended M&E function later splits: an independent statutory body evaluates while the coordinator monitors (UK's Adaptation Sub-Committee under the CCC, Ireland); the coordinator monitors while a third party is commissioned to evaluate (Germany's UBA monitors, independent scientific investigators evaluate; Mexico similarly outsources); or an internal cross-agency committee handles evaluation without full independence (France's ONERC monitors + a convened Evaluation Committee of civil servants/scientists/NGOs evaluates; South Korea's sector-specific review committees).

Boss then flagged that none of this yet answers a prior question: "scale? project level, sectoral, regional, or national? data? primary or secondary?" — i.e. before picking which of the three organizational models fits, DCCE needs to know what scale its M&E is actually meant to operate at, and whether it's working from newly collected (primary) or already-existing (secondary) data. This is queued for Round 3.

Round 3 answered both parts. At the project or community level, keeping monitoring and evaluation separate is the older, common model, mostly built by donor requirements. At the national policy level, though, countries almost never start with separate monitoring and evaluation. They start with one central unit doing both, usually the environment ministry or a central planning office, and only formally separate the two roles once the system is already quite mature — the stage the literature calls "evaluation published." This means the three organizational models found in Round 1 are all things a national system grows into over years, not something DCCE would adopt as a starting point. On data: across the countries studied, 80% rely mainly on data that already exists in government records rather than collecting new data, specifically to avoid asking already-stretched ministries for more than they can realistically supply.

## Judgement
**Open.** The three organizational-split options above are ready for Boss's judgment on which fits DCCE's political and administrative reality — but Boss's scale/data-type question is a genuine prior gap the evidence hasn't addressed yet, not a nitpick. Also relevant to [[q2.3]]'s MEL-vs-MERL question — DCCE's actual working model may not match either the literature's default nor its own stated framework.
