# Minimal Phase 3→4 pilot design for the next TOR

## Question
What should the next TOR concretely ask for as a first Phase 3→4 pilot — how many ministries, what indicator subset, over what timeframe — within the fixed ฿3,000,000/240-day envelope?

## Evidence
The notebook recommends "one or two real reporting cycles through a small number of sector ministries," then tying reporting to an existing budget/planning instrument, but gives no Thailand-specific numbers or ministry names. [[q4.7]] confirmed the TOR should show the full picture then pick a doable slice — this note is where that slice gets defined concretely.

Round 2's full extraction of the notebook's Thailand-specific synthesis document surfaced a fact not visible in fragments: it already knows **Thailand has 137 indicators across six sectors**, and its own "Open Research Questions" section independently proposes selecting **10–15 of them for a first operational pilot** — arrived at by the corpus's own prior analysis, not by how this cycle's queries were phrased. The same document names three concrete open questions almost identical in shape to this note: which Thai budget/planning instrument could require ministry reporting each fiscal year, whether existing Thai climate-expenditure coding or BTR workflow could be extended to adaptation indicators, and which 10–15 of the 137 indicators suit a first pilot.

Two closed notes now feed this directly: [[q3.9]] confirms DCCE has already run one round of data collection (first indicator values from focal point agencies) — so the pilot design should build on and repeat that existing round, not start from zero. [[q3.4]]/[[q3.8]] confirm Thailand's budget-tagging system exists but has no climate/adaptation category — the concrete options for what to tie reporting to are: add an adaptation category to the existing tagging system, use NESDC's or another ministry's existing mandatory planning cycle the way Tonga used its planning office's quarterly report, connect with OPDC/NESDC's existing general performance-monitoring function (see [[q3.12]]), or route through Thailand's existing statistical work (Green GDP, sectoral emissions accounting, national surveys).

Round 3 gives a complete, working example that matches DCCE's situation closely: Tonga. No legal requirement for adaptation reporting, no funded position for it, and a national planning office that already ran a mandatory quarterly report from every government body. Tonga's climate unit did not build a new reporting system. It added five climate-related questions onto the report ministries already had to file, turned the answers into a plain three-way score (done, partly done, not done) for each of 92 planned activities, and only after running the cycle twice discovered the reporting schedule was too frequent — so it was changed to twice a year, based on that direct experience rather than advance planning. On what happens between collecting data and using it — the step just past where DCCE currently stands, per [[q3.9]] — the comparator pattern is consistently simple: a checklist score or plain count rather than a complex calculation, checked either by the same body that collected it or handed to an existing national statistics office (Kenya's does this for other agencies' submissions), and the resulting numbers are then used directly in a decision — revising the next plan, moving budget between projects, or changing how often to report next time, as Tonga did.

Boss added a further point that shapes the whole design. Adaptation reporting in Thailand is built around the six NAP sectors, and those six sectors are the organizing unit for everything — risk assessment, planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluation, and reporting all run through them. Every project's narrative is framed by which sector it belongs to. This means a pilot cannot be organized by agency or by theme without cutting against how the entire system already describes itself.

## Judgement
Closed. The pilot's shape is now settled by the notes above, and the two still open beneath it ([[q3.8]] and [[q3.14]]) turn out to be choices the pilot can carry rather than gaps that block it.

**Where it starts.** Not from zero. DCCE has already collected one round of indicator values from focal point agencies ([[q3.9]]), so the pilot repeats and formalizes that collection instead of designing a new one. It works from the M&E platform's own indicator groundwork rather than the GGA-mapping list, because [[q3.10]] established that the GGA side is a retrofit reporting exercise while the platform's own indicators are something DCCE actually designed. The literature's own Thailand analysis suggests 10 to 15 of the 137 indicators for a first cycle, and since the six sectors are how everything in the system is organized, those indicators should sit inside one sector rather than being scattered across several.

**What data it uses.** Only what line agencies already report. The comparator pattern (80 percent of countries work from existing government records) and Boss's own reasoning in [[q3.11]] arrive at the same conclusion, which is that new data streams need ongoing budget to maintain and a pilot cannot commit DCCE to that. For the evaluation step DCCE adds contextual information it can gather itself, rather than asking agencies for more.

**How the three functions are arranged.** Monitoring stays where it already sits, centralized at DCCE. Turning submitted data into indicator values stays deliberately simple, a plain score or count in the manner of Tonga's done, partly done, not done, checked by DCCE itself rather than an outside body. Evaluation runs through a cross-agency committee convened by DCCE, following the France arrangement chosen in [[q3.11]], working from the centralized data plus DCCE's contextual additions, with the method written so it can be handed to line agencies later. Learning is not a standing national forum. Evaluation flags where along the path from policy to results something is not converting, one learning activity runs at the sector or project closest to that point, and DCCE carries what it finds back into that specific link ([[q3.13]]).

**How many times it runs.** At least twice if the calendar allows. Tonga's most useful finding, that its reporting schedule was too frequent, came only from running the cycle a second time rather than from planning ahead. Within 240 days this is tight, so the interval between rounds is a genuine design question for the TOR to work out rather than something to fix here.

**What the pilot is for beyond its own output.** Running one real cycle is how DCCE finds out which existing government routine could carry adaptation reporting afterward, which is exactly what [[q3.8]] is still open on. The pilot should be scoped to produce that answer — where reporting actually stalls, who owns which gap, which data never arrives — instead of committing to a mainstreaming instrument in advance. That turns [[q3.8]]'s deferral from a blocker into part of the pilot's purpose.

**What the TOR has to say plainly.** That the NAP does not build on a Thailand-specific risk assessment, that "adaptation" has no shared operational definition across sectors, and that the six sectors do not share one organizing logic ([[q3.15]], [[q3.14]]). And that the pilot produces a tested method, explicitly not something ready for national enforcement, which is the guard [[q4.10]] identified against DCCE's tendency to move a freshly built method straight into operation.

**What is deliberately left out.** Full rollout across all six sectors and the 18 agencies. An independent evaluator, since no Thai precedent exists ([[q3.12]]). Any new data collection. Building local-level MEL as a new activity, since local work already happens informally ([[q4.10]]). And committing to a Phase 4 mainstreaming instrument before the pilot has shown which one could actually carry it.
