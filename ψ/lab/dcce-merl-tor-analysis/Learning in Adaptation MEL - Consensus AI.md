# Learning in **Adaptation MEL** is operationalized through **iterative review**, **structured participation**, and **embedded knowledge use**.

Learning is operationalized across three linked facets: **processes**, **tools**, and **parties/roles**. In this literature, learning is usually not a separate end-stage product; it is an iterative function built into adaptation planning, monitoring, evaluation, review, and adjustment (Goodwin & Olazabal, 2025; Van Den Berg et al., 2023; Coger et al., 2021).

## Learning Processes

Learning is most often operationalized as a **recurrent cycle**: collect information, interpret it jointly, reflect on whether actions and assumptions were right, then revise plans, practices, or institutions (Tschakert & Dietrich, 2010; Butler et al., 2016; Van Den Berg et al., 2023). The literature describes this as learning-by-doing, often with single-, double-, and sometimes triple-loop learning, where actors move from improving actions to questioning causal assumptions and even underlying norms or institutional rules (Butler et al., 2016; Gonzales-Iwanciw et al., 2020; Baird et al., 2014).

- Learning cycles usually combine **monitoring, review, and corrective action** rather than stopping at reporting (Van Den Berg et al., 2023).
- Effective learning is **explicitly designed and monitored**, not left to incidental stakeholder interaction alone (Sommerauer et al., 2026).
- Learning processes must address **power, scale, and uncertainty**, or they risk becoming extractive or superficial (Tschakert & Dietrich, 2010; Fisher & Dodman, 2019; Goodwin & Olazabal, 2025).

## Learning Tools

The tools are mixed-method and usually combine indicators with deliberative and qualitative devices that explain why outcomes happened in context (Moss & Jacobs, 2026; Mohamed & Okumu, 2025). Common tools include scenario planning, back-casting, participatory models, concept maps, social network analysis, theory of change, workshops, focus groups, direct observation, and knowledge-sharing platforms (Butler et al., 2016; Baird et al., 2014; Coger et al., 2021; Van Den Berg et al., 2023; Mohamed & Okumu, 2025).

## Parties Involved

The parties are consistently **multi-actor**: government agencies, local communities, researchers, facilitators, experts, knowledge brokers, and sometimes universities or boundary organizations that help translate between knowledge producers and users (Lucy et al., 2022; Moss & Jacobs, 2026; Lemos, 2015). Learning tends to work best when stakeholders are not only interviewed but given co-design or leadership roles, because deeper participation aligns with process-integrated learning and stronger downstream impacts (Sommerauer et al., 2026).

- **Facilitators and brokers** help create learning spaces, mediate disciplines, and sustain reflection across scales (Butler et al., 2016).
- **Sector staff and local actors** often do the practical review, data interpretation, and adaptation of interventions (Van Den Berg et al., 2023; Coger et al., 2021).
- **Administrative systems** matter because coordination, accountability, and existing knowledge flows determine whether learning is actually used (Lucy et al., 2022; Njuguna et al., 2023).

Operationally, learning in adaptation MEL is a **designed social process**: repeated review cycles, mixed tools for sense-making, and shared roles across government, experts, and affected stakeholders. The strongest recurring message is that learning becomes real when it is embedded in routine decision processes and supported by facilitation, knowledge management, and institutional fit.
