## q3.11 — scale and data source for separating monitoring from evaluation
Q1 gives a clear answer, and it changes how this note should be read. At the project or community level, monitoring and evaluation have long been kept separate — this is the older, donor-driven model. At the national level, though, countries almost never start with monitoring and evaluation as separate functions. They start blended, run by one central unit, usually the environment ministry or a central planning office. The formal separation into distinct roles — an independent body, or a third party doing evaluation — only happens once a system is already quite mature, at the stage the corpus calls "evaluation published." This means the three organizational models found in Round 1 (UK's independent body, Germany's third-party evaluation, France's internal committee) are all things a country grows into, not a starting point. For DCCE, still moving from framework design into routine use, the honest comparison is not "which of these three models should DCCE adopt now" but "DCCE is currently in the blended, single-unit stage that every comparator country passed through first, and formal separation is a later step, not an immediate one." On data: 80% of countries measuring adaptation rely mainly on data that already exists in government records rather than collecting new data, specifically to avoid asking already-stretched ministries for more than they can deliver.

## q3.13 — scale and data source for the first learning activity
Also answered directly. When resources are limited, the first learning activity almost never happens at the national scale. It happens locally, or within a single sector. Indonesia's example ran at the level of one province, testing ideas in specific districts before any attempt to change national planning. The East Africa example ran within one sector — livestock — bringing together government and non-government people to work through problems in that sector alone before trying to scale up. The one case where learning happened at the national scale from the start, Finland, was informal and unfunded — a volunteer coordination group that assessed itself, not a designed national program. This means DCCE's realistic first learning activity is more likely to work at the sector or project level than at the national level, matching the pattern in [[q3.8]] and [[q3.14]] where sector-level entry points already look more workable than trying to move all 18 agencies at once.

## q4.9 — a complete example of the whole cycle, from collecting data to using it
Q2 and Q3 together give exactly what was asked for: two full working examples, not fragments. Tonga is the clearest match to DCCE's situation — no legal requirement for adaptation reporting, no funded staff position for it, and a national planning office that already ran a routine, mandatory quarterly report from every part of government. Tonga's climate unit did not build a new reporting system. It added five questions about climate action onto the report that ministries already had to file. Those answers were turned into a plain three-way score — done, partly done, or not done — for each of 92 planned activities, and only after two rounds did anyone realize the reporting was scheduled too often, so it was changed to twice a year. That last part matters: the decision to slow down came directly from running the cycle once, not from planning in advance.

Q3 fills in the step DCCE has already reached but not gone further with — DCCE has already collected first indicator values from focal point agencies (per [[q3.9]]), so the open question is what happens next: turning that raw data into indicator values, and then using those values in an actual decision. The comparator pattern for turning raw data into indicator values is almost always simple — a checklist score, a plain rubric, or a straightforward count, not a complex calculation — and checking the data for quality is usually done either by the same central coordinating body that collected it, or by handing that check to an existing national statistics body (Kenya's national statistics office checks other agencies' submissions for errors; Uganda has a dedicated office for this). What happens to the finished numbers afterward, in every comparator case, is that they go into a decision — revising the next national plan, reallocating budget between projects, or simply changing how often the next report is due, as Tonga did.
