# Roll-up 3 — Visual plan

## Selected concept — Thailand: one changing climate, different regional pressures

**Concept:** A pale, recognisable Thailand silhouette sits behind the content as a spatial reference. Six compact regional callouts form the primary reading layer around it, each naming one dominant risk context. Two quieter modules below show why the same hazard can produce unequal burdens, and why natural coastal protection has physical limits.

**Primary message:** Climate change is shared, but its most visible risks differ by place.

**Why this concept:** It makes regional similarities and contrasts immediately legible from standing distance without pretending to be a national risk ranking. The lower modules add the human and ecological dimensions without turning the map into a dense multi-variable assessment.

**Reading order:** title and spatial claim → Thailand map and six regional callouts → same hazard, different ability to cope → ecosystems and the limits of natural protection → bridge to Roll-up 4.

## Composition blueprint

### Canvas and hierarchy

- **Format:** 60 × 160 cm vertical roll-up; working zone 8–152 cm high.
- **Grid:** inherit Roll-up 2’s 8-column grid, safe margins and rounded-card system.
- **Text handling:** all title, labels, badges, source notes and status markers remain editable in the layout application.
- **Visual hierarchy:** regional callouts are dominant; the Thailand map is a quiet spatial reference; the two lower modules are explanatory and quieter.

### A. Header — 8–25 cm

- Left: series marker `03`, working title and a one-sentence claim: `One changing climate. Different pressures in different places.`
- Right: a restrained amber heat arc and cyan rain line, inherited from Roll-up 2, crossing without a directional arrow.
- Add a compact key: `Observed`, `Projected`, `Observed + projected`.
- Do not use a choropleth legend, a score scale or a “most at risk” headline.

### B. Why impacts differ across Thailand — 25–37 cm

- Full-width pale card with the heading: `Why do climate impacts differ across Thailand?`
- Main line: `Climate conditions vary by place. Their impacts also depend on what is exposed—and on the resources, infrastructure and services people can use to prepare, cope and recover.`
- Under it, show three simple comparison tokens: `climate conditions`, `exposure`, `vulnerability and adaptive capacity`.
- This is a source-grounded reading key for the map, not a new regional taxonomy or a repeat of Roll-up 1’s full risk framework.

### C. Thailand spatial-comparison field — 37–103 cm

- Redraw the Thailand silhouette independently and validate its northern mass, central narrowing and peninsula against the project map reference.
- Treat the silhouette as a **background watermark**, not the main illustration: approximately 28–32 cm wide, pale blue-grey, with roughly 8–12% visual weight. A thin navy outline may be used only where necessary to keep the form recognisable.
- Use only muted terrain cues: northern contours, basin river lines, a small city-grid cue at Bangkok, and shallow coastal water. Do not shade entire regions as risk levels or put text inside the silhouette.
- Place three opaque callout cards to the left and three to the right, creating a clear two-column reading field around the watermark. Use six numbered dark circular badges; leader lines terminate in broad regional bands or approximate coastal/urban areas, never a province pin.

| Callout | Placement and visual cue | Editable narrative | Small source/status tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 Northern mountains | Upper left; slope contour + rain strokes | `Northern Thailand’s steep mountains, narrow valleys and headwater systems make the movement of water central to daily life. When tropical storms brought sustained rain to slopes in Chiang Rai and Chiang Mai in 2024, saturated ground gave way as landslides. The concern is disruption to hillside communities, road access and mountain ecosystems.` | `BTR/5NC · observed local example` |
| 02 Northeast | Left middle; dry soil + broken rain band | `The Korat Plateau has relatively poor soils and recurrent drought, while many livelihoods depend on rain-fed farming. Across 27 northeastern stations, annual rainfall ranged from 1,112.3 mm to 1,972.8 mm between 1993 and 2022. When rain arrives unevenly, limited water storage becomes pressure on crops, household income and food security.` | `ART04 + BTR/5NC · observed regional evidence` |
| 03 Chao Phraya Basin | Centre-left or lower left; river / overflow line | `The Chao Phraya is a low-lying, highly connected river basin and one of Thailand’s most productive agricultural areas. Sustained monsoon rain flooded the basin in 2022; when rivers overflow, water moves through farms, settlements, transport routes and water systems together. The key concern is system-wide disruption, not a single flooded site.` | `BTR/5NC · observed basin evidence` |
| 04 Bangkok Metropolitan Area | Centre-right near the Gulf; compact city grid + amber heat halo + cyan runoff | `Bangkok concentrates people, buildings, roads and services on low-lying land only about 0.5–1.5 metres above mean sea level. Dense heat-retaining surfaces, continuing subsidence and constrained drainage intensify heat and heavy-rain impacts. The result is disruption to travel, work, health and city services.` | `BTR/5NC + ART07 · city evidence` |
| 05 Eastern Gulf | Right middle coast; warm-water ripple + coral silhouette | `The eastern Gulf is part of a shallow, semi-enclosed sea that retains heat more readily than open water. In May 2024, sea-surface temperature there reached about 32.7°C, one of the highest levels recorded in the area. This marine warming puts coral ecosystems—and coastal livelihoods and tourism that depend on them—under pressure.` | `BTR/5NC · observed local evidence` |
| 06 Southern peninsula | Lower right, aligned to peninsula; monsoon cloud + coastal waterline | `Southern Thailand is a narrow peninsula between two seas, where settlements, productive land and tourism are closely tied to coasts and monsoon systems. After consecutive days of very heavy rain, severe floods occurred in late November and again in mid-December 2024. Here, heavy rain and shoreline change can disrupt local livelihoods, services and tourism together.` | `BTR/5NC + ART05 · observed regional evidence` |

**Map comparison cue:** A subtle vertical contrast rail beside the map groups `mountain`, `inland water`, `urban`, and `coastal / marine` settings. It explains why rain, heat and warming do not produce the same pressures everywhere. The cards and rail—not the map fill—carry the primary content.

### D. Same hazard, different ability to cope — 103–125 cm

- Use a full-width white rounded card with a dark navy heading: `The same hazard can create unequal burdens.`
- Show three equal-weight “hazard → unequal option” comparisons: `heat → work and travel outdoors`; `drought → reliable water or irrigation`; `coastal erosion / flooding → secure land rights and assistance`.
- A thin dotted line from the map enters this card, then splits evenly across the three comparisons. It must not connect a particular comparison to a single map region.
- Use simple, respectful contrasts—sun + bus/hat; dry field + water source; wave + home/document—rather than distressed people or disaster imagery.
- Keep the copy to one short conclusion: `Risk depends on what people can do to avoid, absorb and recover from a hazard.`

### E. Ecosystems and the limits of natural protection — 125–143 cm

- Use two adjacent pale cards, visually calmer than the map.

**Left: Thai protected areas**

- Visual: forest canopy / bird / leaf cluster, not a protected-area map.
- Copy space: `Protected-area species face growing climate pressure.`
- Reserve an editable scenario badge for the ART06 figures and label `Scenario-based · Thailand`.

**Right: coasts and nature-based protection**

- Visual: mangrove, reef and dune as three layered natural barriers; show a gradual rising waterline and a small visual “limit” marker rather than a wall.
- Copy space: `Natural barriers can reduce impacts, but they need space and suitable conditions to keep working.`
- Label `Global framing` beside the ART10 source note. Do not give it a Thailand location, rate or projection.

### F. Footer — 143–152 cm

- Left: compact source line: `Main source: Thailand BTR/5NC adaptation chapter. Supporting: ART06, ART10, ART07, ART05.`
- Right: DCCE identity area and series bridge: `Next: how do risks cascade across systems?`
- Use the inherited restrained green/footer band; no additional data graphic.

## Evidence-to-visual controls

| Item | What the visual may say | What it must not imply |
|---|---|---|
| Six regional callouts | Dominant regional risk contexts differ | a national ranking, uniform regional conditions, or province-level precision |
| Bangkok heat | Heat is widespread; dense urban settings can concentrate exposure | heat occurs only in Bangkok or all Bangkok residents experience it equally |
| Unequal burdens | Heat, drought and coastal hazards can create different options to avoid, absorb and recover | a fixed vulnerability score or a stereotype tied to a region |
| ART06 biodiversity | Scenario-based national pressure on protected-area species | every protected area faces the same outcome or a map of threatened sites |
| ART10 sea-level rise | Global slow-onset framing and physical limits of nature-based protection | Thailand-specific magnitude, timing or location |

## Style and production rules

- Retain Roll-up 2’s flat public-interest editorial illustration, navy/cyan/amber/green palette, dark circular badges, dotted connectors and generous negative space.
- Use the risk accent only in the header claim and bridge, not to colour regions.
- Combine colour with labels and distinct symbols; never make colour the only distinction.
- Give each regional card one editable narrative paragraph and one compact source/status tag. The paragraph must carry the causal sequence—local climate condition → exposed system → consequence—with any evidence cue woven into the prose, rather than presented as a fact stack. Keep cards opaque outside the silhouette. The watermark must never reduce text contrast or compete with the cards.
- Keep illustration text-free for any generated base artwork. Add every label, status marker, source and number in the controlled layout.
