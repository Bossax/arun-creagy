# Roll-up 3 — Spatial distribution evidence map

**Stage:** 1 — focused spatial evidence plan  
**Primary source:** S02 — Thailand BTR/5NC adaptation chapter  
**Purpose:** Show how climate risks and their sectoral consequences vary across Thailand’s regions. This is a comparison of dominant regional risk contexts, **not** a national risk ranking or a map of uniformly affected zones.

## Core message

Thailand faces connected climate pressures, but their dominant expression differs by place: **mountains face rainfall-triggered slope risks; the Northeast faces drought and rainfall variability; the Central Basin faces flood–drought pressure; Bangkok concentrates urban heat and flooding; eastern coasts face marine warming; and the South faces intense rain and coastal change.** These pressures affect water, agriculture, health, ecosystems, tourism, settlements and infrastructure in different combinations.

## Regional comparison for the map

| Region / map label | Climate condition | Affected systems and visible consequence | Displayable source-grounded evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **North** | **Heavy rain + steep terrain** | **Mountain ecosystems, settlements and infrastructure:** soil erosion and landslides can damage exposed systems on slopes. | **S02, observed 2024:** more than 310 landslide incidents were recorded nationwide, including Chiang Rai and Chiang Mai, after tropical storms brought sustained rain to steep terrain. | Observed + projected |
| **Northeast** | **Uneven rainfall + drought** | **Water and rain-fed agriculture:** water shortages constrain crop production, rural incomes and food security. | **ART04, 27 stations, 1993–2022:** annual Northeast rainfall ranged from **1,112.3 mm (1993)** to **1,972.8 mm (2017)**. **S02 projection:** Bueng Kan, Udon Thani, Nong Khai and Amnat Charoen are among provinces projected to face persistently high drought risk through 2100. | Observed + projected |
| **Central / Chao Phraya Basin** | **Intense rain + river overflow** | **Agriculture, settlements and infrastructure:** flooding disrupts production cycles, transport, water systems and economic activity. | **S02, observed 2022:** sustained monsoon rain flooded the Chao Phraya, Chi and Mun basins. **S02 projection, 2040–2059:** a flood level comparable to the 2006 event may recur about every **19 years (SSP2-4.5)** or **6 years (SSP5-8.5)**, rather than every 120 years. | Observed + projected |
| **Bangkok Metropolitan Area** | **Widespread heat + dense urban form; intense rain + runoff** | **Health, work, travel and city services:** heat stresses people and raises energy demand; flooding disrupts transport, infrastructure and daily economic activity. | **S02:** Bangkok is only about **0.5–1.5 m above mean sea level** and is subsiding. **ART07:** **62.7%** of respondents reported severe heat interference with daily travel. | Observed + projected |
| **Eastern Gulf coast** | **Warmer sea** | **Marine ecosystems and coastal tourism:** warmer waters drive coral bleaching, weakening ecosystem condition and destination attractiveness. | **S02, observed May 2024:** sea-surface temperature in the eastern Gulf reached about **32.7°C**. In the 2024 bleaching event, **92.3%** of coral reefs in the Gulf of Thailand were affected. | Observed + projected |
| **South** | **Intense monsoon rain + coastal change** | **Coastal communities, production and tourism:** flooding disrupts agriculture and services; storms, erosion and sea-level variability damage coastal infrastructure and livelihoods. | **S02, observed 2024:** severe southern flooding occurred in late November and again in mid-December after consecutive days of very heavy rain. **ART05, 2010–2020:** shoreline change at Nakhon Si Thammarat mangroves ranged from **−66 to +16.4 m/year**. | Observed + projected |

## Similarity and contrast to make visible

| Shared pattern | Regional contrast |
|---|---|
| **Rainfall is changing everywhere, but risk depends on terrain and setting.** | North: slope failure; Central: river flooding; Bangkok: drainage and urban runoff; South: monsoon/coastal flooding. |
| **Water stress and excess water can coexist in Thailand.** | Northeast: drought and uneven distribution; Central: flood pressure, with drought also possible; South: short-duration heavy rain and longer dry spells can both occur. |
| **Warming affects both land and sea.** | Bangkok: urban heat; Eastern Gulf: sea-surface warming and marine ecosystems; Northern/Northeastern regions: longer hot spells and warm nights. |

## Dedicated section — same hazard, different ability to cope

**Message for display:** **The same climate hazard can create unequal burdens because people do not have the same options to avoid, absorb or recover from it.**

| Situation | The specific insight | Evidence use |
|---|---|---|
| Urban heat | A person who must travel or work outdoors carries more heat exposure than someone who can stay in a cooled indoor space; access to air conditioning does not protect travel or outdoor activity. | **ART07:** Bangkok heat-stress evidence; **S02:** vulnerable-group context. |
| Agricultural drought | Drought becomes harder to manage where farming households lack reliable access to permanent water or irrigation. | **S02:** agricultural water-access and livelihood context. |
| Coastal erosion and flooding | When households lack secure land rights, protecting property and accessing compensation can be more difficult. | **ART05:** site-specific Southern Thailand tenure and compensation barriers; **S02:** coastal-community vulnerability. |

**Visual rule:** Use three short “hazard → unequal option” comparisons below the map. Do not turn them into a demographic checklist, fixed vulnerability score, or a claim that any group is inherently vulnerable.

## Dedicated section — ecosystems and the limits of natural protection

**Message for display:** **Natural coastal barriers can reduce impacts, but they are not unlimited.** Mangroves, coral reefs and dunes need suitable space, water depth and environmental conditions; sea-level rise can push them beyond their physical limits.

| Content item | Evidence status and boundary | Visual use |
|---|---|---|
| **Thai protected-area species** | **ART06:** the percentage of modelled species assessed as threatened is projected to rise from 11% currently to 35% under RCP2.6 and 54% under RCP8.5 by 2070. This is a national, scenario-based ecological context—not a protected-area map or regional ranking. | A small protected-area / biodiversity inset; label **scenario-based**. |
| **Slow-onset sea-level rise and ecosystem limits** | **ART10:** global framing on sea-level rise and the physical limits of mangroves, coral reefs and sand dunes as nature-based protection. It is not Thailand-specific spatial evidence. | A coast-and-ecosystem concept strip showing barrier → rising water → limit; label **global framing** and do not attach a Thailand number or location. |

## Map rules

- Use six labelled regional callouts; each gets one dominant risk context only.
- Do not colour the country as high, medium or low risk.
- Use arrows or subtle bands to show the north–south and inland–coastal contrast; do not imply province-level precision.
- Mark the Eastern Gulf, Central Basin and future-facing statements as **observed + projected** where space permits.
- Keep a short footer: **“Climate risk is spatial: the same changing climate produces different pressures in different places.”**
- Add two compact sections below the map: **Same hazard, different ability to cope** and **Ecosystems and the limits of natural protection**. They explain, rather than add regions to, the map.

## Deliberately outside this panel

- Detailed causal chains and cascading impacts across sectors (reserve for Roll-up 4).
- National or provincial risk rankings.
- Study-specific figures from S01 articles; they may be used in a later layout only as optional, labelled examples.
