# Roll-up 3 — Content plan

**Status:** setup complete — ready for Stage 1 evidence review

## Role in the series

Move from Roll-up 2’s changing climate conditions to the places, people, assets, ecosystems, and livelihoods that are exposed and vulnerable in different ways across Thailand.

## Working title

Where does climate risk concentrate in Thailand?

## Draft primary message

Climate pressures are spatially uneven in Thailand: each region has a different dominant risk context, shaped by its terrain, water systems, urban form, and coastal or marine setting. These spatial pressures also create unequal impacts for people, livelihoods and ecosystems.

## Audience takeaway

Climate change is shared, but its visible impacts differ by place: northern rainfall threatens mountain ecosystems and infrastructure; northeastern drought strains water and rain-fed farming; central flooding disrupts production, settlements and transport; urban heat and flooding affect health, work and city services; warmer seas pressure reefs and tourism; and southern monsoon/coastal hazards affect production, infrastructure and livelihoods.

## Message hierarchy

1. **Spatial contrast:** Thailand’s regions face different dominant climate-risk contexts.
2. **Regional comparison:** show the climate condition, affected systems and visible consequence for each selected region.
3. **Shared pattern:** changing temperature and rainfall affect all regions, but terrain and setting shape the outcome.
4. **Unequal impacts:** show that poverty, livelihood dependence, age, disability, outdoor work and uneven access to services shape who is most affected.
5. **Ecosystems and slow-onset change:** show that risk also accumulates in protected areas and coasts, not only in settlements.

## Evidence boundary for review

- **S03 — IPCC AR6 WGI/WGII extract:** risk determinants — hazard, exposure, and vulnerability — and the dynamic, place-specific nature of risk.
- **S02 — Thailand BTR/5NC adaptation chapter draft:** national topography, climate context, vulnerability patterns, infrastructure, and source-supported observed/projection statements.
- **S01 — TOR 5.5 article package:** source-verified place/sector examples. Prioritise relevant articles only after checking their verification layers.

Do not use S04 impact-chain analysis as the central framework here; reserve it for Roll-up 4.

## Candidate risk contexts to verify

These are working candidates, not map zones or rankings:

- North: heavy rain and steep terrain → landslides affecting mountain ecosystems, settlements and infrastructure.
- Northeast: uneven rainfall and drought → water stress for rain-fed agriculture, rural incomes and food security.
- Central / Chao Phraya Basin: intense rain and river overflow → flooding that disrupts production, settlements, transport and water systems.
- Bangkok Metropolitan Area: heat is widespread, but dense urban form concentrates exposure; heat and intense rain affect health, work, travel, energy demand and city services.
- Eastern Gulf coast: warmer sea → coral bleaching, marine ecosystem pressure and reduced tourism attractiveness.
- South: intense monsoon rain and coastal change → disruption to production, coastal infrastructure, tourism and livelihoods.

For every selected example, verify:

- the geography and spatial scale;
- whether the statement is observed, projected, scenario-based, or conditional;
- the relevant climate condition, exposure, and vulnerability components;
- whether it is a representative illustration rather than a national ranking.

## Intended visual role

Use a recognizable Thailand map as an organising device, with six concise regional callouts or bands. Each callout should state a **climate condition → affected systems → visible consequence**, rather than a national score or ranking. The visual should make the similarities and contrasts between regions clear without colouring the whole country as uniformly high or low risk.

## Constraints

- Do not create a national risk ranking without a comparable national dataset and method.
- Do not use hazard-only icons without a clear regional label and short consequence.
- Do not turn candidate examples into province-level claims without source verification.
- Do not repeat Roll-up 2’s CID framework or pre-empt Roll-up 4’s cascading impact chains.
- Keep final titles, labels, figures, and sources editable in the layout application.

## Dedicated cross-cutting content section

Place this below or beside the map as two compact, clearly separate modules; it must not compete with the regional comparison.

### A. Same hazard, different ability to cope

**Message:** Inequality is not an abstract list of vulnerable groups. It changes what people can actually do when risk arrives: people who must travel or work outdoors carry more heat exposure; farms without reliable water have fewer options during drought; coastal households without secure land rights can find it harder to protect property or access assistance.

**Sources:** S02 national vulnerability and water-access context; ART07 for unequal heat burden and public-transport dependence in Bangkok; ART05 for coastal tenure and compensation barriers.

### B. Ecosystems and the limits of natural protection

**Message:** Risk also accumulates in ecosystems and along coasts. Climate change can increase pressure on protected-area species. Mangroves, coral reefs and dunes can reduce coastal impacts, but they need suitable space, water depth and environmental conditions; they are not an unlimited wall against sea-level rise.

**Sources:** ART06: scenario-based projected change in threatened species in Thai protected areas; ART10: global slow-onset sea-level-rise and nature-based-solution limits. Clearly label ART10 as a global framing source, not Thailand-specific spatial evidence.
