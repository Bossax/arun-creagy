# DESIGN-02.md — Roll-up 2 Design System

## 1. Purpose

This design system governs Roll-up 2 of the five-panel climate-change-risk series.

**Working title:** What climate hazards are changing?

**Panel function:** Move from the risk framework in Roll-up 1 to the physical climate conditions that can create or intensify risk in Thailand. This panel introduces climatic impact-drivers (CIDs); it does not repeat the full risk framework or attempt to show all downstream impacts.

**Primary message:** As the climate warms, climate impact-drivers such as extreme heat, heavy rainfall, drought, sea-level rise, and compound extremes change. Their consequences in Thailand depend on location, season, exposure, and vulnerability.

## 2. Evidence and reference boundary

Use these sources in the content plan and final source note:

- **S03 — IPCC AR6 WGI/WGII extract:** definition of climatic impact-drivers and global physical-climate framing.
- **S02 — Thailand BTR/5NC adaptation chapter draft:** Thailand’s climate context, monsoon seasons, observed and projected climate information, subject to claim-level verification.
- **S01 — TOR 5.5 article package:** verified Thailand examples, used selectively after claim-level review.

Show CIDs as physical climate conditions, not as impacts by themselves. Distinguish observed conditions from projected changes, and show monsoon variability and ENSO as climate-variability context rather than a single direct consequence of global warming.

### Visual references

- `00_project/reference_images/Infographic_Climate-threat-to-the-Arctic-1024x725-1-1024x700.png` — primary drawing-style reference: flat editorial illustration, layered environmental scene, rounded information labels, dark circular badges, dotted connectors, and small narrative vignettes.
- `00_project/reference_images/istockphoto-523868158-1024x1024.jpg` — secondary reference for spacing and connector discipline only. Do not use its low-poly rendering style.
- `00_project/reference_images/thailand-travels-map-2236148789.jpg` — geographic-form reference for Thailand’s outline and north-to-south proportions. Trace or redraw the silhouette; do not embed, crop, or reproduce the watermarked stock illustration, its labels, icons, or regional content.

The IPCC figures used in Roll-up 1 are not the scientific anchor for this panel. Use original, source-checked diagrams, maps, and climate-driver illustrations for Roll-up 2.

## 3. Core style statement

Create a 60 × 160 cm vertical scientific-editorial roll-up that reads as a climate system moving from a warming backdrop to Thailand-specific climatic impact-drivers.

The visual language combines:

- flat, approachable editorial drawing;
- a layered atmosphere–land–water–settlement scene;
- strong, compact driver labels and dark circular badges;
- restrained institutional framing appropriate for a public-sector series;
- enough white space for exhibition-distance reading.

It should feel scientific, clear, contemporary, and serious without becoming a technical dashboard or a disaster poster.

Avoid photorealism, glossy 3D, low-poly rendering, generic sustainability graphics, dramatic disaster scenes, a dense Thailand risk map, and decorative environmental elements that imply unsupported regional impacts.

## 4. Narrative and reading sequence

1. **CID framework:** define CIDs as physical climate conditions, not impacts.
2. **Thailand climate context:** tropical monsoon setting, with seasonal and regional variation.
3. **Changing climatic impact-drivers:** extreme heat, heavy rainfall, drought/rainfall variability, sea-level rise or coastal/ocean change, and compound extremes.
4. **Why this matters for risk:** CIDs become hazards when they exceed a system’s tolerance and interact with exposure and vulnerability.
5. **Bridge forward:** the next panel locates where exposure and vulnerability concentrate in Thailand.

Use a short definition near the top:

> Climatic impact-drivers are physical climate conditions — means, events, and extremes — that affect people and ecosystems.

Do not call every CID a hazard. Use language such as “can become hazardous when…” to retain the IPCC distinction.

## 5. Composition system

### 5.1 Canvas and grid

- Vertical roll-up: 60 × 160 cm.
- Keep critical content at least 4 cm from the sides and 8–10 cm from top and bottom hardware zones.
- Use an 8-column grid, 6–7% outer margins, 2–3% gutters, and 3–5% vertical spacing.
- Allow landscape bands and illustrations to overlap zones gently, but align text cards to the grid.

### 5.2 Vertical zones

#### Zone A — Header: the question

Approx. 12–16 cm.

- Series marker: `02`
- Title: `What climate hazards are changing?`
- One-line subtitle that frames changing climatic impact-drivers in Thailand.
- A small atmosphere/heat/sun motif at the edge; keep the title area quiet.

#### Zone B — CID framework card

Approx. 20–24 cm. This is the first major explanatory module after the title.

Content:

- Heading: `What are climatic impact-drivers (CIDs)?`
- Definition: `Physical climate conditions — means, events, and extremes — that affect people and ecosystems.`
- Three linked, illustrated capsules: `Climate means` / `Climate events` / `Climate extremes`.
- Closing relationship: `In a harmful context, a CID is evaluated as a hazard.`

Treatment:

- Use a wide white rounded card with a navy heading, five small weather-condition symbols, and a dotted connector that leads downward into the Thailand examples.
- Make the distinction visually explicit: a CID card on the left, then a small arrow labelled `in a harmful context`, then a compact `hazard` chip. Do not include impacts or sector icons in this card.
- This module is conceptual and source-led; it should read before the viewer encounters the country illustration.

#### Zone C — Global-to-Thailand climate context

Approx. 14–18 cm.

Show a concise visual transition, not a causal pipeline:

- warming atmosphere and warmer ocean as broad context;
- Thailand within a tropical monsoon setting;
- curved seasonal wind, cloud, or ocean-current cues;
- a small ENSO/monsoon variability note only if claim wording is verified.

Use a broad curved path or layered bands. Do not draw a single arrow from a global globe to one Thai outcome.

#### Zone D — Main illustrated climate-driver field

Approx. 62–72 cm; this is the dominant visual.

Use a simplified, recognizable Thailand silhouette as the organising form, checked against `thailand-travels-map-2236148789.jpg` for its north-to-south proportions and southern peninsula. Redraw it in the project’s flat editorial language. Place five CID modules around or within it:

- **Extreme heat** — sun/heat bands and urban or field heat vignette.
- **Heavy rainfall** — cloudburst, intense rain, river or drainage cue.
- **Drought and rainfall variability** — dry soil, reservoir, crop, or uneven seasonal-rain cue.
- **Sea-level rise and coastal/ocean change** — coastal waterline, wave, mangrove, or marine cue.
- **Compound extremes** — two or more climate conditions interacting; use overlapping weather symbols rather than an unsupported impact chain.

Each module needs a short label, one plain-language line, and a clear visual distinction. Use only source-verified regional examples; otherwise show the driver without a regional claim.

#### Zone E — CID-to-risk bridge

Approx. 18–22 cm.

Use a simple band:

`Changing climate conditions → become hazards when they meet exposure and vulnerability → climate risk`

Use this as a reminder of Roll-up 1, not a repeat of its diagram. Show people, ecosystems, services, and infrastructure as small neutral silhouettes without assigning them to specific Thai regions.

#### Zone F — Footer and series handoff

Approx. 8–10 cm.

- Source note and observed/projected caveat.
- DCCE identity area, if required.
- A small forward cue: `Next: where does risk concentrate in Thailand?`
- Use a quiet landscape, waterline, or city silhouette as a base strip without competing with the drivers.

## 6. Illustration and information rules

### 6.1 Drawing style

Follow the Arctic reference’s flat editorial language:

- soft, simplified vector shapes;
- limited dark outlines or darker-toned object edges;
- layered atmosphere, land, water, settlement, and ecosystem bands;
- rounded label capsules, dark circular badges, and dotted connectors;
- simple people, crops, buildings, roads, wetlands, and coastlines;
- strong silhouettes and minimal internal detail.

Keep the illustration informative, not playful. Environmental scenes provide context; CID modules and their labels provide the scientific content.

### 6.2 Information-card rules

- Treat the CID framework card as the panel’s primary explanatory element; the Thailand driver modules are examples of the framework.
- Give every CID a direct label; do not rely on icon recognition alone.
- Use rounded cards or capsules and one short explanatory sentence.
- Use dotted connectors only for relationships that need explanation; keep them thick enough for print.
- Use dark circular badges for an essential term or verified number only. Do not invent statistics to fill badges.
- Mark observed and projected content explicitly when both appear.
- Keep causal language conditional where evidence is conditional.

### 6.3 Do not show

- a country-wide uniform drought, flood, or heat condition;
- the stock travel-map artwork, watermark, tourist icons, labels, or regional content from the geographic-form reference;
- an unverified provincial hotspot map;
- a direct line from ENSO to every Thai climate event;
- impacts such as health, crop loss, or infrastructure failure as if they were CIDs;
- final Thai/English labels embedded in generated imagery.

## 7. Color system

Preserve series continuity with the existing scientific and institutional palette, using flatter illustrated shapes and muted derived tints.

| Role | Color | Use |
|---|---:|---|
| Deep climate navy | `#17375E` | Headings, outlines, climate-system context, dark badges |
| Climate cyan | `#00A6CF` | Rain, water, atmosphere, coastal/ocean cues |
| Heat / dry amber | `#F2A000` | Heat, drought, and warning emphasis |
| Response amber-brown | `#C97A00` | Use sparingly for linked risk-response context only |
| Risk magenta | `#8A1B61` | Reserve for the small Roll-up 1 risk bridge; never use decoratively |
| DCCE dark green | `#165A3C` | Footer, institutional framing, restrained landscape accents |
| DCCE bright green | `#239B45` | Positive action or forward-series cue only |
| Climate light grey | `#F1F2F3` | Main canvas background |
| Scientific white | `#FFFFFF` | Cards, negative space, high-contrast fields |
| Annotation grey | `#8A8A8A` | Secondary labels, dotted connectors, sources |
| IPCC blue | `#4A90D6` | Header rules and secondary atmosphere accents |

Use pale derived tints for scenery. Do not introduce an unrelated Arctic-reference palette, use saturated rainbow weather coding, or let magenta compete with the CID modules.

## 8. Typography

Use a modern scientific sans-serif throughout, with an optional restrained editorial serif for the main title only.

- Title: Source Serif 4, Merriweather, Noto Serif, Plus Jakarta Sans, Inter, or equivalent.
- Labels/body: DM Sans, Inter, Aptos, or equivalent.
- Title must read from about 2 m; keep callout body short and large enough for standing viewers.
- Target total visible copy: about 100–140 words, excluding sources.
- Use direct labels, short declarative sentences, and no text over busy illustrations.

## 9. Draft copy guidance

### Title

What climate hazards are changing?

### Subtitle

Warming changes the climate conditions that shape risk in Thailand.

### CID definition

Climate impact-drivers are physical climate conditions — means, events, and extremes — that affect people and ecosystems.

### Driver labels

- Extreme heat
- Heavy rainfall
- Drought and rainfall variability
- Sea-level rise and coastal/ocean change
- Compound extremes

### Bottom takeaway

These changing conditions do not create the same risk everywhere. Risk depends on where people, ecosystems, and assets are exposed and vulnerable.

## 10. Layout / generation handoff vocabulary

Use:

- vertical 60 × 160 cm scientific editorial roll-up;
- Thailand climate system and climatic impact-drivers;
- flat editorial vector illustration;
- layered atmosphere, monsoon cloud, land, river, city, farm, coast, and ocean bands;
- rounded information capsules;
- dark circular emphasis badges;
- dotted connectors;
- five differentiated climate-driver modules;
- clear top-to-bottom narrative;
- pale grey background; navy, cyan, amber, restrained green palette;
- clear blank areas for controlled typography and sources.

Avoid:

- full IPCC risk diagram;
- low-poly/faceted style;
- photorealism or glossy 3D;
- generic sustainability poster;
- dense technical dashboard;
- unsupported maps, numbers, or regional claims;
- single straight-line causal arrows;
- final text embedded in the artwork.

## 11. Quality checklist

### Content

- [ ] CIDs are presented as physical climate conditions, not impacts.
- [ ] The CID framework card appears before Thailand-specific examples and clearly distinguishes a CID from a hazard.
- [ ] Observed, projected, seasonal, regional, and conditional statements are distinguished.
- [ ] ENSO and monsoon context are not depicted as a simple one-way causal chain.
- [ ] The panel does not repeat Roll-up 1 or pre-empt Roll-ups 3–4.

### Composition

- [ ] The panel reads CID framework → climate context → Thailand examples → risk bridge.
- [ ] Five CID modules are differentiated and legible from exhibition distance.
- [ ] The Thai organising form is recognizable without making unsupported regional claims.
- [ ] Thailand’s outline and southern peninsula have been checked against the geographic-form reference and redrawn independently.
- [ ] There is adequate space for title, labels, caveats, and source note.

### Style and accessibility

- [ ] Flat editorial illustration, rounded labels, badges, and dotted connectors are consistent.
- [ ] The palette remains coherent with Roll-up 1.
- [ ] Labels do not rely on colour alone.
- [ ] Illustration never obscures text or scientific distinctions.

## 12. Final style summary

Roll-up 2 should first establish the CID framework, then make it concrete through a Thailand weather-and-climate story: a warming atmosphere and ocean form the context, differentiated climatic impact-drivers become the examples, and a small risk bridge connects back to Roll-up 1. The illustration is warm, flat, and editorial, but the science remains disciplined: climate drivers are not impacts, variation is visible, and every Thailand-specific claim is source-checked.
