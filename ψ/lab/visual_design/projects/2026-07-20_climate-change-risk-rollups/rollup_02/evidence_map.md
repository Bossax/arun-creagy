# Roll-up 2 — Evidence map

## Evidence boundary

This panel explains changing **climatic impact-drivers (CIDs)** in Thailand. It does not assess where risk is greatest, quantify sector impacts, or show a full impact chain; those functions belong to later panels.

## Selected claims

### E01 — Climatic impact-drivers are physical climate conditions

**Claim:** CIDs are physical climate-system conditions — including means, events, and extremes — that affect elements of society or ecosystems. They are not automatically harmful; when detrimental in a specific context, they are evaluated as hazards.

**Source:** S03, `IPCC-AR6-WGI-WGII-extract.md`, sections 1 and 6–7.

**Evidence strength:** High.

**Visual use:** A brief definition under the title; five driver modules treated as physical conditions rather than as impact categories.

**Visual caution:** Do not label every CID as a disaster or impact. Use “can become hazardous when…” in the bridge to risk.

---

### E02 — Thailand experienced exceptional heat in 2024

**Claim:** Thailand’s 2024 annual mean temperature was 28.5°C, 1.1°C above the 1991–2020 climatological normal, and the highest annual mean temperature in the 1951–2024 record.

**Source:** S02, `260527_UNDP_BTR2_second_interim_report.md`, Temperature Trends, p. / text block beginning “Thailand experiences three main seasons”.

**Evidence strength:** High within the BTR draft.

**Visual use:** Primary observed-data badge for the **Extreme heat** module: `2024: +1.1°C above the 1991–2020 normal`.

**Visual caution:** Keep the comparison basis and time period attached. Do not convert this national annual-mean figure into a claim about every location or every heatwave.

---

### E03 — Rainfall is projected to become more variable and more intense in many places

**Claim:** Under CMIP6 downscaled projections, annual precipitation increases across Thailand, while rainfall is projected to occur in more intense, shorter-duration events and longer dry spells may occur in some periods and places.

**Source:** S02, `260527_UNDP_BTR2_second_interim_report.md`, Precipitation Trends.

**Evidence strength:** High for the stated projection framing.

**Visual use:** **Heavy rainfall** and **Drought / rainfall variability** modules connected by one seasonal-rain band; use a small `projected` marker.

**Visual caution:** Do not imply a contradiction between more annual rainfall and drought. Show uneven timing and distribution, not a uniform national wetting or drying story.

---

### E04 — Monsoon variability and ENSO contribute to drought conditions

**Claim:** Drought conditions in Thailand are influenced by climate variability, including southwest-monsoon variability, the position of the monsoon trough, and El Niño; these can reduce rainfall and create uneven spatial and temporal distribution.

**Source:** S02, `260527_UNDP_BTR2_second_interim_report.md`, Drought.

**Evidence strength:** High for the stated contributors.

**Visual use:** A small contextual note near the rainfall-variability module: `Monsoon variability and ENSO can shape when and where rain falls.`

**Visual caution:** Do not show ENSO as a direct cause of every Thai drought or flood. Do not use unverified numerical ENSO claims from ART04; its verified-facts file remains a placeholder scaffold.

---

### E05 — Thailand’s coastal waters are warming

**Claim:** The BTR reports a long-term warming signal in sea-surface temperature in Thai waters. In May 2024, parts of the eastern Gulf of Thailand reached about 32.7°C.

**Source:** S02, `260527_UNDP_BTR2_second_interim_report.md`, Sea Surface Temperature.

**Evidence strength:** High within the BTR draft.

**Visual use:** **Coastal / ocean change** module; optional observed-data badge: `Eastern Gulf, May 2024: ~32.7°C`.

**Visual caution:** Present this as sea-surface temperature, not as a nationwide ocean condition or direct proof of a particular ecosystem impact.

---

### E06 — Sea-level rise varies across Thailand’s coast

**Claim:** Sea level along Thailand’s coast has risen, but the rate differs by location and period because relative sea level reflects both global sea-level change and local land-level change. Future projections also vary spatially.

**Source:** S02, `260527_UNDP_BTR2_second_interim_report.md`, Sea Level Rise.

**Evidence strength:** High for the qualified statement.

**Visual use:** Add a slow-onset sea-level line or waterline to the **Coastal / ocean change** module; label it `slow onset · spatially variable`.

**Visual caution:** Avoid one national rate, a uniform inundation coastline, or a Bangladesh/Fiji example from ART10 as if it were Thailand evidence.

---

### E07 — Climate drivers can compound

**Claim:** Multiple CIDs can interact. The BTR identifies compound flooding from sea-level rise, precipitation, and river discharge as an example of interacting climate conditions.

**Source:** S03, `IPCC-AR6-WGI-WGII-extract.md`, section 7; S02, `260527_UNDP_BTR2_second_interim_report.md`, Sea Level Rise.

**Evidence strength:** High for the general principle and cited Thailand example.

**Visual use:** A fifth **Compound extremes** module showing overlapping weather and water symbols, with the short label `Conditions can interact`.

**Visual caution:** Keep this conceptual. Do not build an impact chain or assign compound risk hotspots; that belongs to Roll-up 4.

## Evidence-to-visual selection

| Driver module | Claim IDs | Display status | Recommended treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extreme heat | E01, E02 | Observed | Sun / heat-wave illustration and one 2024 badge |
| Heavy rainfall | E01, E03 | Projected | Cloudburst / intense-rain illustration and `projected` tag |
| Drought and rainfall variability | E01, E03, E04 | Mixed | Dry-period illustration linked to uneven seasonal rain; no nationwide drought map |
| Coastal and ocean change | E01, E05, E06 | Mixed | Warm-water and rising-waterline illustration; label spatial variation |
| Compound extremes | E01, E07 | Conceptual | Overlapping driver symbols; no downstream impact chain |

## Messages not to carry on this panel

- Thailand-specific vulnerability, livelihood, infrastructure, and health impacts as the main story.
- Provincial or regional risk rankings.
- The full hazard–exposure–vulnerability diagram.
- Detailed ENSO correlations or lag statistics from ART04 before its verification layer is populated.
- Exact future flood-return periods or impact estimates; these are too detailed for this panel and combine climate with exposure and vulnerability.
