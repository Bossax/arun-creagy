# Evidence Map — Roll-up 1

## Roll-up 1 focus

Working title:
What is climate change risk?

Primary message:
Climate risk is not the same as climate hazard. Risk emerges when climate hazards interact with exposed people, assets, ecosystems, livelihoods, infrastructure, and the vulnerability or adaptive capacity of those systems. Human responses can reduce risk, but poorly designed responses can also create new risks.

Role in five-roll-up story:
This panel defines the conceptual language that later panels use: hazard, exposure, vulnerability, impacts, response risk, maladaptation, and cascading risk.

## Claim E01 — Climate risk means potential adverse consequences

Claim:
In the IPCC framing, risk is the potential for adverse consequences for human or ecological systems. Climate risks arise from both climate-change impacts and human responses to climate change.

Source:
S03, Q1 and Q2.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Large central word `RISK` surrounded by consequence icons: people, ecosystems, infrastructure, livelihoods, health.

Visual caution:
Do not define risk as the climate hazard alone. Risk should be shown as consequence potential, not only as weather events.

Display-language candidate:
Climate risk is the chance of harm to people, ecosystems, infrastructure, livelihoods, and services.

---

## Claim E02 — Risk is produced by hazard, exposure, and vulnerability

Claim:
Climate risk is driven by dynamic interactions among three core determinants: hazard, exposure, and vulnerability.

Source:
S03, Q2.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Risk triangle or three interlocking circles:

- Hazard: changing climatic conditions / climate impact-drivers when detrimental.
- Exposure: people, livelihoods, ecosystems, infrastructure, assets, and services located where they can be affected.
- Vulnerability: propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected, including sensitivity and lack of coping/adaptive capacity.

Visual caution:
Avoid using a simple equation that implies fixed arithmetic. The source emphasizes dynamic interaction across space and time.

Display-language candidate:
Risk rises when a hazard meets exposed and vulnerable systems.

## Claim E03 — Climatic impact-drivers are physical climate conditions; they become hazards when detrimental

Claim:
Climatic impact-drivers are physical climate system conditions such as means, events, and extremes. They are value-neutral until they affect a system; detrimental CIDs are treated as hazards in risk assessment.

Source:
S03, Q1, Q6, Q7.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Small upper module: `Climate impact-driver → hazard when harmful`, with examples such as heat, heavy rainfall, drought, sea-level rise, storms.

Visual caution:
Do not show every climate driver as inherently negative for all places or systems. Impact depends on system tolerance, exposure, and vulnerability.

Display-language candidate:
A climate driver becomes a hazard when it can harm an exposed system.

## Claim E04 — Exposure includes people, livelihoods, ecosystems, infrastructure, and assets

Claim:
Exposure refers to the presence of people, livelihoods, species or ecosystems, environmental functions/services/resources, infrastructure, and economic/social/cultural assets in places and settings that could be adversely affected.

Source:
S03, Q2.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Exposure node with icons for household, farmer/livelihood, road or power line, ecosystem, tourism/cultural asset.

Visual caution:
Do not reduce exposure to population only. The definition includes natural systems, infrastructure, services, and assets.

Display-language candidate:
Exposure is what sits in harm’s way.

---

## Claim E05 — Vulnerability includes sensitivity and lack of capacity to cope and adapt

Claim:
Vulnerability is the propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected. It includes sensitivity or susceptibility to harm and lack of capacity to cope and adapt.

Source:
S03, Q2.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Vulnerability node with two sub-components: `sensitivity` and `limited coping/adaptive capacity`.

Visual caution:
Avoid depicting vulnerability as an inherent trait of people alone. It is shaped by social, economic, institutional, infrastructure, ecological, and capacity conditions.

Display-language candidate:
Vulnerability is why the same hazard harms some systems more than others.

## Claim E06 — Responses can reduce risk or generate response risk

Claim:
Societal responses such as mitigation, adaptation, and sustainable development are linked to risk in two ways: they can reduce existing/future risk, but they can also generate new risks through failure, trade-offs, path dependency, or maladaptation.

Source:
S03, Q3 and Q4.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Two-way response module below the risk triangle:

- Good response: lowers hazard, exposure, or vulnerability.
- Poor response: creates trade-offs, lock-in, maladaptation, or shifted risk.

Visual caution:
Do not show adaptation as automatically positive. The point of Roll-up 1 is that response quality matters.

Display-language candidate:
Responses can reduce risk — or create new risks if poorly designed.

---

## Claim E07 — Maladaptation is a specific form of response risk

Claim:
Maladaptation occurs when adaptation actions inadvertently increase greenhouse gas emissions, lock in long-term vulnerabilities, diminish welfare, or shift/increase risks for some groups or future generations.

Source:
S03, Q3 and Q4.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
High-emphasis callout attached to the response area of the IPCC AR6 diagram:

`Maladaptation = response that creates more risk`

Use an amber/orange warning panel, lock-in icon, and forked arrow showing:

- intended response path → reduced risk;
- maladaptive path → increased future vulnerability / shifted risk.

Visual caution:
Do not overload Roll-up 1 with detailed examples. The callout should clearly state the concept and consequence: some responses can increase or shift risk.

Display-language candidate:
Maladaptation is when a response meant to help ends up increasing or shifting risk.

## Claim E08 — Risk determinants change over space and time

Claim:
Hazard, exposure, and vulnerability interact dynamically and change across space and time in response to socioeconomic developments and decisions.

Source:
S03, Q2 and Q4.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Subtle time arrow or shifting layered background behind the risk triangle: `today → future`.

Risk changes as climate, development, and decisions change.

## Claim E09 — Cascading risk requires a systems approach

Claim:
Cascading risks or impacts occur when one climate-related event or impact triggers another in a chain reaction across sectors, regions, or systems.

Source:
S03, Q8.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Small preview strip: `hazard/impact → linked impacts → system-wide risk`, pointing toward later panels.

Do not make cascading risk the dominant Roll-up 1 visual. It should preview later panels.

Climate risks can cascade through connected systems.

## Claim E10 — Key risks make the risk framework concrete

Claim:
Key risks are potentially severe climate risks that merit society’s full attention at global and regional scales. They are evaluated using criteria such as magnitude of adverse consequences, likelihood and timing, and the ability of systems to respond or adapt.

Source:
S03, Q5.

Evidence strength:
Strong.

Potential visual:
Compact “key risks” example module below or beside the main IPCC diagram. Use 4–5 icon chips for representative domains:

- coastal socio-ecological systems;
- terrestrial and ocean ecosystems;
- critical infrastructure, networks, and services;
- living standards, human health, and cultural heritage;
- food and water security.

Visual caution:
Do not present the example domains as a complete risk assessment for Thailand. They are illustrative IPCC key-risk examples, used to make the abstract framework concrete.

Display-language candidate:
Key risks are severe risks that demand attention — for example risks to ecosystems, infrastructure, health, food, and water.

# Synthesis for Roll-up 1

## Most defensible core visual

A dynamic risk-system diagram:

1. Top: climate impact-drivers / hazards.
2. Center: three interacting determinants — hazard, exposure, vulnerability — converging into climate risk.
3. Bottom: response pathway showing risk reduction vs response risk/maladaptation.
4. Key-risk example module showing what climate risks look like concretely.
5. Small footer preview: risks can cascade, leading into later roll-ups.

## Priority claims

High priority:

- E01 risk is potential adverse consequences.
- E02 hazard–exposure–vulnerability interaction.
- E03 climate impact-drivers become hazards when detrimental.
- E06 responses can reduce or generate risk.
- E08 risk changes over space and time.
- E10 key risks make the framework concrete.
- E07 maladaptation as a high-emphasis response-risk warning.

Medium priority:

- E04 exposure definition.
- E05 vulnerability definition.
- E09 cascading risk preview.

## Claims to avoid or defer

- Do not include detailed national rankings or 2024 loss statistics on Roll-up 1 unless the panel becomes too abstract. Those figures belong better in Roll-up 3 or an evidence callout later.
- Do not map all Thailand hazards on Roll-up 1. Spatial risk belongs to Roll-up 3.
- Do not use detailed impact-chain examples here. Full impact-chain analysis belongs to Roll-up 4.
- Do not use S02 or S04 evidence on this panel unless a later revision explicitly reintroduces them.
- Do not imply the key-risk examples are exhaustive or Thailand-specific.

## Recommended panel takeaway

Climate risk is a system, not a single event: hazards become risks when they meet exposed and vulnerable people, assets, ecosystems, and institutions. The result can appear as key risks to ecosystems, infrastructure, health, food, water, and livelihoods — and the way society responds can reduce or increase future risk.
