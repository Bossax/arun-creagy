## Option A — Framework-first

### Story sequence

1. Climate risk is the potential for adverse consequences.
2. Risk is not the same as a climate hazard.
3. Risk emerges from the interaction of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability.
4. Climate impact-drivers become hazards when they can cause harm.
5. Responses can reduce risk or create new response risks.

### Strength
Most faithful to the IPCC risk framework. Clear for policy and technical audiences because it defines the vocabulary needed for the rest of the roll-up series.

### Weakness
Can feel abstract unless the visual system makes the interaction tangible.

### Best visual form
Dynamic risk triangle / interlocking system diagram with a response branch, based primarily on the IPCC ellipse/node language in `SRL-image-1.png`.

### Scientific risk
Low, if the diagram avoids presenting risk as a fixed arithmetic equation.

## Option B — Misconception-first

### Story sequence

1. A flood, drought, or heatwave is a hazard — not automatically a risk.
2. It becomes risk when people, ecosystems, infrastructure, or livelihoods are exposed.
3. The level of harm depends on vulnerability and adaptive capacity.
4. Poorly designed responses can increase future risk.
5. Therefore, climate risk must be managed as a system.

### Strength
Immediately corrects a common misunderstanding: hazard is not risk.

### Weakness
Examples like flood/drought/heat may imply Thailand-specific hazards even though Roll-up 1 is S03-only. It may also pull attention toward hazards rather than the framework.

### Best visual form
Before/after comparison: `Hazard alone` vs `Hazard + exposure + vulnerability = risk`.

### Scientific risk
Moderate. The equation-like comparison may oversimplify dynamic interactions unless carefully worded.

## Option C — Consequence-first

### Story sequence

1. Climate risk is about possible harm to human and ecological systems.
2. Harm can affect lives, livelihoods, health, ecosystems, infrastructure, and assets.
3. The potential for harm depends on hazard, exposure, and vulnerability.
4. These determinants change across space and time.
5. Responses shape future risk.

### Strength
Audience-centered and easier to understand because it begins with what is at stake.

### Weakness
May under-emphasize climate impact-drivers and the hazard/exposure/vulnerability framework if not visually structured well.

### Best visual form
Central `Risk` node surrounded by consequence icons, with hazard/exposure/vulnerability feeding into it.

### Scientific risk
Low to moderate. Must keep the determinant structure visible and not reduce risk to a list of impacts.

## Option D — Response-first

### Story sequence

1. Society responds to climate change through mitigation, adaptation, and development.
2. Good responses reduce hazard, exposure, or vulnerability.
3. Poor responses can create response risk and maladaptation.
4. Therefore, climate risk assessment must include both climate impacts and response consequences.
5. Hazard, exposure, and vulnerability remain the core determinants.

### Strength
Highlights a sophisticated IPCC AR6 point: responses are part of the risk landscape.

### Weakness
Too advanced for the first roll-up. It may bury the basic definition of risk.

### Best visual form
Forked pathway: `risk-informed response` vs `response risk`.

### Scientific risk
Low, but pedagogically weaker as the opener.

## Option E — IPCC-diagram guided

### Story sequence

1. Start by showing that IPCC risk framing has evolved from hazard–exposure–vulnerability to greater attention to response risk and complexity.
2. Use the AR6-style response-risk diagram as the central visual anchor.
3. Explain the four interacting components visible in the diagram: hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response.
4. Show that risk sits at the center because it emerges from interactions among these components.
5. Use short callouts to translate the technical diagram for a non-specialist audience.
6. End with the takeaway that risk is dynamic, not a fixed hazard map.

### Strength
Best fit with the user’s request to insert the two IPCC diagrams into the visual. It keeps scientific authority visible while using surrounding infographic design to make the diagrams more readable.

### Weakness
Requires careful layout. A full IPCC figure can be visually dense on a narrow 60 × 160 cm roll-up if inserted without cropping or simplification.

### Best visual form
Primary: enlarged/adapted `SRL-image-1.png` response-risk diagram.

Secondary: compact “IPCC risk framing through time” strip derived from `SRL-image-0.png`, showing AR5 base framework → AR6 response risk and complexity.

Supporting tone: low-poly climate icons and white-node callouts inspired by the iStock reference.

### Scientific risk
Low if the IPCC diagram’s category relationships and colors are preserved. Moderate if the figure is redrawn too freely or over-decorated.

# Recommendation

Select Option E — IPCC-diagram guided.

Rationale:
Roll-up 1 must establish the vocabulary and logic for the whole five-panel story, and the user wants the two IPCC diagrams inserted into the visual. The best structure is therefore not a wholly new diagram, but an IPCC-guided layout: use the AR6 response-risk diagram as the main anchor, place the “risk through time” diagram as a smaller context strip, and use custom infographic callouts to translate the concepts.

Use elements from Option A for clarity:
The surrounding callouts should still explain the framework in a simple order: climate impact-driver/hazard → exposure + vulnerability → risk → response risk/maladaptation.
