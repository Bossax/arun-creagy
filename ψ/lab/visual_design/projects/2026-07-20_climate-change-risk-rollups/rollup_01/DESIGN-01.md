# DESIGN-01.md — Roll-up 1 Design System

## 1. Purpose

This design system governs Roll-up 1 of the five-panel climate change risk series.

Roll-up 1 title:
What is climate change risk?

Panel function:
Define the IPCC AR6 climate-risk framework before later roll-ups move into Thailand-specific hazards, geography, cascading impacts, and adaptation governance.

Evidence boundary:
Use S03 only:
`ψ/inbox/notebooklm_runs/2026-07-20_202000/IPCC-AR6-WGI-WGII-extract.md`

Visual-reference boundary:

- `00_input/reference_images/SRL-image-0.png` — IPCC risk assessment through time; use as a compact context strip or adapted source figure.
- `00_input/reference_images/SRL-image-1.png` — IPCC response-risk diagram; use as the primary scientific visual anchor.
- `00_input/reference_images/istockphoto-523868158-1024x1024.jpg` — use only for icon/callout tone: low-poly environmental facets, white node connectors, clean infographic styling.

The two IPCC diagrams should be inserted or adapted with visible source attribution. The low-poly climate image should not define the science.

---

## 2. Core Style Statement

Create a 60 × 160 cm vertical scientific-editorial roll-up centered on the IPCC AR6 risk diagram. The design should preserve IPCC scientific authority while translating the diagram through clear callouts, restrained institutional styling, and geometric climate-infographic support elements.

The roll-up should feel:

- scientific and evidence-led;
- contemporary and clear;
- institutional but not bureaucratic;
- readable at exhibition distance;
- visually connected to IPCC diagram language;
- coherent with the broader five-roll-up series.

Avoid:

- generic climate-campaign styling;
- decorative sustainability graphics;
- a copied screenshot pasted without layout integration;
- over-designed icons that compete with the IPCC diagram;
- Thailand-specific claims on this first panel;
- a simplified equation that misrepresents risk as static.

---

## 3. Roll-up 1 Narrative Logic

Primary message:
Climate risk is more than a climate hazard. In IPCC AR6, risk emerges from interactions among hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response.

Reading sequence:

1. Title question: what is climate change risk?
2. Compact IPCC context strip: AR5 risk graphic → AR6 additions of response risk and complexity.
3. Main AR6 risk diagram: hazard, vulnerability, exposure, response, and central risk.
4. Plain-language callouts explaining the components.
5. Key-risk examples: concrete severe risk domains such as ecosystems, infrastructure, health, food, water, and coastal systems.
6. Prominent maladaptation warning: a response meant to reduce risk can create more risk if poorly designed.
7. Footer takeaway: risk is dynamic and can cascade through connected systems.

Do not introduce Thailand-specific examples here. Roll-up 1 is the conceptual gateway.

## 4. Composition System

## 4.1 Canvas

- Format: vertical roll-up.
- Physical size: 60 cm wide × 160 cm high.
- Safe margin: minimum 4 cm from left/right edges.
- Hardware caution: keep critical content out of the top and bottom 8–10 cm.
- Main working content zone: approximately 8–152 cm.

## 4.2 Grid

- Use an 8-column vertical grid.
- Outer margin: 6–7%.
- Horizontal gutter: 2–3%.
- Vertical section spacing: 3–5% of canvas height.

## 4.3 Recommended vertical zones

### Zone A — Header / title
Approximate height: 12–16 cm.

Content:

- Roll-up number marker: `01`
- Title: `What is climate change risk?`
- Short subtitle: `IPCC AR6 frames risk as an interaction among hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response.`

### Zone B — IPCC context strip
Approximate height: 22–28 cm.

Content:

- Compact adapted/inserted version of `SRL-image-0.png`.
- Focus on the transition from AR5 risk framing to AR6 response risk and complexity.

Treatment:

- Do not insert the full tall image at unreadable scale.
- Crop or rebuild as a clean two-step strip.
- Preserve labels and relationships if adapted.
- Add source/caption nearby.

### Zone C — Main scientific anchor
Approximate height: 62–74 cm.

Content:

- Large adapted/inserted `SRL-image-1.png`.
- This is the dominant visual.

Treatment:

- Preserve the central magenta `Risk` node.
- Preserve category positions and colors:
  - Hazard: navy.
  - Exposure: cyan.
  - Vulnerability: orange.
  - Response: amber/brown.
  - Risk: magenta.
- Keep pale-grey overlapping fields.
- Keep dashed interaction boundary if legible.

### Zone D — Translation callouts
Approximate height: integrated around Zone C.

Content:

- 4–5 concise callouts connected to the main diagram:
  - Hazard: climate conditions that can cause harm.
  - Exposure: what is in harm’s way.
  - Vulnerability: sensitivity and limited capacity to cope/adapt.
  - Response: decisions that can reduce or create risk.
  - Risk: potential adverse consequences.

Treatment:

- Use white circular endpoints and thin grey connector lines inspired by the low-poly reference.
- Keep text outside the inserted IPCC diagram, not on top of dense figure areas.

### Zone E — Key risks / maladaptation / cascading-risk bridge
Approximate height: 24–30 cm.

Content:

- Compact key-risk example module with a one-sentence explanation for each domain:
  - ecosystems;
  - coastal socio-ecological systems;
  - critical infrastructure, networks, and services;
  - living standards, human health, and cultural heritage;
  - food and water security.
- Prominent maladaptation warning callout:
  - `Maladaptation = response that creates more risk`
  - explain that poorly designed responses can lock in vulnerability, shift harm, or limit future choices.
- Small linked-node strip: risk can cascade through connected systems.

Treatment:

- Use response green only as a framing/action color; do not recolor the IPCC response category if adapting the diagram.
- Treat key-risk examples as illustrative IPCC domains, not as Thailand-specific claims.
- Use amber/orange emphasis for maladaptation; it should draw attention without overpowering the central IPCC risk node.

### Zone F — Footer / sources
Approximate height: 8–10 cm.

Content:

- Source note for S03.
- Attribution for adapted/inserted IPCC diagrams.
- DCCE identity area if logo is used later.

## 5. Scientific Diagram Rules

## 5.1 IPCC diagram insertion

When inserting or adapting `SRL-image-0.png` and `SRL-image-1.png`:

- Preserve the scientific relationship.
- Preserve category colors unless contrast corrections are required.
- Do not move hazard/exposure/vulnerability/response in a way that changes meaning.
- Do not add decorative elements inside the scientific diagram.
- Keep the diagram on a white or very pale grey field.
- Add a visible source note: `Adapted from IPCC AR6 risk framework diagram` or equivalent.

## 5.2 Main diagram priority

`SRL-image-1.png` is the main visual anchor. `SRL-image-0.png` is secondary context.

If space becomes constrained, keep `SRL-image-1.png` and reduce `SRL-image-0.png` to a minimal context strip.

## 5.3 Callout rules

Callouts translate, not reinterpret.

Use:

- short labels;
- one-sentence explanations;
- thin grey connectors;
- white circular endpoints;
- small category-color chips.

Avoid:

- long paragraphs;
- arrows that imply one-way causation where the IPCC shows interaction;
- callouts crossing over the central risk node;
- icons that obscure the original diagram.

## 6.1 Low-poly tone reference

Use `istockphoto-523868158-1024x1024.jpg` only for:

- geometric climate-object silhouettes;
- clean infographic connector rhythm.

Do not copy:

- stock layout hierarchy;

## 6.2 Supporting icons

Icons may represent:

- people / livelihoods;
- ecosystems;
- coastal systems;
- infrastructure;
- health;
- food and water;
- assets/services;
- adaptation/response;
- cascading links.

Icon style:

- geometric vector;
- simple flat or lightly faceted;
- no heavy black outlines;
- no glossy 3D;
- consistent scale;
- readable at small size.

## 6.3 Environmental illustration

Use sparingly. Roll-up 1 is diagram-led. Environmental motifs should support the concept, not become the main subject.

Allowed:

- subtle faceted climate arc;
- small globe/land/ocean motif in the header or footer;
- low-opacity geometric background texture.

Avoid:

- large decorative Earth competing with the IPCC diagram;
- dramatic disaster scenes;
- photorealistic climate imagery.

| Role | Color | Use |
|---|---:|---|
| Climate light grey | `#F1F2F3` | canvas background |
| Scientific white | `#FFFFFF` | diagram fields, callout cards |
| Annotation grey | `#8A8A8A` | connector lines, secondary labels, source notes |

## 7.4 Color restrictions

- Do not use magenta decoratively; reserve it for risk.
- Do not use orange as generic warning unless tied to vulnerability/response-risk.
- Use orange warning emphasis specifically for maladaptation when highlighting response risk.
- Do not rely on color alone; pair colors with labels.
- Do not use saturated rainbow palettes.
- Do not place category-colored text on low-contrast backgrounds.

## 8. Typography

## 8.1 Character

Use a modern scientific sans-serif. Prioritize clarity over personality.

Recommended:

- Headings: Plus Jakarta Sans, Inter, Aptos Display, or similar.
- Body/labels: DM Sans, Inter, Aptos, or similar.

## 8.2 Type hierarchy for 60 × 160 cm

Approximate physical sizing should be validated in layout software.

| Role | Relative size | Notes |
|---|---:|---|
| Main title | 1.00 | large, readable from 2 m |
| Subtitle | 0.42–0.50 | one line or two short lines |
| Section label | 0.32–0.40 | category or zone title |
| Diagram labels | preserve/adapt from IPCC | must remain legible |
| Callout body | 0.22–0.28 | one short sentence |
| Source note | 0.14–0.18 | footer only |

## 8.3 Text rules

- Keep total visible word count around 100–140 words.
- Use direct labels instead of legends where possible.
- Do not place body text over complex diagram areas.
- Do not generate final text inside AI imagery.
- Add final Thai/English text in a controlled layout tool.

## 9. Content Copy Guidance

## 9.1 Title
What is climate change risk?

## 9.2 Subtitle
IPCC AR6 frames risk as an interaction among hazard, exposure, vulnerability, and response.

## 9.3 Key callouts

- Risk: potential adverse consequences.
- Hazard: climate conditions that can cause harm.
- Exposure: people, ecosystems, infrastructure, services, and assets in harm’s way.
- Vulnerability: sensitivity and limited capacity to cope or adapt.
- Response: decisions that can reduce risk or create new risk.
- Maladaptation: a response that creates more risk by locking in vulnerability, shifting harm, or limiting future choices.

## 9.4 Bottom takeaway
Risk is dynamic: it changes with climate, exposure, vulnerability, and the choices society makes — and appears concretely as risks to ecosystems, infrastructure, health, food, water, and coastal systems.

## 9.5 Key-risk example cards

Header:
`Key risks are potentially severe climate risks that demand attention.`

- Coastal systems — sea-level rise, waves and storms can affect ecosystems, livelihoods and settlements together.
- Ecosystems — climate conditions beyond tolerance can erode habitats, species and ecosystem services.
- Infrastructure and services — extreme conditions can disrupt water, energy, transport and essential services.
- Health and cultural heritage — climate risks can affect health, living standards and what communities value.
- Food and water security — heat, drought and rainfall variability can undermine reliable food production and water access.

Use these as illustrative IPCC domains, not as a Thailand risk assessment.

## 9.6 Maladaptation emphasis copy

Primary callout:
Maladaptation warning

Supporting line:
A response meant to reduce risk can create more risk if it locks in vulnerability, shifts harm, or limits future choices.
