# Introduction: Rationale and Objectives for NCAIF

Thailand's current climate adaptation information landscape fails users along three concrete, specific dimensions, not one vague "lack of information." Slide 13 of the CRDB dissemination deck traces how users actually search — through a general search engine, through the government open-data portal (data.go.th), or directly through DCCE's own website — and finds that all three channels converge on the same three failure modes: users "cannot find the information they are looking for" because it is scattered with no single point of collection; they "cannot verify the credibility" of what they do find, because the source, standard, or quality assurance behind the data is unclear; and, even when data is found and trusted, it is "not in the format needed" for analysis, planning, or decision support. This is why Slide 9 states the stakes plainly: without a "good information system," the country cannot systematically assess climate risk, compare conditions across time, or update its decisions as new data arrives. The consultant designed NCAIF to close exactly this three-part gap — not by producing one more report or map, but by building a national information architecture that makes climate adaptation data findable, source-verified, and delivered in a form decision-makers can actually use.

To make that architecture concrete, the consultant organized the project's entire scope of work around four guiding questions (Slide 14 of the dissemination deck), and NCAIF's four main outputs are the direct answers to each one:

| Guiding Question | Digital Infrastructure Pillar (NCAIF Output) |
|---|---|
| 1. What must exist? | Information Architecture — the overall data structure, information architecture, and content categorization needed to manage climate risk |
| 2. What already exists? | Dataset and Product Catalog — an inventory of existing data assets and information products already held across agencies |
| 3. What must be built? | Real-world Use Cases — an analysis of actual usage needs, identifying gaps and the new products, data, or information services required |
| 4. How will it be sustained? | Data Governance — roles, standards, exchange rules, and the operating mechanisms that keep the system running over time |

Framing the project this way matters for how DCCE should read the rest of this report: NCAIF is not simply a sitemap exercise. It is an answer to "what needs to exist," backed by an honest accounting of "what already exists," extended by "what must still be built," and made sustainable by "how it will be driven." Each of the four pillars above becomes one strand of evidence running through the sections that follow.

These four pillars are not four separate deliverables sitting side by side — they form a single layered structure, matching the relationship shown on Slide 9 of the dissemination deck. At the top sits the **web platform** — the outward-facing website — which itself contains two catalogs: the inventory of information and data products, and the inventory of baseline datasets. Spanning both of these is the study of the services and information products the country still needs. Underneath this presentation layer sit two foundational components: **data governance** and the **data platform**. In other words, the website is what a policymaker, official, or member of the public actually sees and uses; the data platform and data governance are the operating disciplines — standards, roles, and infrastructure — that make what the website shows trustworthy and current.

The consultant did not design this as a desk exercise. Slide 15 of the dissemination deck documents that the consultant carried the project through three distinct engagement formats in sequence between January and August 2026: one-on-one interviews with government agencies to understand current data use, ongoing projects, and future needs; internal focus group discussions with DCCE staff to examine internal data use patterns, data governance, and website structure; and a multi-stakeholder brainstorming workshop to present proposed climate services and jointly prioritize which datasets and information products the country needs most. At a summary level, the overall shape of this process was: **evidence gathering → synthesis → drafting of NCAIF and the data governance framework → stakeholder consultation → refinement → the final NCAIF and Data Management Framework presented in this report.** The remainder of this executive summary follows that same logic: international lessons (Section 1.1) inform NCAIF's design principles; DCCE's own current status and constraints (Section 1.2) ground the design in institutional reality; the consultation process (Section 1.3) tests and confirms the resulting design; and the final NCAIF and Data Management Framework (Section 1.4) is what that whole process produced.

# 1.1 Lessons from International Approaches and Design Principles

The consultant reviewed three international benchmark platforms — Japan's A-PLAT, Germany's KlimAdapt/KLiVO Portal, and the European Union's Climate-ADAPT — to identify structural lessons for NCAIF, not to copy any one platform's content or feature list. Across the three, the consultant found a common pattern: each platform gives users more than one way in (by user role, by sector, by region, or by knowledge type — data, tools, or case studies) rather than a single generic homepage; each treats its dataset and product listings as a governed, curated catalog with clear admission criteria (relevance, currency, scientific basis, public accessibility) rather than an open-ended list of links; and each functions partly as a hub that links out to authoritative external sources rather than trying to physically centralize every dataset itself. The consultant's synthesis across all three platforms converged on a common structural floor: a modern national adaptation platform needs four working layers operating together — a policy and explanatory content layer, a services-and-tools layer, a knowledge-and-case-study layer, and a screening–metadata–governance layer that keeps the other three trustworthy and consistent. NCAIF's structure — a Policy Maker Center, an Adaptation Information section organized by cycle, a combined Knowledge/Tools/Data Services section, and the underlying Data Management Framework — was designed to satisfy exactly this four-layer minimum, and the strongest caution the consultant carried forward from this review is that good navigation and content design cannot be sustained on their own without a governed data structure behind them — which is why NCAIF pairs its content design with a Data Management Framework from the outset rather than adding governance later.

Alongside platform benchmarking, the consultant used four international knowledge frameworks — IPCC, WMO, UNFCCC, and ISO 14090/14091 — to define what content categories and structuring logic NCAIF actually needs, not simply to lend it academic legitimacy. Because these frameworks converge on essentially the same underlying logic — assess risk, plan and act, then monitor and adjust — the consultant did not need to invent a new content taxonomy: NCAIF adopts the international adaptation-management cycle as its stable content backbone, which keeps the platform simultaneously legible to international reporting audiences (UNFCCC's National Adaptation Plan reporting logic in particular) and auditable against management-standard requirements (ISO 14090/14091's emphasis on traceable methods, assumptions, and responsibilities). This is also why the Policy Maker Center is designed as a tailored information service, in line with WMO's climate-services model of moving from observation to user-facing service through ongoing co-production, rather than as a static one-way publication channel.

A content taxonomy chosen correctly can still fail in use if the navigation and interaction design around it create excessive cognitive load, so the consultant also grounded NCAIF's structure in established user-experience and information-architecture principles. When critical content sits several clicks deep beneath dense or technical headings, users lose the "information scent" that tells them a click is heading toward what they need, and they hesitate, backtrack, or abandon the platform — so the consultant kept NCAIF's top-level navigation shallow and organized it around task-oriented user pathways (the Policy Maker Center, Adaptation Information by Cycle, Risk and Area Profiles) rather than around DCCE's own internal departmental structure. The consultant also applied progressive disclosure — presenting a simplified view first and pushing advanced or technical detail behind expandable layers — and kept interactive tools and dashboards structurally separate from raw data catalogs and downloads, because the two serve fundamentally different user tasks even though their underlying data is tightly integrated on the back end. These are not abstract preferences: NCAIF's own locked sitemap decisions cite this design-principles work directly to justify both the shallow, task-oriented top-level navigation and the separation of "Interactive tools/dashboards" from "Data catalog/download/APIs" within the Knowledge, Tools, and Data Services section.
