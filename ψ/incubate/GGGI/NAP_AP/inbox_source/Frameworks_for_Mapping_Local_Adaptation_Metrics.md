

The best-supported approach for mapping local line-agency metrics to the GGA, including the Belém Adaptation Indicators, is a **tiered, mixed-methods framework**: use a small set of globally harmonized core indicators, allow locally defined complementary indicators, and connect them through common themes or adaptation-cycle stages rather than forcing one-to-one metric equivalence. The literature consistently shows that purely top-down standardized systems lose local relevance, while fully decentralized systems weaken comparability and aggregation (Craft & Fisher, 2018; Barkai & Winkler, 2024; Gonzalez et al., 2026).

A practical mapping architecture usually has four layers: local observations and administrative data; indicator calculation methods; national reporting templates; and global aggregation rules. This is the logic proposed for government adaptation tracking, biodiversity monitoring, and SDG localization, where local data can remain context-specific if it is translated through auditable workflows and shared metadata (Berrang‐Ford et al., 2019; Affinito et al., 2024; Gonzalez et al., 2026).

## Framework Options

The most transferable adaptation-specific framework is the policy-cycle model that tracks **risk/vulnerability, goals/targets, efforts, and results**. It is scalable across governments and is designed for longitudinal tracking, which makes it useful for aligning line-agency metrics to GGA thematic and dimensional targets (Berrang‐Ford et al., 2019).

For aggregation across scales, three linking strategies recur: **standardized indicators**, **common themes with different indicators at different levels**, and **informal synthesis of diverse national results**. The evidence favors the second option for adaptation because it preserves local context while still allowing national and global synthesis (Craft & Fisher, 2018).

Recent GGA and post-2030 indicator work points to a similar architecture: **global core indicators, optional global indicators, and custom local indicators**. This structure addresses persistent problems of insufficient data, indicator overlap, and local misfit in global frameworks (Sato, 2025; Simpson et al., 2026).

Intersectional and equity-oriented frameworks add an important correction: localizing the GGA requires metrics that capture who benefits, who is excluded, and how vulnerability differs across groups. This is especially relevant if local line agencies report service delivery outputs that do not reveal distributional outcomes (Amorim-Maia & Olazabal, 2024; Barkai & Winkler, 2024).

## Data Gathering And Matching

Most local adaptation systems still over-measure **outputs** and under-measure outcomes, impacts, targets, timeframes, and data sources. In the largest cross-city review, most local indicators tracked implementation activities rather than whether vulnerability fell or resilience improved (Goonesekera & Olazabal, 2022).

The strongest field methods are therefore mixed. Studies support combining administrative records, official statistics, remote sensing or Earth observation, document review, field verification, stakeholder interviews, and community-generated or citizen data where quality controls are explicit (Benedek et al., 2021; Guo et al., 2022; Karki et al., 2021; Borghys et al., 2024).

Earth observation is especially useful where local coverage is sparse or inconsistent, and it can support GGA-relevant themes such as agriculture, biodiversity, extreme events, and health. But EO still needs standardized operational indicator definitions and cannot substitute for local social and institutional data (Connors et al., 2025; Gilruth et al., 2021).

Community-level and locally led methods improve relevance and equity, particularly when metrics are co-developed with affected groups and complemented by contextual qualitative data. Evidence from Africa and ecosystem-based adaptation shows that field collection often exposes aggregation problems that are invisible in desk-based systems (Okeke‐Ogbuafor et al., 2024; Gilruth et al., 2021).

Methods for indicator matching are most robust when they use **crosswalks** rather than exact matches. Harmonization studies compare local indicators first against the global framework, then against sectoral or city reporting systems to identify synergies, common themes, and feasible starter sets (Iralde et al., 2024).

Mapping local agency metrics to Belém indicators should therefore use a crosswalk with at least these columns: local metric, responsible agency, data source, spatial unit, reporting frequency, adaptation-cycle stage, GGA target/theme, Belém indicator match type, and quality/confidence flag. The need for baselines, metadata, and low-burden alignment with existing national and international channels is explicit in the emerging Belém literature (Simpson et al., 2026).

Local evidence systems also need a validation step. Vulnerability and adaptation indices are more credible when indicator-data links are explicit and when internal or external validity is tested rather than assumed (Birkmann et al., 2021).

## Decentralized Vs Centralized Data

Evidence is mixed on decentralized data-sharing versus central databases because each solves a different failure mode.

Decentralized or federated systems are favored when the goal is to preserve local relevance, data sovereignty, and community participation. Biodiversity and locally led adaptation frameworks argue that bottom-up monitoring, community-based systems, and federated standards can aggregate upward if they share common principles, metadata, and analytical pipelines (Affinito et al., 2024; Gonzalez et al., 2026; Okeke‐Ogbuafor et al., 2024).

But decentralized systems are less reliable when institutions collect indicators in isolation. Stakeholder interviews in adaptation practice warn that multiple organizations developing frameworks separately create inconsistency, and city-level reviews show that most authorities define their own indicators, which makes benchmarking difficult (Andries et al., 2023; Iralde et al., 2024).

Central databases improve comparability, reproducibility, and transparency when they enforce standard workflows and metadata. Open databases, SDG portals, and object-relational repositories can consolidate heterogeneous indicators and support repeated reporting cycles (Benedek et al., 2021; Jossin & Peters, 2022; Griffith1 et al., 2026).

Yet central systems also fail when they flatten local context or rely on incomplete national averages. Local SDG studies show that higher-level datasets can miss municipal disparities, and adaptation scholars warn that top-down generalization can exclude actors without the resources to report in externally imposed formats (Borghys et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2021; Yule et al., 2025).

For BTRs and NAPs, the current evidence supports a **hybrid architecture**: decentralized collection by line agencies and communities, but centralized national quality assurance, metadata standards, and compilation. BTRs were designed to improve consistency, but available evidence shows they still often lack the standardized, high-quality data needed for coherent comparison, while NAP systems remain absent or non-operational in many countries (Biesbroek et al., 2025; Leiter, 2021).

## Recommended Design

The most defensible design for mapping local line-agency metrics to the Belém Adaptation Indicators is a **federated national adaptation data system**. Local agencies collect mixed quantitative and qualitative data using shared templates; national coordinators maintain a central registry, metadata dictionary, and QA/QC rules; and global reporting uses a core-plus-optional indicator set with narrative fields for context, equity, and causal interpretation (, 2024; Andries et al., 2023; Simpson et al., 2026).

In practice, the best field methods are: routine administrative data, targeted field verification, geospatial layers, EO where coverage is weak, and participatory collection for locally led and equity-sensitive indicators. The best matching method is a thematic crosswalk tied to the adaptation cycle, not a rigid one-to-one correspondence between local and global metrics (Karki et al., 2021; Gilruth et al., 2021; Connors et al., 2025; Craft & Fisher, 2018).

So, for compiling a BTR or NAP, decentralized collection alone is not reliable enough, and central databases alone are not context-sensitive enough. The literature supports a hybrid, standards-based system as the best framework for mapping local metrics to GGA and Belém targets.
