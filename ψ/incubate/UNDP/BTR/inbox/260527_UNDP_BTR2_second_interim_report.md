## 1. 1 National Circumstances, Institutional Arrangements and Legal Frameworks
### 1.1.1  National Circumstances Relevant to Adaptation
 
Thailand is located in mainland Southeast Asia within the tropical zone. The country shares borders with Myanmar and Lao PDR to the north, Lao PDR and Cambodia to the east, Malaysia to the south, and Myanmar and the Andaman Sea to the west. Its geographical location and climatic conditions expose the country to a range of climate-related hazards, including floods, droughts, tropical storms, coastal erosion, and heatwaves.

Thailand’s topography is highly diverse and can be broadly divided into four major regions. The Northern Region consists of mountainous terrain and fertile river valleys and serves as the source of several major river systems. The Northeastern Region is dominated by the Korat Plateau and is characterized by relatively poor soils and recurrent drought conditions. The Central Region comprises the Chao Phraya River Basin, one of the most productive agricultural areas in the country. The Southern Region is a narrow peninsula bordered by the Gulf of Thailand and the Andaman Sea, featuring extensive coastal and marine ecosystems.

Thailand experiences a tropical monsoon climate with three distinct seasons: the rainy season, influenced by the southwest monsoon; the cool season, influenced by the northeast monsoon; and the hot season preceding the monsoon period. Variations in rainfall and temperature across regions influence water availability, agricultural productivity, ecosystem conditions, and exposure to climate-related hazards.

Thailand had a population of approximately 65.95 million people in 2024, with around 34.3 per cent residing in urban areas. The country is also undergoing a demographic transition towards an ageing society (National Statistical Office, 2025). Over recent decades, socio-economic development has significantly improved access to healthcare, education, and public services. More than 99.7 per cent of the population is covered by health insurance schemes, while access to electricity is nearly universal nationwide. Nevertheless, disparities remain between urban and rural areas in terms of income levels, access to public services, infrastructure, and digital connectivity. These differences influence the ability of individuals and communities to prepare for, respond to, and recover from climate-related hazards and impacts.

Despite substantial progress in poverty reduction over the long term, socio-economic vulnerability remains an important challenge. In 2024, approximately 3.43 million people, or 4.89 per cent of the population, lived below the national poverty line of THB 3,078 per person per month. Agricultural households accounted for 45.49 per cent of the country’s poor population, highlighting the vulnerability of livelihoods that depend heavily on climate-sensitive sectors. Such households are particularly exposed to climate variability, droughts, floods, and fluctuations in agricultural production. While expenditure inequality has gradually declined, Thailand’s expenditure Gini coefficient remained at 0.333 in 2024, indicating persistent disparities in access to education, economic opportunities, infrastructure, and public services across population groups and regions. These socio-economic conditions play an important role in shaping vulnerability and adaptive capacity to climate change, particularly among low-income households, rural communities, older persons, and other vulnerable groups (NESDC, 2025).

Thailand is an upper-middle-income economy with a diversified economic structure that includes agriculture, manufacturing, industry, and services. Although agriculture contributes a relatively modest share of national GDP, it remains a major source of employment and livelihoods in rural areas. Other climate-sensitive sectors, particularly fisheries and tourism, also play important roles in supporting local economies and household incomes. Consequently, climate variability and extreme weather events can affect food security, employment, livelihoods, and economic stability in many parts of the country.

Thailand is recognized as one of the world's biodiversity-rich countries, possessing diverse terrestrial, freshwater, coastal, and marine ecosystems. These ecosystems provide essential services, including food production, water regulation, carbon sequestration, coastal protection, and livelihood support. However, climate change, together with land-use change and environmental degradation, poses increasing risks to ecosystem resilience and biodiversity conservation.

Infrastructure systems are critical for supporting climate resilience and adaptive capacity. Key infrastructure includes water resource management systems, irrigation networks, transportation systems, energy networks, healthcare facilities, telecommunications, early warning systems, and urban services. Nevertheless, infrastructure resilience varies considerably across regions. Flood-prone lowland areas in the central region, drought-affected areas in the northeast, rapidly urbanizing metropolitan areas, and coastal zones exposed to sea-level rise and erosion face different levels of climate risk and adaptive capacity.

Thailand’s climate vulnerability is shaped by the interaction of physical exposure, socio-economic conditions, demographic trends, ecosystem dependence, and infrastructure resilience. Vulnerability is particularly pronounced among low-income households, smallholder farmers, coastal communities, older persons, persons with disabilities, outdoor workers, and populations whose livelihoods depend heavily on climate-sensitive natural resources. Rapid urbanization has also increased exposure to climate hazards in metropolitan areas, especially Bangkok, where flooding, extreme heat, and infrastructure disruptions present growing challenges.

To strengthen resilience, Thailand has established a comprehensive policy and institutional framework for climate adaptation. The National Committee on Climate Change Policy (NCCC) serves as the principal decision-making body for climate policy, while the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) provides strategic direction for reducing climate risks and enhancing resilience across priority sectors. Adaptation planning has increasingly been integrated into sectoral and local development processes to ensure that responses are tailored to regional circumstances and vulnerability profiles.

Thailand continues to invest in climate information systems, climate projections, spatial risk assessments, early warning systems, social protection mechanisms, and capacity-building programmes to support evidence-based decision-making. These efforts are complemented by cross-sectoral coordination, stakeholder engagement, and partnerships across national and subnational levels. Despite ongoing progress, adaptive capacity remains uneven across regions and population groups, highlighting the need for targeted, inclusive, and risk-informed adaptation measures to address existing vulnerabilities and strengthen long-term resilience.

### 1.1.2 Institutional Arrangements and Legal Framework on Adaptation 

Thailand has established a comprehensive institutional arrangement for climate change adaptation, covering the full process from policy formulation and planning to implementation, monitoring, and reporting. Roles and responsibilities of relevant agencies are clearly defined and systematically linked across each stage.

At the policy level, **the National Committee on Climate Change Policy (NCCC)** serves as the primary mechanism for setting national directions. The Cabinet provides approval for key plans and measures, while the NCCC oversees, supervises, monitors, and evaluates the implementation of actions by government agencies and all relevant sectors to ensure alignment with, and achievement of the NAP.

In terms of planning and integration, **the Working Group on National Climate Change Adaptation Implementation Integration** is responsible for formulating the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and supporting its implementation. Meanwhile, **the Subcommittee on Climate Change Policy and Planning Integration** provides policy, strategic, and programmatic recommendations. At the subnational level, provincial working groups have been established to develop adaptation plans aligned with local contexts.

For risk and impact assessment, the DCCE is responsible for conducting long-term climate analysis and impact assessments. It also develops a Climate Risk Map Database and high-resolution climate projections to support sectoral risk assessments and inform policy decision-making. These data are disseminated through online platforms, including [https://ccic.dcce.go.th/riskarea](https://ccic.dcce.go.th/riskarea) and [https://clim-webbased.dcce.go.th/](https://clim-webbased.dcce.go.th/), to ensure accessibility and utilization by relevant agencies.

In addition, the TMD plays a key role in developing the National Framework for Climate Services (NFCS), which supports the DCCE in enhancing climate data systems, projections, and risk and impact assessments, thereby facilitating data use across all sectors.

Regarding coordination, the DCCE serves as the National Focal Point for climate change adaptation, facilitating collaboration across sectors and levels of governance. Provincial mechanisms are also supported to enhance data integration and implementation through both top-down and bottom-up approaches.

At the sectoral level, Thailand has sectoral focal point across six priority sectors: water resources management, agriculture, tourism, public health, natural resources, and human settlements and infrastructure. Each sector is supported by responsible agencies tasked with implementing adaptation measures in accordance with the NAP. Cross-cutting issues are addressed through collaboration among key agencies, including the ONEP, the MNRE, the MHESI, and the MOI. These agencies jointly develop shared databases, research, and support mechanisms, while also strengthening capacity and raising awareness among stakeholders.

Thailand also emphasizes consultation and participation by engaging stakeholders from government, private sector, academia, civil society, and local authorities throughout all stages of the adaptation process—from risk assessment and planning to implementation and evaluation.

Implementation of adaptation measures is undertaken by responsible agencies in accordance with their respective mandates under the NAP, with priority sectors guided by measures aimed at reducing risks and vulnerabilities while enhancing adaptive capacity and resilience. Finally, for monitoring, evaluation, and reporting, the DCCE serves as the central coordinating agency, working in collaboration with sectoral focal points to ensure a systematic and harmonized approach. Progress on NAP implementation and national resilience-building efforts is regularly tracked and reported.

Thailand has established a comprehensive legal framework to support climate change adaptation, disaster risk reduction, gender equality, social inclusion, and resilience-building. These legal instruments contribute to strengthening adaptive capacity, reducing vulnerability, protecting the rights of vulnerable and marginalized groups, and promoting equitable and inclusive responses to climate-related risks. The key legal frameworks relevant to adaptation are described below:

#### Draft Climate Change Act

Thailand is in the process of developing the Climate Change Act, which is expected to serve as a key legal framework for strengthening long-term climate governance and supporting the country’s transition toward climate-resilient and low-carbon development. The draft legislation is being developed by the Department of Climate Change and Environment (DCCE) and aims to establish an integrated framework for climate change mitigation and adaptation, including institutional arrangements, policy coordination, financing mechanisms, data and information systems, and implementation oversight across sectors and levels of government.

In the area of adaptation, the Draft Climate Change Act places significant emphasis on strengthening climate resilience through risk-informed planning and evidence-based decision-making. The draft includes provisions related to the development of climate data and knowledge systems to support climate risk assessments, vulnerability assessments, climate projections, and adaptation planning. Relevant government agencies are expected to cooperate in data sharing and technical coordination to support national and sectoral adaptation efforts.

The draft legislation also supports the implementation of the NAP by promoting the integration of climate adaptation considerations into national and local development planning processes. The framework encourages vulnerable sectors and local authorities to develop adaptation actions that are consistent with national priorities while reflecting local socio-economic and environmental contexts. In addition, the draft promotes monitoring and reporting mechanisms to track adaptation progress and improve policy effectiveness over time.

A key feature of the draft legislation is the establishment of the Climate Change Fund, which is intended to serve as a dedicated financing mechanism to support Thailand’s transition toward low-carbon and climate-resilient development. The Fund is expected to provide financial support for activities that enhance adaptive capacity, strengthen resilience, reduce climate vulnerability, and create an enabling environment for climate action. The establishment of the Fund is also expected to improve access to climate finance and support the implementation of adaptation measures across national and local levels.

Furthermore, the draft Climate Change Act aims to strengthen institutional coordination among government agencies and support long-term climate governance through clearer mandates and implementation mechanisms. The legislation is expected to contribute to enhancing Thailand’s adaptive capacity, reducing climate vulnerability, and supporting sustainable development objectives.

#### Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Act (2007)

The Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Act (2007) serves as the primary legal framework governing disaster risk management in Thailand. The Act establishes a systematic approach covering disaster prevention, preparedness, emergency response, and post-disaster recovery, while defining the institutional structure and responsibilities of relevant authorities at national and local levels.

The Act mandates the development of disaster prevention and mitigation plans at national, provincial, and local levels to strengthen preparedness and response capacities. It also provides the legal basis for disaster declarations, emergency operations, resource mobilization, evacuation procedures, and assistance mechanisms for affected populations.

In the context of climate change adaptation, the Act plays an important role in supporting risk reduction and resilience-building efforts against climate-related hazards such as floods, storms, droughts, landslides, and heat-related events. The framework supports the integration of disaster risk reduction measures, early warning systems, emergency preparedness, and recovery planning into broader climate adaptation efforts.

In addition, the Act promotes coordination among government agencies and supports the implementation of integrated disaster risk management approaches, including the application of the Incident Command System (ICS), Emergency Operations Centers (EOC), and community-based disaster risk management (CBDRM). These mechanisms contribute to strengthening Thailand’s capacity to address climate-related loss and damage and enhance resilience at national and local levels.

#### Regulations of the Ministry of Finance on Emergency Relief Advances for Disaster Victims (2019)

The Regulations of the Ministry of Finance on Emergency Relief Advances for Disaster Victims (2019) provide the legal and financial basis for the rapid allocation of government funds to support emergency response and recovery efforts following disasters. The regulations enable responsible agencies to access advance funding for immediate relief operations, including emergency assistance, temporary shelter, restoration of essential services, and support for affected households.

In the context of climate change adaptation, these regulations strengthen Thailand’s adaptive capacity by ensuring the timely availability of financial resources following climate-related disasters such as floods, storms, droughts, and other extreme weather events. The framework helps reduce recovery time, supports vulnerable populations, and enhances resilience through improved disaster response and recovery mechanisms.

#### Gender Equality Act (2015)

The Gender Equality Act (2015) is a key legal instrument of Thailand aimed at promoting gender equality and protecting individuals from unfair gender-based discrimination, covering women, men, and persons of diverse gender identities. The Act stipulates that any act or policy resulting in discrimination, exclusion, or restriction of rights on the basis of sex or gender shall be considered unfair gender discrimination, except in cases justified by national security, religious principles, or legitimate public interests as prescribed by law.

The Act also establishes institutional mechanisms to advance gender equality, including the Gender Equality Promotion Committee, which is responsible for formulating national policies and measures; the Committee on Consideration of Unfair Gender Discrimination, which handles complaints and adjudication processes; and the Gender Equality Promotion Fund, which supports remedies and activities related to gender equality promotion.

In the context of climate change adaptation and disaster risk management, the Act serves as a supportive framework for human rights and equality, contributing to the promotion of gender-responsive approaches and the protection of vulnerable groups. It encourages government agencies to consider equitable access to information, services, assistance, and public measures without discrimination on the basis of sex or gender.

#### Child Protection Act (2003)

The Child Protection Act (2003) establishes legal measures to safeguard the rights, welfare, and development of children in Thailand. The Act provides protection against neglect, abuse, exploitation, and other conditions that may adversely affect children’s well-being. It also outlines responsibilities for government agencies, communities, and families in ensuring child protection and access to essential services.

Within the context of climate change adaptation and disaster risk management, children are recognized as a particularly vulnerable population group due to their physical, developmental, and social characteristics. The Act supports adaptation efforts by promoting access to healthcare, education, social protection, and emergency assistance for children affected by climate-related hazards and disasters.

#### Older Persons Act (2003)

The Older Persons Act (2003) provides a legal framework for promoting the welfare, protection, and quality of life of older persons in Thailand. The Act supports access to healthcare services, social assistance, welfare benefits, and other measures aimed at improving the well-being and security of older populations.

As Thailand transitions into an ageing society, the Act has increasing relevance to climate change adaptation. Older persons are generally more vulnerable to heat stress, extreme weather events, disasters, and climate-sensitive health risks. The Act contributes to resilience-building by supporting social protection mechanisms and ensuring that the needs of older persons are considered in adaptation planning, disaster preparedness, and emergency response measures.

#### Persons with Disabilities Empowerment Act (2007)

The Persons with Disabilities Empowerment Act (2007) promotes equal rights, accessibility, and opportunities for persons with disabilities. The Act establishes measures to improve access to public services, healthcare, education, employment, information, and social protection while reducing barriers that may limit participation in society.

In relation to climate change adaptation and disaster risk reduction, persons with disabilities often face disproportionate risks during emergencies and disasters due to physical, communication, and social barriers. The Act supports inclusive adaptation by encouraging accessible information systems, inclusive disaster preparedness measures, equitable access to assistance services, and participation of persons with disabilities in decision-making processes related to climate resilience.

#### Draft Biodiversity Act

Thailand is currently developing the Draft Biodiversity Act to strengthen biodiversity conservation and promote the sustainable use of biological resources. The proposed legislation aims to establish a comprehensive framework for biodiversity governance, conservation planning, ecosystem restoration, benefit-sharing mechanisms, and the protection of ecosystem services.

From an adaptation perspective, healthy ecosystems play a critical role in enhancing climate resilience by supporting water regulation, food security, coastal protection, carbon sequestration, and livelihood sustainability. The Draft Biodiversity Act is therefore expected to contribute to ecosystem-based adaptation (EbA) approaches, strengthen ecosystem resilience, and support long-term adaptation and sustainable development objectives.

### 1.2.2 Current and Projected Climate Trends and Climate Hazards  

#### Characteristics of Climate Change

1) **Air Temperature Trends**

Thailand experiences three main seasons: the hot season, the rainy season, and the cool season. In 2024, the country experienced significantly warmer conditions than the long-term average. The annual mean temperature was recorded at 28.5°C, which is 1.1°C higher than the 30-year climatological normal (1991–2020) of 27.4°C. This marks the highest annual mean temperature in Thailand over the past 74 years (1951–2024), exceeding the previous record observed in 2023, when the annual mean temperature was 28.1°C.

Throughout the year 2024, temperature anomalies were consistently above normal across all regions. The hot season was characterized by prolonged periods of high temperatures, while the rainy season exhibited variability in precipitation distribution, with some areas experiencing below-average rainfall and others facing intense rainfall events. The cool season was generally shorter and less pronounced than usual (TMD, 2024b). 
 

To assess future air temperature trends, Thailand utilizes outputs from the EC-Earth3-Veg global climate model under the framework of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6). These outputs are dynamically downscaled using a Regional Climate Model (RegCM) to enhance spatial resolution for national-scale analysis. 

In a near-term period (2021–2040), Tmean is projected to increase by approximately 0.98–1.34°C, rising to 1.42–1.98°C in the mid-century period (2041–2060), and reaching 2.01–3.39°C by the end of the century (2081–2100). The SSP5-8.5 scenario exhibits significantly higher temperature increases compared to SSP2-4.5, reflecting the strong dependence of temperature change on greenhouse gas emission levels.

When considering daily temperature components, both maximum temperature (Tmax) and minimum temperature (Tmin) show increasing trends throughout all future periods. Tmax is projected to increase by approximately 0.9–1.16°C in the near term, 1.33–1.84°C in the mid-century period, and up to 1.92–3.42°C by the end of the century. This trend reflects an increase in both the intensity and frequency of extreme heat events and heatwaves.

Meanwhile, Tmin is projected to increase at a slightly higher rate, with increases of approximately 1.08–1.43°C in the near term, 1.47–2.08°C in the mid-century period, and up to 2.03–3.49°C by the end of the century. This indicates a rise in nighttime temperatures and a higher frequency of “warm nights.”   

These trends are consistent with the analysis of temperature-based climate extreme indices, which indicate changes in the intensity, duration, and frequency of extreme temperature events. **The diurnal temperature range (DTR)** is projected to decrease under both scenarios, reflecting a faster increase in nighttime temperatures compared to daytime temperatures. This trend is more pronounced in the Northern and Northeastern regions of Thailand. At the same time, **the Warm Spell Duration Index (WSDI)** is projected to increase across all regions, particularly in the mid- and late-century periods, with greater changes under SSP5-8.5. This indicates that periods of abnormally high temperatures are expected to become longer in duration.

In addition, **the number of summer days (SU35),** representing the frequency of extremely hot days, is projected to increase continuously under both scenarios, indicating a significant rise in the frequency of extreme temperature events in the future. The differing rates of increase between Tmax and Tmin contribute to a reduction in the diurnal temperature range, reflecting increased heat retention in the atmosphere during nighttime and changes in surface–atmosphere energy and moisture exchange processes.

Furthermore, rising temperatures are associated with changes in seasonal characteristics. The hot season is projected to become longer and start earlier, while the cool season is expected to shorten and become less distinct. These changes reflect shifts in the surface–atmosphere energy balance and may influence monsoon dynamics and regional atmospheric circulation patterns.

2) **Precipitation Trends**

Overall precipitation levels remained relatively high. In 2024, total annual rainfall amounted to 1,704.4 millimeters, exceeding the long-term average by 81.5 millimeters, or approximately 5 percent (the 1991–2020 average annual rainfall is 1,622.9 millimeters). This total ranks as the 21st highest annual rainfall recorded over the past 74 years and is 183.8 millimeters higher than in 2023\. Rainfall was relatively low during the early part of the year, after which both the amount and spatial distribution of precipitation increased, particularly during July, August, and September, when Thailand typically experiences peak rainfall. During these months, episodes of heavy to very heavy rainfall were reported intermittently, resulting in flooding across many areas, including prolonged inundation in some locations, especially in the Northern and Northeastern regions. In the Southern region, several areas experienced severe flooding in late November, followed by another episode of significant flooding in mid-December due to consecutive days of extremely heavy rainfall (TMD, 2024b).

The precipitation projection results relative to the baseline period (1960–2014) indicate that annual precipitation is expected to increase across all regions of Thailand, with greater increases under SSP5-8.5 compared to SSP2-4.5. Spatially, the western part of the Central region, the Eastern region, and the Southern region are projected to experience larger increases, particularly in the far future period (2081–2100), reflecting spatial variability in climate change impacts.

The assessment of future precipitation trends in Thailand, outputs from the EC-Earth3-Veg global climate model under the framework of the CMIP6 are dynamically downscaled using a RegCM, under the GHG emission scenarios SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5.


Despite the overall increase in total annual precipitation, rainfall distribution is projected to become more variable. Rainfall is expected to occur in more intense, short-duration events, while longer dry spells may occur in certain periods. This reflects an intensification of the hydrological cycle under rising temperatures. These changes are further supported by precipitation-based climate extreme indices. **The maximum 1-day precipitation (Rx1day)**, representing rainfall intensity, is projected to increase in many regions, particularly in the Southern, Eastern, and Central parts of the country. **The number of very heavy precipitation days (R20mm)** is also projected to increase, indicating a higher frequency of extreme rainfall events. In contrast, **the number of consecutive wet days (CWD)**, which reflects rainfall persistence, shows mixed changes across regions, suggesting spatial variability in the duration of wet periods. At the same time, the number of dry days is projected to increase in certain areas, particularly in the Southern region, indicating a tendency toward longer dry spells despite the increase in total rainfall.

Seasonally, changes in precipitation are associated with shifts in monsoon dynamics, which may lead to increased irregularity in the timing and intensity of the rainy season. These changes have important implications for flood and drought risks across different regions of Thailand.


3) **Sea Surface Temperature**

Sea Surface Temperature (SST) is a key indicator of climate change in the ocean system, particularly in Thailand’s territorial waters, which cover both the Gulf of Thailand and the Andaman Sea. Long-term observations and satellite-based analyses over recent decades indicate a significant warming signal in sea surface temperatures. This trend has implications for marine ecosystems as well as the country’s socio-economic structure (Kongdee, 2024).

Seasonally, SST in Thailand exhibits clear variability driven by monsoon influences. The Gulf of Thailand, characterized as a shallow and semi-enclosed basin (Koad et al., 2012), tends to retain heat more efficiently than open ocean systems. SST generally increases from the beginning of the year, reaching its peak during April–May, before declining during the monsoon season. This pattern is associated with seasonal changes in wind direction and ocean circulation linked to the South China Sea and the Indian Ocean (Na-u-dom et al., 2020\). The relatively limited water exchange in the Gulf further enhances heat accumulation compared to more open marine environments such as the Andaman Sea.

The situation in 2024 reflects an intensification of this warming trend. Abnormally high SSTs were recorded in several parts of Thailand’s waters, particularly in the eastern Gulf of Thailand, where temperatures reached approximately 32.7°C in May. This value represents one of the highest SSTs ever recorded in the area, highlighting the increasing magnitude and persistence of ocean warming in recent decades (Kongdee, 2024; Na-u-dom et al., 2020; NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, 2024).

The increasing trend in SST in Thailand is consistent with global climate change, primarily driven by the accumulation of GHGs in the atmosphere, which enhances ocean heat uptake. In addition, large-scale climate variability, such as ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation), plays an important role in intensifying anomalously high SST during certain periods.

Future SST projections are based on outputs from global climate models under the CMIP6 framework, as part of the CORDEX Southeast Asia initiative. Selected models with strong performance in simulating the regional climate include EC-Earth3-Veg, MIROC6, and CMCC-ESM2. These outputs are dynamically downscaled using the regional climate model RegCM5.0 to a spatial resolution of 25 × 25 km, covering the historical period (1981–2014) and future projections (2015–2100) under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios.

The future SST projection indicate that SST in Thailand is projected to increase continuously throughout the 21st century. Under the intermediate emissions scenario (SSP2-4.5), SST is expected to rise gradually, while under the high-emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5), the increase is more rapid and pronounced, particularly toward the end of the century. This trend may lead to significant ecological consequences, including widespread and potentially irreversible coral bleaching.

#### Change in Climate Events

1) **Flood**

Over the past decades, climate change has significantly altered rainfall patterns in Thailand in terms of variability, frequency, and intensity, resulting in more frequent and severe flood events. According to the DDPM, during the period 1989–2024, floods have been the most damaging natural hazard and have affected large areas of the country on a recurring basis.

In 2022, Thailand experienced widespread flooding across 69 provinces due to a strengthened southwest monsoon and a monsoon trough extending over the upper part of the country, resulting in sustained heavy rainfall and river overflows in major basins such as the Chao Phraya, Chi, and Mun Rivers (HII, 2022). In 2024, flooding continued to affect several regions, particularly the North and Northeast, driven by persistent heavy rainfall associated with monsoon systems, resulting in flash floods and riverine flooding in multiple provinces.

These events highlight that increasing flood severity in Thailand is driven by the interaction between climatic and non-climatic factors. Rising global temperatures enhance the atmosphere’s moisture-holding capacity, leading to more intense rainfall over shorter durations. At the same time, urban expansion, loss of permeable surfaces, and limitations in drainage infrastructure reduce the capacity to manage excess water, thereby exacerbating both the severity and duration of flooding events.

Regarding future projections, assessments by DCCE, indicate that Thailand is likely to experience more frequent and intense heavy rainfall events. These projections are based on climate model outputs from the CMIP6 under the SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, with particularly pronounced increases under the high-emission scenario (SSP5-8.5). In addition, the duration of extreme rainfall events is expected to increase, leading to greater accumulation of rainfall, and consequently higher risks of runoff, flash floods, and riverine flooding. Flood-prone areas are also projected to expand, particularly in the Chao Phraya River Basin, the eastern region, and the southern part of the country.

In terms of event frequency (return period), large-scale flood events in the Chao Phraya River Basin are projected to occur more frequently. Events that previously had an estimated return period of approximately 120 years (e.g., the 2006 flood level) may decrease to once every 19 years under SSP2-4.5, or as frequently as once every 6 years under SSP5-8.5 during the period 2040–2059. Furthermore, there is a 50% probability that a flood event comparable in severity to the 2011 flood could occur again by 2050, reflecting a substantial increase in systemic flood risk in Thailand’s major river basins (World Bank, 2025b).

At the subnational level, provinces in the Chao Phraya River Basin, such as Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya, Pathum Thani, Nonthaburi, Chainat, and Nakhon Sawan, are projected to face high risks of river overflow and urban flooding. Coastal and southern provinces, including Nakhon Si Thammarat, Songkhla, Trang, and Pattani, are expected to experience increased risks from heavy rainfall, monsoon influences, and potential sea-level rise.

Major urban areas, particularly Bangkok, face compound risks from intense rainfall, surface runoff, and limitations in drainage infrastructure. These risks are further exacerbated by the city’s low-lying topography, with an elevation of only approximately 0.5–1.5 meters above mean sea level, and ongoing land subsidence. Without adequate adaptation measures, large parts of Bangkok could face severe flood risks by mid-century (World Bank, 2025b).

2) **Drought**

Thailand has been increasingly affected by drought conditions that are becoming more severe and complex, largely driven by climate variability. Key contributing factors include variability in the southwest monsoon, anomalous positioning of the monsoon trough, and the influence of the El Niño phenomenon, all of which result in reduced rainfall and uneven spatial and temporal distribution of precipitation.

Based on statistical data from 1989 to 2024, drought conditions in Thailand exhibit high variability. In some years, impacts are relatively localized, while in others, drought events cause severe damage at the national level. A notable example is the 2015 drought, which was one of the most severe in recent decades and the worst in approximately 15 years. This event significantly affected agricultural production, water storage systems, and domestic water supply across many parts of the country.

In addition to climatic drivers, structural constraints in water resource management further exacerbate drought vulnerability. In particular, unequal access to water resources remains a critical issue in the agricultural sector, where a large proportion of farming households lack reliable access to permanent water sources, and only a limited share is covered by irrigation systems.

Future drought projections based on climate models under the CMIP6, using SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 scenarios, assessment by the DCCE and publicly available through the CCIC, indicate that Thailand is likely to experience a more pronounced and intensified cycle of “severe floods and extreme droughts.” Although total annual rainfall is projected to increase, precipitation is expected to occur in shorter, more intense events with uneven distribution, thereby sustaining high drought risks in certain areas.

In addition, drought hazard intensity is projected to increase, particularly in parts of the northeastern and central regions. Provinces projected to face persistently high drought risk through 2100 include Bueng Kan, Udon Thani, Nong Khai, Amnat Charoen, and Sukhothai. Moreover, Nakhon Si Thammarat is identified as an area with a notable increase in drought risk under the SSP5-8.5 scenario, reflecting rising urban water demand combined with limitations in water management systems.

3) **Landslide**

Landslides in Thailand are primarily triggered by prolonged and intense rainfall, which leads to the infiltration and accumulation of water within soil layers. This process increases pore-water pressure and reduces soil shear strength, thereby weakening slope stability. When combined with steep topographic conditions—particularly in mountainous areas of the northern and southern regions—surface runoff rapidly flows downslope, further exacerbating slope instability and increasing the likelihood of slope failure.

In addition, land use plays a critical role in influencing landslide risk. Deforestation, the conversion of forested areas into agricultural land, and infrastructure development on steep slopes significantly reduce root cohesion and the soil’s ability to retain structural integrity. These changes increase soil susceptibility to saturation, thereby substantially elevating the probability of landslides during periods of heavy rainfall (Tetteh et al., 2025).

Thailand has experienced recurrent landslide events, particularly in mountainous regions. Notable examples include the landslide event in Uttaradit Province in 2006 and multiple events in southern Thailand associated with accumulated monsoonal rainfall. More recently, in 2024, over 310 landslide incidents were recorded nationwide. Significant events occurred in Phuket, Chiang Rai, and Chiang Mai, largely influenced by tropical storms that brought sustained heavy rainfall over steep terrain.

In terms of future projections, an ensemble of 10 GCMs under GHG emission scenarios RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5 has been applied to simulate future rainfall patterns. These outputs were integrated with slope stability models to estimate the distributed probability of slope failure, based on the relationship between cumulative rainfall and soil stability conditions.

The results from climate and slope stability modeling clearly indicate that the probability of landslide occurrence in extreme events—such as those associated with a 100-year return period—is projected to increase significantly. Under the RCP 4.5 scenario, such probabilities may rise by up to approximately 90% across more than 40% of the country, while under RCP 8.5, high-risk areas could expand to cover up to 80% of the national territory. These increases are particularly pronounced in mountainous regions of northern and southern Thailand, where steep slopes and high rainfall sensitivity prevail (Komori et al., 2018).

Spatially, the models suggest that high hazard zones are likely to expand beyond existing mountainous cores into adjacent foothill areas and regions characterized by intensive land use. Areas undergoing agricultural expansion or deforestation are especially vulnerable, as reduced soil cohesion and increased soil erosion (soil loss) further compromise slope stability.

From a temporal perspective, projections for the mid-century period (approximately 2040–2059) indicate that, under RCP 4.5, landslide risk will increase through a steady rise in the frequency of moderate-to-severe events. Under RCP 8.5, however, the increase is more pronounced, with both the frequency and intensity of landslides rising significantly, alongside a marked expansion of areas with very high probability (probability \> 0.9).

By the end of the century (approximately 2070–2100), the models indicate a structural shift in the risk regime. Events that were historically considered low-frequency but high-impact are projected to occur more frequently, effectively becoming part of the baseline risk in many regions. Under RCP 8.5, large portions of the country—excluding some low-lying central plains—may experience persistently high probabilities of landslide occurrence.

This process significantly reduces soil shear strength and lowers the rainfall threshold required to trigger landslides. 4) **Windstorm and Cyclone**

Windstorms in Thailand constitute a high-frequency natural hazard driven primarily by strong winds, often associated with tropical cyclones, summer storms, and convective thunderstorm systems. Over the past decade, windstorms have consistently ranked among the most recurrent disasters across the country, affecting a wide geographical area. However, in terms of long-term trends, there is no clear increasing or decreasing pattern in their frequency of occurrence. Instead, windstorm events are characterized by high interannual variability and a tendency toward episodic extremes. These events are most prevalent during the summer season, driven by summer storms, and during the rainy season, when the influence of tropical cyclones and the monsoon is more pronounced.

In addition, climate change is contributing to shifts in the characteristics of windstorms. There is increasing evidence of more intense and abrupt wind gusts, along with a broader spatial extent of impacts. These changes are closely linked to rising temperatures and enhanced atmospheric instability, which in turn promote more frequent and severe convective storms and strong wind events in certain areas.

Concurrently, tropical cyclones—another major driver of wind-related hazards in Thailand—continue to play a significant role in the country’s climate system. Over the 74-year period from 1951 to 2024, Thailand has been affected by a total of 206 tropical cyclones making landfall or moving into the country, corresponding to an average of approximately 2.78 events per year. The majority of these systems were classified as tropical depressions, while tropical storms and typhoons accounted for a significantly smaller proportion. Seasonally, tropical cyclones predominantly occur during the latter half of the year, particularly from August to November, which together account for 80.6% of all events. The peak months are October (27.2%) and September (26.7%), whereas no tropical cyclones have been recorded entering Thailand during February and March throughout the entire period, clearly reflecting the seasonal nature of these systems in the region.

The annual frequency of tropical cyclones entering Thailand exhibits considerable interannual variability and is influenced by large-scale climate variability, particularly the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO). In some years, no cyclones make landfall, while in others the number exceeds the long-term average. Notably, since around 2012, there has been a slight declining trend in the number of tropical cyclones affecting Thailand compared to earlier decades.

Under the context of climate change, the characteristics of tropical cyclones are projected to evolve. There is increasing evidence that cyclones may move more slowly and weaken more gradually after landfall, resulting in prolonged periods of heavy rainfall and heightened risks of accumulated flooding. In addition, cyclones are likely to penetrate further inland and exhibit a poleward shift in both genesis and intensification regions. The potential for rapid intensification prior to landfall is also expected to increase, posing significant challenges for forecasting and disaster preparedness.

Future projections are based on climate simulations from the CMIP6, employing a downscaling approach to generate more than 64,000 synthetic tropical cyclones from nine global climate models. These simulations consider both intermediate (SSP2-4.5) and high-emission (SSP5-8.5) scenarios and compare historical, present-day, and late 21st-century conditions. The results indicate substantial changes in cyclone intensity, behavior, and duration across Southeast Asia, including Thailand.

In the case of Thailand, particularly Bangkok, despite its inland location, projections suggest a significant increase in extreme wind intensity. For the most severe events (99th percentile), maximum wind speeds may increase by more than 100% relative to historical conditions. Concurrently, the duration of cyclone impacts is projected to lengthen, with extreme cases increasing by approximately 72% under the SSP2-4.5 scenario. Cyclones are also expected to exhibit slower translation speeds over land, delayed dissipation, and deeper inland penetration, all of which contribute to elevated risks of prolonged rainfall and flooding.

5) **Forest Fire**

Over the past decade, Thailand has experienced a high frequency and extensive impacts of forest fires, with a cumulative total of more than 60,000 incidents, accounting for over 1.4 million rai of burned area. The northern region has consistently recorded the highest occurrence of forest fires in the country. In particular, during the period 2015–2024, a total of 42,305 fire incidents were reported, resulting in 984,143 rai of affected area, clearly reflecting the ecological vulnerability of this region.

Seasonally, forest fires typically begin in January or February, intensify to their peak in March, and gradually subside with the onset of the rainy season in May. The first ten days of March represent the most critical period, during which fire intensity reaches its highest levels. More than 90% of forest fires are attributed to human activities, with the primary causes being forest product collection and hunting, followed by agricultural burning for land preparation and negligence (DNP, 2026).

In recent years, the dynamics of forest fires in Thailand have become increasingly complex, influenced by both climate change and shifts in land use. Global warming and the El Niño phenomenon have contributed to prolonged drought conditions, rising temperatures, and reduced precipitation, all of which facilitate the accumulation of dry fuel and accelerate fire spread (World Bank, 2025a). In addition, recurring burn patterns have been observed, with approximately 67% of previously burned areas experiencing at least two fire events, and around 22% experiencing five or more recurrences during 2014–2023. This indicates a persistent cycle of repeated burning that is difficult to reverse (Bihari et al., 2025).

At the same time, the expansion of commercial agriculture—particularly crops such as rice, sugarcane, and maize, as well as the regeneration of grass on livestock grazing land—has further driven the use of fire for land clearing, thereby increasing the risk of forest fires across multiple regions (Bihari et al., 2025). Beyond impacts on natural resources, forest fires are also directly linked to air pollution, especially the rise of PM2.5 concentrations to hazardous levels, which continue to pose significant health risks to populations in northern Thailand and surrounding areas.

The projection of wildfire occurrence in Thailand was conducted through the application of geospatial analysis techniques in conjunction with a 10-year dataset of fire hotspots, with the aim of examining the spatial and temporal distribution patterns of wildfires and assessing future risk across different regions of the country. The results indicate that wildfire occurrence in Thailand exhibits a clear seasonal pattern, predominantly occurring during the dry season from January to May, with a high concentration in the northern region. In particular, areas characterized by dry dipterocarp and deciduous forests, moderate elevations, and sloping terrain show a higher susceptibility to fire.

At the provincial level, several northern provinces, including Chiang Mai, Chiang Rai, and Mae Hong Son, experience a high frequency of wildfire events due to their favorable topographic and ecological conditions, combined with agricultural land use practices and ease of access to forest areas. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that more than 50% of wildfire-prone areas are distributed across 22 administrative regions, with some protected areas also identified as being at high risk. Key factors influencing wildfire occurrence include drought conditions, soil moisture, temperature, precipitation, and vegetation status, as well as topographic characteristics such as elevation and slope (Talukdar et al., 2024).

When considered alongside climate trends, increasing temperatures are likely to enhance evapotranspiration and accelerate soil moisture loss, leading to faster drying of vegetation and greater accumulation of combustible fuel. Meanwhile, precipitation patterns are becoming more variable, particularly with reduced rainfall during the dry season and increasingly uneven distribution, resulting in prolonged periods of drought.

6) **Sea Level Rise**

Sea level in Thailand has exhibited a continuous rising trend since the mid-20th century. However, the rate of increase varies across locations and time periods. During 1951–1991, observations from the Chao Phraya River mouth (Fort Phrachul) indicate a relatively high rate of relative sea level rise (rSLR) of approximately 16.4 ± 0.85 mm/year, which is considerably higher than the global average. In contrast, stations along the Andaman coast, such as Ko Taphao Noi, show a much lower rate of about 2.3 ± 1.1 mm/year. Furthermore, for the period 1940–2014, the average rate of sea level rise along the Thai coastline is estimated at around 6 mm/year, reflecting the combined influence of global mean sea level rise and local land-level changes (Narenpitak et al., 2025\)

In the context of Bangkok, this trend is further intensified by land subsidence. Historically, excessive groundwater extraction led to a rapid increase in relative sea level, reaching rates as high as approximately 0.021 m/year prior to 2004\. However, following the implementation of groundwater regulation measures since 1997, the rate has declined to approximately 0.013 m/year (Supharatid & Rojpratak, 2026).

Although the overall trend indicates a rise, the rate of change is not constant. In recent years, the rate of increase in some areas of the Gulf of Thailand has decreased to approximately 3.0–5.5 mm/year, largely due to reduced land subsidence following groundwater management measures. At the same time, sea level exhibits seasonal variability driven by monsoonal winds and ocean dynamics. Sea levels in the Gulf of Thailand may increase by about 0.2 m during the northeast monsoon, while along the Andaman coast, increases of around 0.1 m are observed during the southwest monsoon. These findings highlight that sea level rise in Thailand results from the interaction of multiple factors, including global sea level changes and local land-level variations, leading to non-linear and spatially heterogeneous patterns.

Regarding future projections, GCMs under the frameworks of CMIP6 and GeoMIP6 have been employed to assess future sea level trends. These include greenhouse gas emission scenarios such as SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5, as well as solar radiation modification (SRM) scenarios to evaluate their potential in mitigating long-term sea level rise. The simulation results indicate that sea levels in Thailand will continue to rise over the coming decades, with spatial variability in the rate of increase. The upper Gulf of Thailand, particularly areas near Bangkok, is projected to experience higher rates than the global average due to historical land subsidence. In contrast, other coastal areas, such as the Andaman coast, are expected to experience lower rates closer to the global mean. Nevertheless, climate model projections suggest an acceleration of sea level rise in the future. Under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, the rate of increase is projected to be approximately 2 mm/year, while under SSP5-8.5, it may exceed 5 mm/year by the end of the century (Narenpitak et al., 2025).

For the Bangkok Metropolitan Region, future impacts are projected to intensify significantly. Rising sea levels are expected to lead to a continuous expansion of flood-prone areas. Compared to the 2011 flood event, inundated areas are projected to increase by approximately 3.2–3.6% by 2030, rising further to 8.2–8.4% by 2050, 14–15.1% by 2070, and potentially reaching 21.4–26.2% by 2090\.

Rainfall is projected to increase by approximately 12.72–21.47% by 2100, while discharge from the Chao Phraya River may rise by as much as 39–51% by 2090\. 7) **Extreme Temperature**

The occurrence of extreme temperature, particularly extreme heat, in Thailand is defined based on the criteria of the TMD, which uses daily maximum temperature as the primary indicator. Temperatures between 35.0–39.9°C are classified as “hot,” while temperatures equal to or exceeding 40.0°C are classified as “very hot” (TMD, 2026). 

Long-term observations since 1951 indicate a clear warming trend across Thailand, with increases observed in both annual mean temperature and annual maximum temperature. Data from the TMD show a consistent rise in annual mean maximum temperature over recent decades, consistent with scientific evidence indicating an increase in both the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events (Kliengchuay et al., 2024).

In 2024, Thailand recorded an annual mean temperature of approximately 28.5°C, the highest in more than 70 years of observations (TMD, 2024b). During April, extreme heat conditions were observed across much of the country, with a maximum temperature of 44.2°C recorded in Thoen District, Lampang Province. Although this value did not exceed the national record of 44.6°C observed in Mae Hong Son (year 2016\) and Tak (year 2023), it reflects the increasing likelihood of temperatures approaching historical extremes (TMD, 2023).

Spatially, extreme heat occurs most frequently in the northern region and the upper central region, particularly in provinces such as Tak, Mae Hong Son, and Lampang. These areas are characterized by basin-like topography, which limits ventilation and enhances heat accumulation. The northeastern region is also prone to high temperatures due to relatively dry conditions and the influence of heat-induced low-pressure systems during the pre-monsoon period.

Moreover, the drivers of extreme heat in Thailand are multifaceted. At the global scale, the ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation), particularly El Niño events, contributes to higher temperatures and reduced precipitation, leading to intensified heat conditions (Madolli et al., 2025). At the regional scale, strong solar radiation, heat-induced low-pressure systems, and relatively stagnant atmospheric conditions during the pre-monsoon season contribute to the accumulation of heat near the surface. At the local scale, urbanization and land-use change contribute to the urban heat island effect, further amplifying temperature extremes (Aung et al., 2024). These factors, combined with long-term warming driven by greenhouse gas emissions, reinforce the observed upward trend in temperature.

### 1.2.3 Observed and Potential Risk, Impacts, and Vulnerability of Climate Change  

Thailand is recognized as one of the most vulnerable nations to the global climate crisis. According to the **Global Climate Risk Index 2026**, Thailand is ranked **17th among the most affected countries in 2024**, a position driven by the increasing frequency and severity of extreme weather events.

From a long-term perspective (1995–2024), Thailand’s risk profile is characterized by staggering economic and social costs. The country is ranked **4th globally in Absolute Losses**, signifying that the total monetary value of assets and infrastructure destroyed by climate-related disasters is among the highest in the world. Parallel to this economic burden is the human cost, with Thailand ranking **6th in the total number of people affected** by climate hazards. While the country’s ranking in terms of losses relative to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) (Rank 24\) suggests a degree of economic resilience, the high rank in absolute losses highlights the massive scale of wealth and development that remains at risk.

The data for 2024 reflects a continuing trend of high-intensity climate shocks. In this single year, Thailand was ranked **11th in Absolute Losses**, indicating that recent extreme weather variability continues to inflict significant financial damage. Perhaps more alarming is the shift in mortality rates; the country’s ranking for fatalities rose to **24th globally in 2024**, which is considerably higher than its long-term average of 35th. This rise suggests that new climate-driven threats, such as lethal heatwaves and increasingly unpredictable flash floods, are posing an escalating danger to public health and human life (Adil et al., 2025).

According to World Bank (2025b), in the absence of effective adaptation measures, Thailand’s economy is projected to experience significant long-term impacts, with GDP potentially declining by 7–14 % by 2050\. Extreme weather events can also generate substantial economic losses and undermine growth stability.

In the social dimension, climate impacts are likely to exacerbate existing inequalities, particularly among low-income populations who face constraints in resources and access to services, resulting in higher vulnerability and slower recovery. At the same time, public health risks are expected to increase, driven by water-borne diseases and heat-related impacts.

1. #### *Observed Risk, Impacts, and Vulnerability of Climate Change*

Climate change represents a significant structural risk to Thailand’s economic and social development, constraining growth, livelihoods, and public health. The NESDC has consistently highlighted climate variability and extreme weather events as key factors limiting Thailand’s GDP growth, particularly through their impacts on primary production and human well-being (NESDC, 2024b). The economic effects of climate change were evident from the beginning of 2024, when the agricultural sector contracted by 3.5% year-on-year in the first quarter due to unfavorable weather conditions and reduced water availability, affecting crop yields. This reflects the high vulnerability of Thailand’s agricultural sector and poses ongoing risks to rural incomes and national economic performance. Historical evidence further indicates that severe climate-related disasters, such as major flooding events, can generate economic losses equivalent to approximately 0.27% of annual GDP in highly affected years, underscoring the growing macroeconomic cost of climate change (Sowcharoensuk, 2024). These pressures have also translated into labor market impacts, with agricultural employment declining by 3.6% year-on-year in the fourth quarter of 2024, highlighting structural exposure of livelihoods to climate variability (NESDC, 2024a). 

Observed climate change impacts in Thailand are interconnected and affect multiple sectors both directly and indirectly, reflecting the complexity of systemic and compound risks. In the water sector, changes in precipitation patterns, rising temperatures, and increasing frequency of extreme weather events have significantly affected water resource management. Intensified rainfall and stronger storms have increased the risk of flash floods and river overflows across multiple regions, damaging infrastructure, irrigation systems, and water supply sources (Chaiwino et al., 2025; Petpongpan et al., 2021). At the same time, rising temperatures and irregular rainfall patterns have contributed to more prolonged and severe droughts, reducing water availability in rivers, reservoirs, and groundwater, while also degrading water quality due to higher pollutant concentrations. The increasing spatial and temporal variability of water availability further complicates long-term planning, particularly in managing risks of reservoir overflow during wet periods and shortages during dry seasons. These hydrological impacts cascade into other sectors, particularly tourism, which is affected by floods, storms, heatwaves, sea level rise, increasing sea surface temperatures, coral bleaching, and the spread of infectious diseases. Such impacts directly damage tourism assets and infrastructure, reduce visitor numbers, and affect local income, while also contributing to population displacement, labor shortages, and congestion in destination areas.

In the health sector, climate change has increasingly manifested through adverse outcomes linked to both environmental exposure and social vulnerability. In 2024, cases of monitored communicable diseases increased by 31.8% year-on-year, with influenza contributing the largest share (NESDC, 2024a). Air pollution has further intensified these risks, with approximately 12 million illness cases recorded in 2024, representing approximately 10% increase from 2023\. Impacts are spatially concentrated in the northern region, particularly in provinces such as Chiang Mai and Chiang Rai, where recurrent haze and high PM2.5 levels persist, reflecting heightened vulnerability among children, the elderly, individuals with pre-existing conditions, and outdoor workers. Heat exposure has also contributed to increased morbidity, particularly among vulnerable occupational groups such as farmers, general laborers, and students, with a clear upward trend (The Nation, 2026). This is further reflected in heat-related mortality, with 63 deaths reported in 2024, predominantly among males and older adults (average age 62), with the highest concentration in the Northeast (54%). Over half of the deceased had underlying diseases, and key risk factors included prolonged outdoor exposure and alcohol consumption during extreme heat. Mortality was strongly seasonal, peaking in April when temperatures reached up to 44°C, indicating heightened vulnerability driven by both exposure and health sensitivity (Department of Disease Control, 2025\). In addition, climate change affects food availability and access, contributing to malnutrition. Stunting among children aged 0–5 increased from 10.5% in 2020 to 12.9% in 2024, and among children aged 6–14 from 8.7% to 9.4%. Similarly, wasting increased among children aged 0–5 (from 5.6% to 6.7%) and 6–14 (from 4.4% to 5.8%), reflecting growing nutritional vulnerability (DOH, 2025a).

Impacts on natural resources and ecosystems are similarly significant. Climate change, combined with human activities, has accelerated ecosystem degradation and biodiversity loss. Coastal erosion remains a critical issue, particularly along the Gulf of Thailand, driven by waves, storms, and flooding. Between 2019 and 2024, erosion was recorded in more than 77 sub-districts, with the most severe loss observed in Bang Tawa Sub-district, Pattani Province (GISTDA, 2026). Marine ecosystems are also under pressure, with coral bleaching driven by rising sea surface temperatures, which lead to the expulsion of zooxanthellae from coral tissues, weakening corals and potentially causing mortality. Coral bleaching in Thailand was first recorded in 1991 in the Andaman Sea and has recurred repeatedly since then. Major global bleaching events occurred in 1998, 2010, 2016, and 2024\. In 2024, sea surface temperatures were higher than in previous severe bleaching years, and by May 2024, 86.6% of coral reefs nationwide were affected (76.7% in the Andaman Sea and 92.3% in the Gulf of Thailand) (DMCR, 2024). Meanwhile, forest cover in Thailand has shown a slight declining trend. In 2024, forest area accounted for 31.46% of the country (approximately 101.79 million rai), decreasing by 0.03% from the previous year. Over the past decade (2015–2024), forest areas increased by 331,952 rai but declined by 832,081 rai overall, reflecting net losses largely driven by land-use change, forest fires, and development activities, despite gains from reforestation and natural regeneration. Rising temperatures and reduced forest moisture further increase fire risk, contributing to carbon emissions, biodiversity loss, and ecosystem degradation (Seub Nakhasathien Foundation, 2025).

Finally, human settlements and urban systems face increasing climate risks, particularly in densely populated and coastal areas. Sea level rise, coastal erosion, storms, and droughts threaten housing security and infrastructure resilience, while urban areas are increasingly affected by the Urban Heat Island Effect due to rapid urbanization and the dominance of heat-retaining materials such as concrete and steel. These conditions elevate urban temperatures, increase energy demand, and negatively affect public health and quality of life, potentially leading to long-term displacement and heightened risks to human security**.** 

#### Potential Risk, Impacts, and Vulnerability of Climate Change

Thailand has conducted future climate projections using scenario-based models and GHG pathways, namely SSP 4.5 and 8.5. These projections are aligned with the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) risk framework and are used to assess sector-specific vulnerabilities under different climate futures. Climate risk maps across priority sectors can be accessed at: [https://ccic.dcce.go.th/riskarea](https://ccic.dcce.go.th/riskarea).

Under mid-century scenarios (2041–2060), climate risks in Thailand are expected to intensify and increasingly interact across sectors, driven by rising temperatures, changes in precipitation patterns, and the growing frequency of extreme events. These changes are not only amplifying individual hazards—such as floods, droughts, and heatwaves—but also creating compound risks that cascade across water systems, agriculture, ecosystems, public health, tourism, and human settlements.

**Water resources** are projected to experience heightened variability, with rising temperatures in the North and Northeast increasing evaporation and intensifying drought conditions, while heavier rainfall in the Central river basins and Southern coastal areas elevates flood risks, particularly in low-lying and hydrologically constrained systems. These dynamics contribute to growing challenges in water allocation, declining water quality, and increasing pressure on infrastructure. The implications extend directly to the **agricultural sector**, where production systems, especially rainfed agriculture in the Northeast are highly exposed to rainfall variability and water shortages. Increasing temperatures further reduce crop yields and quality, particularly for rice, while flooding in the Central plains and parts of the South disrupts production cycles. These combined stresses are likely to reduce farmer income, destabilize supply chains, and increase risks to national food security.

**Tourism** is also expected to face increasing climate-related pressures, with impacts varying across destination types. Coastal areas are particularly exposed to storms, heavy rainfall, and sea-level variability, which disrupt tourism operations and damage infrastructure, while rising temperatures and environmental degradations such as coral bleaching, reduce the attractiveness and competitiveness of destinations. Mountain and nature-based tourism areas are affected by shifting seasonality and ecosystem changes, while urban tourism faces constraints from heat stress, flooding, and infrastructure limitations. These factors contribute to increasing uncertainty in tourism demand and rising operational costs.

**Public health** risks are projected to intensify under the combined influence of heat, flooding, and drought. Rising temperatures are expected to increase the incidence of heat-related illnesses and respiratory conditions, particularly in densely populated urban areas, while flooding creates favorable conditions for the spread of water- and vector-borne diseases. In rural areas, drought conditions may further compromise water quality and sanitation, increasing the risk of infectious diseases. These impacts are compounded by uneven access to healthcare services, with remote and vulnerable populations facing greater constraints. As a result, climate change is expected to contribute to increased morbidity and mortality, more frequent disease outbreaks, and growing risks of malnutrition, placing additional pressure on the public health system.

**Natural resources and ecosystems** are also under increasing stress from both climate variability and human activities. Rising temperatures and prolonged dry conditions increase the risk of forest fires and ecosystem degradation, while heavy rainfall contributes to soil erosion and landslides in mountainous areas. Coastal ecosystems are simultaneously affected by storms and changing ocean conditions, including rising sea temperatures that contribute to widespread coral bleaching. These impacts are particularly severe in degraded or heavily utilized areas, where ecological resilience is already limited, leading to biodiversity loss and a decline in ecosystem services such as carbon sequestration and natural flood regulation.

**Human settlements**, particularly in urban and coastal areas, are facing escalating climate risks. Increasingly intense rainfall leads to urban flooding that disrupts infrastructure, transport systems, and economic activities, while rising temperatures exacerbate heat stress and increase energy demand. Coastal zones are exposed to storms and sea-level variability, and rural areas face growing water insecurity due to drought. These risks are unevenly distributed, with major urban centers generally better equipped to manage impacts, while rural and marginalized communities face greater constraints. The convergence of high population density and multiple climate hazards is expected to amplify risks to infrastructure, livelihoods, and long-term human security.

## 1.3 Priorities and barriers

### 1.3.1 Domestic Adaptation Action Priorities and Progress  

Thailand has made continuous efforts to both mitigate and adapt to the impacts and risks of climate change across multiple dimensions, while recognizing the importance of international cooperation in addressing this global challenge. The country has been a Party to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) since 1994, ratified the Kyoto Protocol in 2002, and acceded to the Paris Agreement in 2016\. Given its high level of vulnerability to climate change **(as outlined in the section on Impacts, risks and vulnerabilities),** Thailand has established a range of policies, plans, and measures to systematically address and manage these impacts.

At the institutional level, Thailand has established the NCCC to provide strategic direction and drive national-level policy implementation. This is complemented by the development of climate change legislation **(see Institutional Arrangements and Legal Framework on Adaptation section)**, as well as the formulation of national strategies and development plans that integrate climate change adaptation. Efforts have also been made to strengthen institutional and technical capacities for climate change management across all sectors **(see Adaptation strategies, Policies, Plans, Goals and Actions to Integrate Adaptation into National Policies and Strategies section).**

To drive climate change adaptation, Thailand has implemented a structured process under the NAP Process, which consists of four key components. First, the assessment of climate risks, vulnerabilities, and impacts. Second, the formulation of policies, strategies, and the NAP, which prioritizes six key sectors: water resources management, agriculture and food security, tourism, public health, natural resources management, and human settlements and human security. These sectors share a common objective of enhancing resilience, reducing vulnerability, and strengthening adaptive capacity in line with specific sectoral and local contexts, ultimately contributing to long-term sustainable development **(see [Thailand’s National Adaptation Plan](https://unfccc.int/documents/638001) for further details).** Thailand is also in the process of developing the Thailand’s Action Plan on Adaptation to operationalize the NAP, aiming to translate policy into practice. This plan is intended to guide lead agencies in each sector in clearly defining their roles, responsibilities, and targets in a systematic manner, while reducing duplication and improving the efficiency of budget and resource allocation. Third, the implementation of adaptation actions, along with monitoring, evaluation, and learning processes, led by the DCCE in collaboration with relevant sectoral agencies. Fourth, monitoring, evaluation, and learning (MEL) processes are being strengthened to assess adaptation progress, effectiveness, and outcomes across sectors. Led by the DCCE in collaboration with relevant agencies, these efforts support evidence-based decision-making, facilitate adaptive management, and contribute to national and international adaptation reporting, including reporting under the UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience and the Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA).

In addition, Thailand places strong emphasis on enabling conditions as key mechanisms to support adaptation across all sectors. These include the development of data systems, research, and technological innovations to support decision-making and early warning systems; the strengthening of policy, legal, and financial mechanisms—such as the advancement of climate budget tagging and the development of guidelines for accessing climate finance for adaptation; as well as capacity building and awareness raising among stakeholders at all levels, including government agencies, the private sector, and the general public. **Further details on progress can be found in the section on Progress on Implementation of Adaptation.**

## 1.4 Adaptation strategies, Policies, Plans, Goals and Actions to Integrate Adaptation into National Policies and Strategies  

Thailand has reported in its first Biennial Transparency Report (BTR1) that it plays a crucial role in achieving the GGA, given its high vulnerability to the impacts of climate change. Thailand recognizes ongoing progress under **the UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience**, which aims to enhance adaptive capacity, strengthen resilience, and reduce vulnerability to climate change at the global level. In this context, Thailand has established a comprehensive policy, institutional, and planning framework to integrate climate change adaptation into national development processes in alignment with **the GGA** with both Dimensional and Thematic Targets **(Detailed in Progress on Implementation of Adaptation Section).**

In addition, **Thailand’s Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0**) further reinforces adaptation as a national priority by strengthening commitments to climate resilience, adaptive capacity, and sectoral adaptation actions in alignment with the Paris Agreement and national development goals. Thailand has recently submitted its updated NDC 3.0 to the UNFCCC, which enhances the country’s climate ambition through more ambitious mitigation targets while reaffirming the importance of adaptation and resilience-building. To support these enhanced commitments, Thailand is currently revising its **Long-term Low Greenhouse Gas Emission Development Strategy (LT-LEDS)**, which will serve as a long-term framework for achieving a low-carbon and climate-resilient future through integrated mitigation and adaptation actions.

At the national level, adaptation is embedded within a structured and hierarchical policy framework. **The 20-Year National Strategy (2018–2037)** provides the overarching vision for sustainable and climate-resilient development. This is operationalized through medium-term frameworks, including **the 13th National Economic and Social Development Plan (2023–2027)** and **the Master Plan under the National Strategy (2023–2037)**, which translate long-term priorities into actionable policy directions. Within this structure, **the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) (2023–2037)** serves as the central framework guiding Thailand’s adaptation efforts. The NAP defines long-term goals to enhance resilience, reduce vulnerability, and strengthen adaptive capacity across key sectors, while ensuring alignment with economic, social, and environmental development objectives. Sectoral adaptation plans, such as those in agriculture and public health, further support the implementation of adaptation measures at the operational level. In addition, related policies and plans support and complement the implementation of the NAP, including **the Policy and Plan for Enhancement and Conservation of National Environmental Quality (2017–2037)** and **the Climate Change Master Plan (2015–2050)**.

To facilitate the translation of policy into action, Thailand is in the process of developing **Thailand’s Action Plan on Adaptation (2025–2030),** which will serve as a key implementation mechanism under the NAP. The action plan is expected to prioritize adaptation measures, define responsible agencies, and support the development of indicators for monitoring progress which aligned with the GGA indicators. This reflects Thailand’s iterative approach to adaptation planning, ensuring continuous alignment between long-term strategies and short-term implementation.

In addition, 3rd Level plans are aligned and integrated with the objectives and strategic directions of the NAP. These plans support the integration of climate change adaptation into sectoral and sub-national planning and implementation processes across key development areas, including water resources management, agriculture and food security, public health, natural resource management, tourism, human settlements, and disaster risk reduction. Together, they facilitate a coordinated and whole-of-government approach to enhancing resilience, reducing vulnerability, and strengthening adaptive capacity in response to climate change. Further information on these plans and their contribution to adaptation implementation is provided in the **Progress on Implementation of Adaptation section**.

Thailand has established a multi-level institutional arrangement to support adaptation. The DCCE serves as the national focal point for coordinating adaptation efforts, including policy formulation, inter-agency coordination, and reporting. Implementation is carried out in collaboration with sectoral ministries, provincial and local authorities, academic institutions, and development partners **(Detailed in Institutional Arrangements and Legal Framework on Adaptation Section)**. Adaptation planning in Thailand is informed by scientific knowledge, including climate projections, risk and vulnerability assessments, and geospatial data. These inputs support evidence-based decision-making and the prioritization of adaptation measures. Thailand’s adaptation governance is further supported by a range of existing laws and regulations that contribute to disaster risk management, social protection, and inclusive development. Key legal instruments include **the Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Act (2007),** provides the principal legal basis for disaster risk management in Thailand, supporting preparedness, prevention, response, recovery, and resilience-building efforts at national and sub-national levels. The framework promotes the integration of climate-related risks into disaster risk reduction planning and implementation, strengthens early warning systems, enhances risk assessment and monitoring processes, and supports coordination among relevant agencies. These efforts contribute to reducing vulnerability, increasing resilience to climate-related hazards, and addressing climate-related loss and damage, consistent with the objectives of both the NAP and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030; and **the Regulations of the Ministry of Finance on Emergency Relief Advances for Disaster Victims (2019),** which enable financial support for emergency response and recovery. Thailand also promotes inclusive and equitable adaptation by integrating gender perspectives, human rights considerations, and the needs of vulnerable groups into adaptation policies and measures. Relevant legal frameworks, such as **the Gender Equality Act (2015), the Child Protection Act (2003), the Older Persons Act (2003),** and **the Persons with Disabilities Empowerment Act (2007),** which collectively promote equal participation, protect vulnerable groups, and enhance access to services and assistance.

In parallel, Thailand is developing new climate- and environment-related legislation to strengthen long-term resilience and climate governance. **The Draft Climate Change Act** is intended to establish a comprehensive legal framework to drive climate action, strengthen institutional coordination, and support both mitigation and adaptation efforts. **The Draft Biodiversity Act** aims to conserve and sustainably use biodiversity while strengthening ecosystem resilience and supporting sustainable development through ecosystem-based approaches.

The development of adaptation-related frameworks and plans has been supported by stakeholder consultation processes, including multi-agency workshops and engagement with relevant stakeholders to capture needs, priorities, and practical considerations. These inputs have informed the design of adaptation strategies and measures, ensuring their relevance across sectors and levels of governance.

Thailand has identified priority sectors for adaptation in line with national climate risks, including agriculture and food security, water resources management, natural resource management, public health, tourism, and human settlements. Adaptation actions in these sectors are designed to address both current and projected climate risks while contributing to long-term resilience. Many of these actions also generate co-benefits, such as improved resource efficiency, reduced disaster risks, and enhanced socio-economic resilience.

Adaptation is increasingly integrated into national development planning and public investment processes. Adaptation actions are supported through domestic budget allocations and complemented by international climate finance, with ongoing efforts to strengthen the integration of adaptation into national budgeting and planning systems. In addition, Thailand utilizes existing government platforms for monitoring and reporting to support data management and enhance transparency in adaptation efforts.

Thailand also promotes nature-based solutions as a key approach to adaptation, including ecosystem restoration, watershed management, and sustainable land use practices. These approaches contribute to enhancing resilience while delivering environmental and socio-economic benefits.

Stakeholder engagement is an integral component of Thailand’s adaptation approach. Efforts are made to involve a wide range of stakeholders, including national and subnational government agencies, the private sector, local communities, and civil society organizations. These participatory processes support the development and implementation of adaptation actions that are responsive to local contexts and needs.

## 1.5 Progress on Implementation of Adaptation 

Thailand has made significant progress in implementing adaptation actions as outlined in **Section of Adaptation strategies, Policies, Plans, Goals and Actions to Integrate Adaptation into National Policies and Strategies**, reflecting a transition from strategic planning to operationalization. This progress demonstrates a transition from strategic planning to concrete implementation. Adaptation efforts encompass ongoing, newly initiated, and completed measures across multiple sectors and governance levels, aligning with the GGA and focusing on strengthening resilience, reducing vulnerability, and enhancing long-term adaptive capacity.

Since the submission of its first BTR, Thailand has continued to advance adaptation efforts under the National Adaptation Plan Process (NAP Process), led by the DCCE. This process covers the full cycle of adaptation, including risk, vulnerability, and impact assessments; the formulation of policies, strategies, and plans; as well as implementation, monitoring, evaluation, and learning **(see Table 1-5).**

At the sectoral level, Thailand has implemented adaptation measures across key priority areas. Many of these measures have been integrated into sectoral plans and programs, contributing both to the reduction of current climate risks and to the strengthening of long-term adaptive capacity.

Institutionally, Thailand has strengthened governance mechanisms for adaptation. DCCE serves as the central coordinating agency, while inter-agency collaboration has been enhanced through formal mechanisms such as Memoranda of Understanding (MOUs). These efforts aim to strengthen sectoral implementation, enhance preparedness for climate change impacts, and support the translation of adaptation planning into effective action at the local level.

In addition, Thailand has made progress in enhancing data systems and knowledge-based decision-making. Risk and vulnerability assessments have been conducted at provincial and sectoral levels, supported by climate projections, geospatial data, and risk mapping. These efforts have strengthened the evidence base for adaptation planning and implementation, alongside increased stakeholder engagement to ensure that adaptation measures are responsive to local contexts and needs.

From a policy and legal perspective, as of 2024, the Draft Climate Change Act remains under consideration and public consultation processes involving government agencies, the private sector, civil society organizations, and relevant stakeholders to further refine and strengthen its provisions in accordance with Thailand’s national context. The proposed legislation is expected to become a key mechanism for enhancing the country’s climate change adaptation efforts in a more systematic and coordinated manner by strengthening data integration, improving inter-agency coordination, and supporting long-term national resilience. In addition, the Government of Thailand has emphasized the importance of expediting the enactment of the Climate Change Act to support the country’s transition toward becoming a regional low-carbon economy leader while enhancing the capacity of communities and society to manage long-term climate-related risks and disasters effectively.

With regard to international commitments, Thailand has made progress in implementing adaptation actions outlined in its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) 3.0 and previously reported under BTR1. Efforts to integrate adaptation into national planning processes, strengthen institutional mechanisms, and enhance data systems have contributed to addressing identified adaptation needs and priorities.

Overall, Thailand’s progress reflects a continuous, systematic, and iterative approach to adaptation, with advancements in planning, implementation, and institutional strengthening. ## 1.6 Monitoring and Evaluation of Adaptation Actions and Processes 

Thailand has established its national framework for climate change adaptation with the NAP serving as the overarching policy instrument (DCCE, 2023\). The NAP provides a monitoring and evaluation framework structured around three key dimensions which provide a comprehensive results chain \- from planning and implementation to measurable resilience outcomes, thereby supporting evidence-based decision-making and continuous policy improvement. The key dimensions include: (1) Adaptation Planning, (2) Adaptation Implementation, and (3) Adaptation Outcomes

1) **Adaptation Planning:** assesses the extent to which climate adaptation has been mainstreamed into national and sectoral development plans. Monitoring under this dimension focuses on the quantity and coverage of long-term and sectoral plans integrating adaptation considerations, thereby reflecting the institutionalization of adaptation within policy and planning systems.  
2) **Adaptation Implementation:** examines the extent to which adaptation actions and mechanisms are effectively deployed to enhance climate resilience. This dimension ensures that adaptation efforts are not only implemented, but also produce meaningful and sustainable results. This includes both short-term monitoring and long-term evaluation aspects.   
   In the short term, the framework tracks:  
* Progress of adaptation projects as planned  
* Achievement of outputs and targets  
* Establishment of mechanisms to enhance resilience  
  In the long term, the evaluation considers:  
* Effectiveness and efficiency of adaptation projects  
* Sustainability of project outcomes  
* Cross-sectoral impacts  
* Potential mitigation co-benefits  
3) **Adaptation Outcomes:** focuses on the ultimate objective of adaptation policy: strengthening the resilience of sectors and the broader socio-economic system to climate change.  
   The M\&E approach assesses whether:  
* Vulnerability to climate hazards (in the short term) and climate change (in the long term) is reduced  
* Adaptive capacity is increased  
* Overall climate resilience is strengthened

Furthermore, the third dimension, Adaptation Outcomes, focuses on whether adaptation efforts ultimately lead to reduced vulnerability, strengthened adaptive capacity, and enhanced resilience of sectors and socio-economic systems. It is within this outcome dimension that sectoral resilience is assessed through a structured transition from monitoring to evaluation.

At the national level, Thailand applies the NAP framework for monitoring and evaluation the outcomes.  The process begins with **Monitoring**, which involves systematic data collection based on three categories of indicators:

1) **Exposure indicators**: measuring the degree to which sectors are exposed to climate hazards  
2) **Sensitivity indicators**: reflecting the magnitude of impacts and risks faced by sectors  
3) **Coping/Adaptive Capacity indicators:** assessing the ability of sectors to respond to and recover from climate risks

These three components correspond conceptually to the determinants of vulnerability and resilience embedded within the Adaptation Outcomes framework.

The collected data are analyzed to generate a Yearly Vulnerability Index, providing an annual snapshot of sectoral vulnerability levels. This represents the first analytical output of the monitoring process.

The framework then moves to **Evaluation**, where the analysis of indicators is reviewed and interpreted over time. By examining the trend of the Vulnerability Index, policymakers can determine whether vulnerability is increasing or decreasing. These trend assessments are subsequently synthesized into a Climate Resilience Index, which reflects the overall resilience status of each sector.

**At the national level,** Thailand has made continuous progress in advancing the M\&E of climate change adaptation in line with the established conceptual framework. As previously reported in the BTR1, Thailand had been developing a manual for monitoring and evaluating climate change adaptation. This effort has now been completed with the publication of the **“*Climate Change Adaptation Monitoring and Evaluation Guidebook”***, which serves to operationalize the conceptual framework into practical implementation guidance.

In the preparation of the Guidebook, sectoral goals identified under the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) were used as the primary reference framework. The Guidebook is publicly accessible at: [https://www.dcce.go.th/media/6597/](https://www.dcce.go.th/media/6597/). Indicators for monitoring were developed based on the three core dimensions of the M\&E framework: (1) Exposure, (2) Sensitivity, and (3) Adaptive Capacity, which are consistent with the conceptual framework of vulnerability under the NAP. However, these indicators were designed at an aggregated sectoral level, with the objective of reflecting the overall vulnerability status and trends of each sector. They were not developed to monitor progress at the level of individual actions or specific measures within each sector. Resilience Indicators were established to assess the outcomes of adaptation efforts in each sector. These indicators aim to reflect the extent to which vulnerability is reduced and resilience to climate change is strengthened within each sector. Nevertheless, despite the establishment of such indicators, detailed methodological approaches, processes, and criteria for systematically synthesizing, analyzing, and evaluating sectoral outcomes have not yet been clearly defined. 

In addition, no specific indicators have been developed to monitor and evaluate cross-cutting issues, such as research, technology, support mechanisms, awareness, stakeholder participation, and capacity-building, which may significantly influence the overall effectiveness and sustainability of adaptation efforts.

Moreover, the DCCE is developing **a** **monitoring and evaluation platform for climate change adaptation** to collect and systematically store adaptation-related M\&E data. The platform is designed to facilitate effective linkage and integration of sectoral adaptation information across relevant agencies. It is also expected to strengthen coherence between national and international reporting, by aligning adaptation monitoring with global frameworks such as the Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA) and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), while ensuring consistency with Thailand’s national development strategies and policy frameworks.

As part of this initiative, an additional set of outcome indicators has been developed. However, the process for selecting these indicators and the detailed specifications of the indicators themselves have not yet been clearly defined.

**At sub-national level,** Thailand is under developing **Thailand’s Action Plan on Adaptation (2025–2030)** to operationalize Thailand’s NAP by translating its strategic directions into concrete actions. It sets out sectoral action plans for the period 2025–2030. The sectoral goals under the NAP (Sectoral Goals) were used as the primary reference framework, and sector-specific indicators were developed accordingly. These sectoral indicators are structured into two main categories: (1) monitoring indicators and (2) evaluation indicators.

•	Monitoring Indicators: Indicators derived from sectoral agencies, used to monitor the implementation of projects and activities. These indicators primarily focus on outputs generated by the respective projects or interventions.

•	Evaluation Indicators: Outcome-oriented indicators used to assess the effectiveness and results of interventions in contributing to national and international climate change strategies and commitments.

However, Thailand is still in the process of developing clearly defined strategic-level indicators, while sub-national level indicators remain under development. This has resulted in significant gaps in linking data from project-level implementation to policy-level decision-making. In addition, Thailand faces constraints in assessing, at both national and project levels, whether implemented measures effectively reduce climate risks or enhance resilience. There is also a lack of systematic methodologies and analytical tools to assess reductions in risk and vulnerability.

These limitations constrain the country’s ability to synthesize adaptation outcomes at the national level, support evidence-based policymaking, and report in alignment with the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF) and the Global Goal on Adaptation (GGA). At present, reporting primarily reflects progress in terms of activities undertaken, and is not yet able to adequately address strategic questions regarding the extent to which resilience has been strengthened.

To address these gaps, Thailand requires support in several areas, including: (i) technical assistance to develop outcome-based indicator frameworks aligned with international standards; (ii) methodological support for assessing reductions in risk, vulnerability, and improvements in adaptive capacity; and (iii) capacity-building for relevant agencies to implement outcome-oriented monitoring and evaluation (M\&E). Such support is essential to enhance the ability of the reporting system to reflect adaptation outcomes and impacts in a systematic and robust manner.

#### *Pre-disaster (Averting / Minimizing)*

Thailand has continuously strengthened its preventive and risk reduction measures for disaster risk management, particularly through the enhancement of the Early Warning System (EWS) to be more systematic, integrated, and aligned with international frameworks compared to the previous reporting in BTR1.

In terms of technological advancement, Thailand is currently developing and piloting the use of **Cell Broadcast Service (CBS)** for disaster warning via mobile phones. CBS represents a key mechanism to enhance risk communication by enabling rapid, accurate, and wide-reaching dissemination of alerts. The system allows warning messages to be delivered directly to mobile devices within designated risk areas without prior registration and supports location-specific targeting at a granular level.

As of the end of 2024, Thailand is in the process of testing CBS for eight hazard types, including earthquakes, tsunamis, storms, floods, landslides, cold waves, PM2.5 air pollution, and public safety incidents. Moving forward, there are plans to expand CBS coverage to a broader range of hazards, including those that are complex or context-specific, such as infectious disease outbreaks, fire and hazardous material incidents, terrorism, transport-related risks, cyber threats, and other emerging risks. In parallel, efforts are underway to enhance warning content to provide clear and actionable messages tailored to specific contexts and target groups. Full operational implementation is expected by 2026, with the objective of enabling timely public response and self-protection.

The CBS system is designed to operate in conjunction with the **national disaster warning system (T-Alert)**, which serves as the central hub for aggregating, analyzing, and validating hazard information from relevant agencies. T-Alert subsequently disseminates warnings through multiple channels, including CBS, ensuring an integrated and coherent communication system.

To support these developments, Thailand has established **Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)** and adopted the **Common Alerting Protocol (CAP)** to standardize operational processes, roles and responsibilities, and data formats across agencies. These measures enhance interoperability between systems, reduce redundancy, and improve the reliability and consistency of warning information.

Building on these efforts, Thailand has further strengthened its impact-based early warning capabilities through the development of the Strategic Risk Intelligence (SRI) Alert Platform, supported by the Thailand Science Research and Innovation (TSRI). The platform integrates hazard monitoring, risk assessment, and real-time warning dissemination to support decision-making and emergency response. It incorporates multiple hazard-specific applications, including flood forecasting and warning systems, earthquake early warning technologies, urban risk mapping, and environmental health surveillance. By combining scientific data, geospatial information, and citizen-generated observations, the platform enhances the accuracy and timeliness of warnings and supports targeted responses to climate-related hazards.

The SRI Alert Platform also promotes people-centred early warning through multi-channel communication and location-based alert services. Warning messages can be disseminated through digital platforms, including LINE Official Account and other communication channels, enabling rapid delivery of risk information to populations in affected areas. The platform follows the Common Alerting Protocol (CAP), ensuring interoperability across agencies and supporting coordinated emergency response. These efforts contribute to reducing disaster losses and strengthening resilience to climate-related risks.

In parallel, Thailand continues to adopt a multi-channel dissemination approach by integrating traditional and digital communication systems **as mentioned in [BTR1](https://unfccc.int/documents/645098).** These include warning towers, satellite-based receivers (EVAC), signal relay stations (CSC), as well as mobile applications and social media platforms. This approach ensures that warnings reach all segments of the population, including vulnerable groups, in a timely and inclusive manner.

At the international level, Thailand’s efforts are aligned with the **“Early Warning for All”** initiative under the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) and United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR), which aims to ensure universal access to early warning systems by 2027\. Thailand has actively engaged in regional cooperation on meteorological observation systems, data sharing, weather forecasting, flash flood warning, climate prediction, and research. In addition, collaboration with both public and private sector stakeholders has been strengthened to support operational improvements in early warning systems. These efforts contribute to enhanced climate adaptation, reduced disaster losses, and minimized impacts of climate change, in line with Thailand’s 20-Year National Strategy and the 13th National Economic and Social Development Plan.

In terms of risk assessment, Thailand has strengthened the integration of multi-source data, including meteorological, hydrological, and geospatial information, to support the development of risk maps and spatial analysis. This enhances the accuracy of early warning systems and informs risk-informed planning and decision-making **(see Impacts, risks and vulnerabilities section).**

For infrastructure, Thailand has invested in and upgraded both structural and non-structural measures to reduce disaster risks. These include flood protection systems, dams, drainage infrastructure, and the promotion of nature-based solutions to enhance long-term resilience.

Regarding land use planning, climate risk information has been increasingly integrated into urban planning and land use management to prevent the creation of new risks and avoid development in hazard-prone areas. These efforts are aligned with measures **outlined in the [NAP](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/NAP_THAILAND_2024.pdf).**

Furthermore, Thailand places strong emphasis on **Community-Based Disaster Risk Management (CBDRM)** by promoting community participation in risk assessment, contingency planning, and emergency drills. This approach strengthens local capacity and enhances preparedness and response capabilities at the community level. As of 2024, a total of 15,851 communities/villages nationwide have implemented disaster risk management approaches based on the CBDRM concept, reflecting significant progress in strengthening grassroots resilience and localized risk governance.

#### *During Disaster (Addressing Loss and Damage)*

During disaster events, Thailand implements systematic response measures to protect lives, minimize damages, and provide timely assistance to affected populations. Relevant authorities declare and escalate disaster levels to the severity of the situation, and establish operational structures aligned with the scale of the event, ranging from local and provincial to national levels **(see Institutional arrangements and financial mechanism to facilitate the implementation of the activities Section).** In cases of severe events, forward command posts may be established to enhance operational efficiency and on-site command and control.

Response operations are conducted under the **Incident Command System (ICS)** framework, which is applied as a standardized mechanism for command, control, and coordination among relevant agencies. This approach ensures unity of command, reduces duplication, and enhances the effectiveness of decision-making during emergency situations.

**Emergency Operations Centers (EOCs)** are activated at all administrative levels to serve as central hubs for situation monitoring, data analysis, decision support, and inter-agency coordination. These centers integrate real-time information from early warning systems, meteorological data, and field reports into disaster management processes.

Evacuation of affected populations is carried out based on early warning information and risk assessments, with the establishment of temporary shelters in safe areas. Special attention is given to vulnerable groups, including the elderly, children, and persons with disabilities, to ensure equitable access to protection and essential services. In some areas, registries of vulnerable populations and targeted assistance protocols have been developed to support more effective and inclusive evacuation and response measures.

Disaster response operations encompass a wide range of functions, including search and rescue, medical services, transportation and logistics, restoration of critical infrastructure, and risk communication. These functions are coordinated through the **Emergency Support Function (ESF)** system, which consists of 18 functional areas. The ESF structure facilitates coordinated action across sectors such as infrastructure, transport, public health, social services, and security.

In addition, a **Joint Information Center (JIC)** is established to ensure consistent, accurate, and timely public communication. Information dissemination is conducted through multiple official channels to reduce misinformation and public panic.

Humanitarian assistance (relief) is continuously provided to affected populations, including food, drinking water, medical supplies, temporary shelter, and essential services. Communication is maintained through official channels to ensure that the public receives reliable and up-to-date information.

A multi-channel communication approach is utilized, including mobile-based alert systems such as **Cell Broadcast Service (CBS)**, mobile applications, broadcast media, and social media platforms, to ensure broad and inclusive outreach.

In cases where national capacity is insufficient, Thailand may request international assistance through regional and global mechanisms, such as the **AHA Centre**, under the **ASEAN Agreement on Disaster Management and Emergency Response (AADMER)**, to ensure timely and effective response in line with international standards.

Thailand also aligns its response operations with international guidelines and standards, including those of the **International Search and Rescue Advisory Group (INSARAG)**, to strengthen its urban search and rescue (USAR) capabilities. The country has established a national **Urban Search and Rescue (USAR Thailand)** team under the Department of Disaster Prevention and Mitigation (DDPM). This team is undergoing the **INSARAG External Classification (IEC)** process, with certification expected in 2025, to ensure compliance with internationally recognized operational standards and to enhance effectiveness in responding to earthquakes and structural collapse incidents.

#### *Post-disaster (Recovery and Addressing Residual Loss and Damage)*

In the post-disaster phase, Thailand places strong emphasis on recovery while simultaneously addressing long-term risk reduction and managing both economic and non-economic loss and damage. Recovery efforts aim to restore public services, livelihoods, and living conditions to pre-disaster levels or improve them in line with the “Build Back Better” approach, thereby enhancing resilience to future risks.

Recovery is implemented through three interconnected phases:

* **Short-term recovery** focuses on immediate post-emergency actions, including initial damage assessments, provision of temporary shelter, and restoration of essential public services.   
* **Medium-term recovery** addresses the rehabilitation of economic, social, environmental, and physical and mental health conditions, enabling affected populations to resume their livelihoods and daily activities.   
* **Long-term recovery** emphasizes the reconstruction of infrastructure and systems with enhanced resilience, aiming to reduce structural vulnerabilities and future disaster risks. 

Thailand applies a systematic post-disaster assessment process, beginning with an **Initial Assessment** within the first 72 hours, followed by a **Rapid Assessment**, and subsequently an **In-depth Assessment**. These processes utilize internationally recognized methodologies, including Damage and Needs Assessment (DANA), Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA), and Damage and Loss Assessment (DALA), to analyze impacts at both sectoral and macroeconomic levels.

The results of these assessments are integrated into a **Recovery Framework**, which guides coordinated planning and resource allocation across social, economic, infrastructure, and environmental dimensions. Assessment findings are also systematically used to inform policy revisions, strengthen disaster risk reduction measures, and support risk-informed recovery planning.

In terms of financial support and compensation, the government provides assistance to affected populations through multi-level mechanisms to facilitate livelihood recovery and mitigate short-term economic impacts. Approval and disbursement processes are carried out through local-level committees in accordance with established regulations. **These mechanisms are linked with national budgetary instruments, including the government emergency advance fund used for disaster relief and emergency assistance and central budget allocations, enabling timely and flexible financial responses during emergencies.**

Regarding **non-economic loss and damage (NELD)**, Thailand recognizes impacts on physical and mental health, quality of life, cultural heritage, and social cohesion. Relevant agencies, such as the Ministry of Public Health and the Ministry of Social Development and Human Security, provide mental health services, psychosocial support, community rehabilitation, and protection for vulnerable groups. Efforts are also made to strengthen community networks and social capital to support long-term recovery and resilience.

For **slow-onset events**, such as sea-level rise, coastal erosion, and long-term climate variability, Thailand implements continuous adaptation measures. These include ecosystem restoration, coastal protection infrastructure, livelihood diversification, and long-term land use planning. Such measures are increasingly integrated into spatial development plans and national strategies to address residual risks and enhance systemic resilience across sectors in a sustainable manner.

### 1.7.3  Institutional arrangements and financial mechanism to facilitate the implementation of the activities  

#### *Institutional Framework for Disaster Risk Management*

Thailand has established a comprehensive institutional framework for disaster risk management (DRM) and for reducing loss and damage associated with climate change. This framework encompasses legal instruments, national plans, command and coordination mechanisms, operational entities, financial instruments, and linkages with regional and global mechanisms. It is structured as a **multi-tiered** and **multi-sectoral** system, aligned with international standards on disaster risk reduction. The key legal, policy, and regulatory frameworks governing disaster risk management in Thailand are summarized as follows:

* **Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Act (2007)** serves as the principal legal framework for disaster risk management in Thailand. It establishes a comprehensive and systematic approach covering prevention, preparedness, emergency response, and post-disaster recovery. The Act mandates the development of disaster prevention and mitigation plans at the national, provincial, and Bangkok Metropolitan levels, which serve as operational frameworks for disaster preparedness and response. It also defines disaster severity levels and corresponding management mechanisms, ranging from normal conditions to major emergencies.  
  In terms of emergency response, the Act provides legal authority for the declaration of disaster zones and enables the rapid mobilization of personnel, equipment, and resources from relevant agencies to support affected populations. It also provides for the establishment of incident command structures to ensure coordinated and effective operations. Additionally, the Act includes provisions for relief and compensation measures, such as temporary shelter, essential supplies, and financial assistance based on established criteria.  
  In the recovery phase, the Act emphasizes the restoration of infrastructure, economic systems, and livelihoods, while promoting long-term disaster risk reduction. It supports the integration of lessons learned into policy and planning improvements and establishes budgetary mechanisms to ensure continuous and adequate support in both normal and emergency situations.  
  **National Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Plan (2021–2027)** outlines Thailand’s strategic direction for proactive and integrated disaster risk management. The plan marks a transition from reactive disaster response to a risk reduction-oriented approach in line with international standards. It aims to strengthen the capacity of all sectors in preparedness, response, and resilient recovery, while enhancing overall national resilience.   
* **The Regulation of the Ministry of Finance on Emergency Relief Advances for Disaster Victims (2019)** focuses on improving the efficiency and flexibility of budget management for disaster relief. The regulation expands the authority and financial ceilings for government agencies to access the government emergency advance fund used for disaster relief and emergency assistance, aligning them with current risk contexts. It also clarifies rules and procedures for fund disbursement, streamlines administrative processes, and establishes standardized criteria for providing timely and equitable assistance to affected populations. 

The institutional structure for disaster risk management in Thailand is outlined as follows.

At the policy level, the **National Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Committee (NDPMC)** serves as the highest-level decision-making body responsible for setting national directions, strategies, and operational plans, as well as overseeing and integrating efforts across government agencies, local authorities, and relevant stakeholders. The Committee is chaired by the Prime Minister or a designated Deputy Prime Minister. In addition, the **National Accident Prevention Committee** is responsible for proposing policies, measures, and guidelines on accident prevention to the Cabinet, as well as providing recommendations and coordinating actions among relevant government agencies. The **National Disaster Early Warning System Committee** is tasked with formulating policies, measures, and plans for the national early warning system, including the development of operational frameworks, programs, and projects to guide implementation by relevant agencies.

At the operational level, Thailand adopts the **Emergency Operations Center (EOC)** approach. The **National Disaster Management Command Headquarters**, chaired by the Minister of Interior, with the Permanent Secretary of the Ministry of Interior serving as Deputy Commander, is responsible for directing, commanding, and coordinating national-level disaster response operations. The **Central Disaster Management Operations Command**, led by the Director-General of the DDPM, functions as the central coordinating body for information integration, monitoring, and early warning. At the subnational level, **Disaster Management Operations Commands** are established at provincial, Bangkok, district, and local levels to support decentralized implementation **(Figure 1-23).** Disaster risk management is operationalized through structured mechanisms at each administrative level, supported by a four-tier disaster classification system. #### *Institutional Coordination Mechanism for Disaster Risk Management*

Thailand has established a coordination mechanism for disaster risk management under **the  Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Act (2007)** and **The National Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Plan (2021 \-2027),** which plays a critical role in supporting climate change adaptation efforts. This mechanism is designed as an integrated system that links operations from the policy level to the operational level, and aligning with global adaptation objectives, including the GGA.

The mechanism is grounded in the principle of **unity of command**, whereby designated commanders and directors are appointed at each administrative level—national, provincial, district, and local. These authorities are legally empowered to direct, coordinate, and integrate the operations of relevant agencies during disaster situations. This structure facilitates rapid response, reduces duplication, and enhances overall operational efficiency.

Structurally, Thailand’s coordination mechanism adopts a **multi-agency coordination** **approach,** involving all relevant public sector entities, including those responsible for security, public health, natural resources, and local governance. These entities collectively contribute to preparedness, response, and recovery efforts. Such an integrated approach is particularly critical for addressing climate-related risks, which are inherently cross-sectoral in nature.

To further strengthen operational coordination, Thailand has established the **Emergency Support Function (ESF)** system. This system categorizes disaster management functions into standardized operational areas, enabling relevant agencies with similar mandates to coordinate their efforts effectively under a unified structure. The ESF system supports the coordination role of the central Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Command and facilitates command, control, and coordination at the provincial and Bangkok incident command centers under the National Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Command, particularly in large-scale (Level 3\) and very severe (Level 4\) disasters.

By organizing disaster management tasks into functional areas, the ESF system enhances the effectiveness of planning, coordination, resource mobilization, communication, and emergency response management. It ensures that operational arrangements are tailored to the specific characteristics and scale of each disaster event. The system is structured into 18 functional areas, covering key aspects of disaster management operations.

In operational practice, Thailand establishes **Emergency Operations Centers (EOCs)** at all administrative levels during disaster events. These centers function as the central hubs for situation monitoring, risk analysis, command and control, and inter-agency resource coordination. The EOCs play a critical role in integrating meteorological, hydrological, and disaster risk information into decision-making processes and early warning dissemination.

In addition, the mechanism supports vertical coordination between national and subnational levels by ensuring that provincial and local plans and operations are aligned with national policy frameworks. At the same time, it enables horizontal coordination through the mobilization of resources and mutual assistance across sectors and jurisdictions, particularly in the case of large-scale disasters.

Furthermore, the coordination system emphasizes the engagement of the private sector and civil society, which play important roles in supporting resources, technology, early warning dissemination, and community-level response. This inclusive approach contributes to strengthening adaptive capacity and resilience at the local level.

#### *Financial Mechanism for Disaster Risk Management*

Thailand utilizes the **“the government emergency advance fund used for disaster relief and emergency assistance”** as the primary financial mechanism for disaster risk management under “The Regulations of the Ministry of Finance on Emergency Relief Advances for Disaster Victims (2019)” **as mentioned in [BTR1](https://unfccc.int/documents/645098)**. This mechanism is designed to ensure that government agencies have immediate access to budgetary resources to provide timely assistance to disaster-affected populations in emergency situations, without being constrained by standard budget disbursement procedures. It primarily focuses on providing immediate relief and initial recovery support, rather than fully compensating for all losses and damages incurred.

Under this framework, the government emergency advance fund used for disaster relief and emergency assistance is allocated across multiple administrative levels to ensure flexibility and alignment with institutional mandates. **At** **the central level,** key agencies, including the Office of the Prime Minister, are allocated substantial funding ceilings to support nationwide emergency operations. **At** **the ministerial level**, budget allocations are provided according to sector-specific responsibilities, such as agriculture, social development, and public health, to address disaster impacts within their respective sectors. **At the subnational level**, Provincial Disaster Prevention and Mitigation Offices are allocated contingency funds, enabling Provincial Governors to authorize and implement response measures promptly in accordance with local conditions.

In terms of governance, the government emergency advance fund used for disaster relief and emergency assistance mechanism operates through a multi-tiered committee system that balances decentralization with oversight. **At the district level**, committees are responsible for damage assessment, verification, and screening of assistance requests. **At the provincial level**, the Provincial Disaster Relief Committee, chaired by the Governor, is responsible for declaring eligible disaster areas and approving expenditures within the authorized limits. In urgent situations, Governors or District Chiefs are empowered to approve emergency expenditures in advance, without convening committee meetings, to ensure a rapid response.

Furthermore, the financial mechanism has evolved to increasingly support pre-disaster interventions. The government emergency advance fund used for disaster relief and emergency assistance may be utilized for disaster prevention or mitigation measures in advance of an anticipated event, within specified financial limits and without the need for formal disaster declaration, provided that a potential disaster is expected in the near term. Nevertheless, expenditures remain subject to strict conditions, limited to essential livelihood support and temporary rehabilitation to restore pre-disaster conditions. The use of funds for permanent infrastructure development or new construction is not permitted under this mechanism.

To ensure financial accountability and transparency, a structured monitoring and oversight system is in place. A central-level committee is mandated to review complaints and ensure fairness in the provision of assistance. In addition, implementing agencies are required to report on fund utilization within a specified timeframe and are subject to internal audit and financial control procedures in accordance with public financial management regulations.

However, Thailand is currently in the process of strengthening its disaster-related financial mechanisms, with planned regulatory revisions in 2025 aimed at enhancing efficiency and responsiveness in light of increasingly complex and severe risks. Key improvements include the expansion of funding ceilings for implementing agencies, the designation of Provincial Finance Offices as coordination hubs for financial management at the subnational level, and the strengthening of monitoring and grievance redress mechanisms to improve transparency and accountability.

## 1.8 Cooperation, Good practices, Experience and Lessons Learned 

Thailand recognizes that adaptation to climate change is a long-term and iterative process that requires robust scientific knowledge, effective policy integration, strong institutional cooperation, and continuous learning. Over the years, Thailand has strengthened its adaptive capacity through the development of climate information systems, the integration of adaptation considerations into national and sectoral planning processes, the promotion of multi-stakeholder collaboration, and the establishment of mechanisms for monitoring, evaluation, and learning (MEL).

Experiences gained from adaptation planning and implementation have generated valuable knowledge, good practices, and lessons learned that contribute to enhancing the effectiveness of adaptation actions and strengthening climate resilience at national and local levels. Key experiences and lessons are presented in the following sections.

### 1.8.1 Advancing Climate Science and Knowledge for Adaptation 

The development and application of climate science and knowledge constitute a fundamental pillar of Thailand’s adaptation efforts. In recent years, Thailand has invested in strengthening climate information systems, climate projections, vulnerability and risk assessments, and climate-related research to support evidence-based adaptation planning and decision-making.

A notable example is the establishment of **the Climate Change Information Center (CCIC)**, which serves as a national platform for climate-related information. The platform integrates climate data, climate projections, vulnerability assessments, and climate risk information to support policymakers, practitioners, researchers, and the general public. In addition, the DCCE has developed **Climate Data Services** that provide access to climate projections and climate-related datasets for use in adaptation planning across sectors.

**Good Practices**

* Developing integrated climate information systems that combine climate, vulnerability, and risk data.  
* Improving accessibility to climate information through publicly available platforms and services.  
* Promoting the use of scientific evidence to inform adaptation planning and policy development.

**Experiences and Lessons Learned**

* Producing climate information alone is insufficient; efforts must also focus on enhancing users’ capacity to interpret and apply the information effectively.  
* Collaboration between climate data providers and end-users is essential to ensure the relevance and usability of climate information.  
* Demand for localized and sector-specific climate information continues to increase, particularly for provincial and local-level planning.

### 1.8.2 Mainstreaming Adaptation into National and Sectoral Planning 

Thailand has progressively integrated climate adaptation considerations into national development planning and sectoral policies. The National Adaptation Plan (NAP) serves as the primary framework for guiding adaptation efforts across key sectors and levels of governance.

Adaptation priorities have been incorporated into national development strategies, sectoral plans, and local planning processes. The NAP identifies six priority sectors, namely water resources management, agriculture and food security, tourism, public health, natural resource management, and human settlements and security, which provide a framework for addressing climate risks and vulnerabilities in a coordinated manner.

**Good Practices**

* Utilizing the NAP as a national framework to guide adaptation planning and implementation.  
* Integrating climate risk considerations into national, sectoral, and local development plans.  
* Prioritizing adaptation actions based on sector-specific vulnerabilities and climate risks.

**Experiences and Lessons Learned**

* Mainstreaming adaptation into existing planning frameworks enhances the sustainability and continuity of adaptation efforts.  
* Effective implementation requires adequate financial resources, institutional support, and monitoring mechanisms.  
* Climate risk information plays a critical role in prioritizing adaptation actions and resource allocation.

### 1.8.3 Strengthening Institutional Coordination and Partnerships 

Adaptation to climate change is inherently cross-sectoral and requires coordinated action among a wide range of stakeholders. Thailand has strengthened institutional arrangements and collaborative mechanisms to facilitate cooperation among government agencies, academic institutions, local authorities, the private sector, civil society organizations, and international partners.

National coordination is supported through existing climate governance mechanisms, while collaboration with international organizations and development partners has contributed to strengthening technical capacities, mobilizing resources, and facilitating knowledge exchange. Such partnerships have played an important role in supporting adaptation planning, implementation, and capacity-building initiatives.

**Good Practices**

* Establishing institutional coordination mechanisms that facilitate collaboration across sectors and levels of governance.  
* Promoting multi-stakeholder engagement in adaptation planning and implementation processes.  
* Leveraging international cooperation to strengthen technical expertise, capacity development, and resource mobilization.

**Experiences and Lessons Learned**

* Adaptation challenges cannot be effectively addressed by a single institution and require coordinated action across sectors.  
* Clearly defined roles and responsibilities help improve efficiency and reduce duplication of efforts.  
* Sustained coordination mechanisms are critical for maintaining momentum and ensuring coherence in adaptation implementation.

### 1.8.4 Promoting Innovation, Pilot Actions and Scaling Up 

Thailand has promoted innovation and pilot initiatives as important approaches for identifying, testing, and scaling up effective adaptation solutions. These efforts include the development of climate services, early warning systems, climate-informed decision-support tools, and innovative approaches for managing climate-related risks in key sectors.

Pilot projects and demonstration activities have provided opportunities to test adaptation measures under different contexts and generate practical lessons that can inform broader implementation. Experiences from these initiatives have contributed to improving adaptation design and facilitating replication and scaling-up.

**Good Practices**

* Using pilot projects and demonstration activities to test adaptation approaches before large-scale implementation.  
* Applying climate information, digital technologies, and decision-support tools to strengthen adaptation planning and risk management.  
* Promoting user-oriented innovation that responds to the needs of affected communities and sectors.

**Experiences and Lessons Learned**

* Successful scaling-up requires long-term institutional commitment and sustainable financing mechanisms.  
* Stakeholder engagement throughout project design and implementation enhances ownership and long-term sustainability.  
* Innovations that are practical, accessible, and responsive to local needs are more likely to be adopted and replicated.

### 1.8.5 Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning for Adaptation  

Thailand recognizes the importance of monitoring, evaluation, and learning (MEL) in enhancing the effectiveness of adaptation actions and informing future decision-making. Efforts are underway to develop and strengthen adaptation MEL frameworks and systems that support the tracking of progress, assessment of outcomes, and continuous learning from implementation experiences.

The development of adaptation MEL systems is expected to support national reporting processes, including reporting under the Enhanced Transparency Framework (ETF), while also facilitating evidence-based policy adjustments and adaptive management.

**Good Practices**

* Developing national frameworks and indicators for monitoring adaptation progress and effectiveness.  
* Promoting evidence-based evaluation of adaptation actions and outcomes.  
* Encouraging knowledge sharing and learning across sectors and stakeholders.

**Experiences and Lessons Learned**

* Measuring adaptation outcomes remains challenging due to the long-term and context-specific nature of adaptation processes.  
* Data availability and consistency continue to be major constraints in adaptation monitoring and evaluation.  
* Continuous learning and feedback mechanisms are essential for improving adaptation policies and practices over time.

### 1.8.6 Key Lessons Learned and Future Priorities  
Thailand’s experience demonstrates that effective adaptation requires an integrated approach that combines scientific knowledge, policy and planning processes, institutional coordination, innovation, and learning mechanisms. Strengthening resilience to climate change depends not only on the implementation of adaptation measures, but also on the systems and enabling conditions that support adaptation planning and decision-making.

Key lessons learned include the importance of strengthening climate information services, mainstreaming adaptation into development planning, enhancing institutional cooperation, promoting innovation and scaling-up successful practices, and establishing robust monitoring, evaluation, and learning systems.

Looking ahead, Thailand will continue to prioritize the development of climate information and knowledge systems, strengthen adaptation planning and implementation under the National Adaptation Plan, enhance adaptation monitoring and evaluation mechanisms, improve access to adaptation finance, and expand partnerships at national and international levels. These efforts will contribute to strengthening resilience and supporting sustainable development in the face of increasing climate risks and uncertainties.
