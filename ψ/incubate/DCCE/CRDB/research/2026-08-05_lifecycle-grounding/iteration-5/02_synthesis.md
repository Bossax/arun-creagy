# Iteration 5 Synthesis — Business NFRs, Functional Specification Structure, Use-Case Prioritization, Data Contracts, and the Procurement Boundary

This iteration's objective was to ground, in requirements-engineering and enterprise-data-architecture literature, five specific mechanics of the CRDB-to-TOR70 handoff package — business NFR content and capture form, functional specification structure for a single use case, use-case prioritization methodology, data contract and assumption log structure, and the technical/procurement content boundary — building directly on iteration 4's finding that Business NFRs are CRDB's own Design-phase output rather than raw material for TOR70. Both notebooks were asked the same five questions; this document compares their answers and draws conclusions for CRDB's package.

## Question 1: What counts as a business NFR, and in what form should CRDB capture it

Both bodies of literature independently draw the same line the iteration-4 correction already established: business NFRs are constraints the business units and end-users impose on the platform, while system NFRs are constraints the infrastructure and engineering teams impose on the hardware and software stack. Both also agree, without prompting, that a business NFR is only useful if it is quantified and testable — a "the data must be fresh" or "the platform must be secure" statement is treated by both as an anti-pattern, not a real requirement.

Where the two diverge is in which business NFR categories they surface and how elaborate the capture mechanism is. The requirements-engineering literature, drawing on an actual data-warehouse requirements-gathering practice, lists data-freshness/refresh-frequency targets, regulatory and compliance thresholds, numeric data-quality divergence rules, access-pattern latency expectations by persona, and data lifecycle/retention rules — categories that map closely onto the concrete examples iteration 4 already flagged (freshness SLAs, compliance thresholds, uptime for critical pipelines). The enterprise-data-architecture literature surfaces a broader and more contemporary set: cost/FinOps ceilings (a "cost-per-insight" target), regulatory auditability requirements tied to specific modern frameworks (the EU AI Act's demand for automated, machine-readable lineage), semantic and metric consistency (a governed semantic layer to prevent different teams computing the same KPI differently — the "spreadsheet of truth" problem), usability/interaction thresholds, and sustainability constraints. The enterprise-data-architecture literature is also the only one of the two to propose a full engineering apparatus for capturing these — an SLI/SLO/SLA translation chain with percentile-based targets, a structured NFR template with fields for ID, category, SLI, SLO, test strategy, pass criteria, trace-source, priority, and owner, plus formal review and change-control gates (a System Requirements Review followed by a Configuration Control Board).

On capture form, both agree a hybrid of narrative text and structured tables is appropriate — narrative for context and strategic rationale, tables for the actual quantified thresholds — but the requirements-engineering literature's version of the table (ingestion parameter checklists, a prioritized requirements matrix with ID and priority rating, role-to-layer access matrices) is lighter-weight than the enterprise-data-architecture literature's SLI/SLO/SLA/NFR-template apparatus, which is closer to what a system-design team would produce, not what a pre-build blueprint team would produce.

Synthesized answer for CRDB: the business NFR categories CRDB should capture are data-freshness/refresh cadence, regulatory and compliance thresholds relevant to DCCE's own regulatory context, numeric data-quality thresholds, access-pattern latency expectations by user persona, and retention/lifecycle rules — with the enterprise-data-architecture literature's semantic-consistency category (a single governed definition per metric or term) added as a business NFR worth naming explicitly, since it maps directly onto CRDB's own glossary work. CRDB should capture these in a lightweight thresholds table (requirement ID, description, quantified target, priority, owner) plus supporting narrative, consistent with the requirements-engineering literature's version of this artifact. The heavier SLI/SLO/SLA/percentile apparatus, the formal Configuration Control Board, and the full nine-characteristic ISO/IEC 25010 system-NFR taxonomy are appropriately TOR70's and system NFRs' territory, not CRDB's — adopting that machinery in the blueprint would over-engineer a coherent-draft deliverable.

## Question 2: Functional Specification structure and detail level for a single prioritized use case

Both notebooks agree on the governing rule: a functional specification should freeze the logical "what" and "why" — outcomes, inputs, constraints, and success criteria — while deliberately leaving physical implementation choices to the downstream build vendor. Both independently name the same discipline for achieving this: every requirement must be quantifiable and testable, subjective adjectives such as "fast," "clean," or "secure" are explicitly banned, and project-management content (cost, schedule, methodology) must stay out of the document entirely.

The requirements-engineering literature is the more directly reusable of the two for CRDB's actual content, because its six-part structure is built around data-pipeline concerns: business context and persona/access-pattern detail, inbound source and pipeline parameters, logical transformation rules, a known-caveats/risk log, verifiable data-quality rules and success metrics, and end-user-validated sample data with a sign-off step before backend work begins. It also gives an explicit exclusion list (physical server specs, index-tuning strategy, storage layout) that is directly usable as a checklist for what CRDB should leave out. The enterprise-data-architecture literature instead supplies formal SRS-writing discipline that the other notebook does not: unique requirement identifiers, an Event-Condition-Action pattern for stating behavior, a four-part external-interface taxonomy (user, hardware, software, communications), a requirement for complete input-response coverage rather than "happy path" only, a rule against unresolved "TBD" placeholders (each one must carry an owner and a deadline if it cannot be avoided), and a requirement for bidirectional traceability back to the business driver and forward to a verification method.

Synthesized answer for CRDB: the Functional Specification for a single prioritized use case should combine both — the data-pipeline content set from the requirements-engineering literature (context/persona, source/pipeline parameters, transformation rules, caveats, quality rules, validated sample data) organized under the formal discipline the enterprise-data-architecture literature specifies (unique IDs per requirement, ECA-style behavior statements, defined external interfaces, and a trace-source back to the business need). Given the two-week timeline and the coherent-draft (not production-ready) quality bar this project operates under, some fields may legitimately remain TBD, but per the enterprise-data-architecture literature's rule, each TBD should be logged with a named owner and a resolution deadline rather than silently left blank — this is a way of reconciling the coherent-draft bar with contract-ready discipline rather than treating them as in tension.

## Question 3: Use-case prioritization methodology

Both notebooks frame prioritization the same way at the top level: narrow a broad candidate list to a small, well-justified first wave using multiple scoring criteria rather than intuition, and treat feasibility/readiness as being as important as business impact when deciding what goes first.

The requirements-engineering literature is the stronger source for the mechanics CRDB most immediately needs: a five-point priority scale (Critical, High, Medium, Low, Future) for rating individual requirements or use cases, and a concrete process for resolving disagreement among stakeholders — one-on-one interviews rather than group workshops so no single voice dominates, forcing ambiguous goals into quantified targets, explicit assumption documentation, and a final gated "last comment" milestone after which the prioritized baseline is frozen and ad-hoc changes are blocked. The enterprise-data-architecture literature is the stronger source for scoping the size of a first wave and for feasibility scoring: it names six "quick win" characteristics for selecting pilot data products (inexpensive, small in scale, impactful, cross-functional, easy to deliver, and aligned to a well-funded sponsor), a three-dimension Data Product Assessment (readiness/maturity, feasibility, cost versus ROI), and an ongoing trust-score mechanism (metadata quality, data quality, availability/freshness) for tracking data products after launch.

One scale mismatch is worth flagging rather than papering over: the enterprise-data-architecture literature's own numeric anchor for a first wave is 10 to 20 critical data assets, a figure drawn from large enterprise data-mesh programs and not directly transferable to DCCE's much smaller candidate set of 8 named products.

Synthesized answer for CRDB: score DCCE's 8 candidate products against the enterprise-data-architecture literature's "quick win" characteristics (impact, feasibility, ease of delivery, sponsor readiness) to shortlist candidates, then apply the requirements-engineering literature's five-point Critical/High/Medium/Low/Future scale to rank use cases and their associated NFRs within the shortlisted set, and use the one-on-one-interview-plus-gated-sign-off process to resolve disagreement among DCCE stakeholders before the "first wave" use case (realistically one or two, not ten to twenty, given DCCE's scale) is frozen for handoff to TOR70.

## Question 4: Data Contract and Assumption Log structure

Both notebooks agree that a Data Contract formalizes the schema and quality agreement between a data producer and a data consumer to prevent uncontrolled schema drift, and that an Assumption Log exists specifically to bound liability by making explicit what the requirements-gathering process assumed rather than verified. Both also agree that if an underlying assumption changes, the requirements it supports must be revisited — the log is a living risk register, not a one-time disclaimer.

The requirements-engineering literature is markedly more complete on this question and supplies a full Data Contract structure directly reusable by CRDB: schema and semantic-mapping definitions (tied to a governed business glossary), data-quality rules and thresholds, operational parameters (ingestion frequency, retention/versioning), access modalities and latency expectations, and a known-caveats section. It also supplies a worked Assumption Log example (an ID, a category, the stated assumption, and the bounded responsibility it protects) drawn from an actual delivered infrastructure project. The enterprise-data-architecture literature is notably thinner on this question and says so explicitly, but it contributes two things the other notebook does not: the idea of an automated schema contract (a machine-enforced check, such as a JSON Schema validation, run at ingestion and in CI/CD to catch breaking changes before production) as the technical realization of a data contract, and a choice of two different Assumption Log formats depending on project governance style — a continuously maintained Agile "assumption wiki-page" versus a formal, point-in-time SRS "Assumptions and Dependencies" section.

Synthesized answer for CRDB: adopt the requirements-engineering literature's fuller Data Contract structure, since its sections (schema/semantic mapping, quality thresholds, operational parameters, access modalities, caveats) reuse content CRDB's data inventory and glossary work already produces. The automated-enforcement mechanism the enterprise-data-architecture literature describes is a TOR70 implementation concern, not something CRDB itself builds, but CRDB should mark in narrative form which contract fields are intended to be machine-enforced later so TOR70 knows what to automate. For the Assumption Log, the formal SRS-style "Assumptions and Dependencies" format fits CRDB's situation better than the Agile wiki-page format: CRDB is a fixed, time-boxed blueprint engagement rather than an ongoing multi-team Agile program, so a dated, point-in-time log with an explicit "if this changes, revisit these requirements" trigger is the right shape, not a continuously-edited collaborative page.

## Question 5: Where the technical/business content boundary ends and procurement content begins

The two notebooks are in near-total agreement here, which itself corroborates the boundary iteration 4 already established. Both define the technical/business side as everything about what the platform must do and how well (business drivers, logical architecture, source and interface inventory, transformation rules, quality rules, security/access constraints, validated sample data, quantified performance criteria) and the procurement side as everything about how the engagement is run and paid for (cost and financial terms, delivery schedule and milestones, resourcing and staffing, reporting procedures, formal acceptance and sign-off procedures). Both give the same reason for keeping them separate: a change to a payment milestone or a delivery date should never force a re-baseline of the technical specification, and vice versa.

The requirements-engineering literature contributes a concrete connective device the other notebook does not: the technical requirements document functions as a "Technical Appendix" or "Exhibit A" that the Statement of Work formally references — the SOW states that the vendor agrees to deliver a system satisfying the prioritized requirements in the exhibit for a fixed price, which anchors the commercial contract to the technical baseline without merging the two documents. The enterprise-data-architecture literature contributes a category the other notebook does not name explicitly: development methodology and tooling choices (Agile/Scrum ceremonies, CI/CD pipeline requirements, Git branching workflows) belong on the procurement/process side, not the technical side — meaning CRDB should not pre-decide how TOR70's team will work, only what it must deliver.

Synthesized answer for CRDB: this iteration reinforces rather than revises the settled technical/procurement boundary. The practical mechanism for connecting CRDB's output to TOR70's contract is for DCCE's procurement instrument to reference CRDB's prioritized requirements document as a contractual exhibit — CRDB does not need to draft that reference itself, but its priority ratings and use-case identifiers need to be stable and unambiguous enough to be cited that way. CRDB should also actively avoid recommending TOR70's build methodology or tooling, since both notebooks agree that is procurement territory, not blueprint territory.
