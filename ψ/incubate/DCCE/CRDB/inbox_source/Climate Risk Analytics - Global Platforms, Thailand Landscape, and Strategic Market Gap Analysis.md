### 1. Executive Summary

The increasing frequency and severity of extreme weather events, driven by global climate change, pose escalating financial and operational risks to businesses, financial institutions, and public organizations globally. Integrating climate risk into corporate strategy has evolved from a voluntary sustainability practice to a mandatory regulatory requirement. This report provides a comprehensive review of leading global and regional climate risk analytics platforms and open-source data catalogs, analyzing their target audiences, asset coverage, geographical and temporal scope, and key output metrics.

Importantly, this report examines the rapidly tightening regulatory landscape in Thailand, including the cabinet-approved Draft Climate Change Act and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) guidelines aligning with the International Sustainability Standards Board (ISSB). Finally, a detailed market gap analysis is presented, outlining strategic entry points and actionable business models for a new company to emerge and serve a niche customer group in Thailand by providing localized hazard, exposure, and vulnerability datasets.

### 3. Thailand's Policy, Legal, and Financial Landscape

Thailand's climate policy is rapidly transitioning from a voluntary, incentive-based approach toward a binding, rules-based system with strict legal enforceability. This structural shift is driven by three main regulatory components:

#### A. Draft Climate Change Act (Cabinet Approved December 2, 2025)

The Act establishes a centralized national governance framework led by the National Climate Change Policy Committee (NCCPC), chaired by the Prime Minister. It legally anchors Thailand's commitments to carbon neutrality by 2050, net-zero emissions by 2065, and an interim target of reducing emissions by 47% by 2035. Key regulations include:

- **Mandatory Emission Reporting:** Annual, independently verified reporting is now mandatory for businesses in energy, transport, heavy industry, agriculture, and waste management. Falsified reporting is a legal offense subject to administrative fines (THB 10,000 to THB 5 million) and criminal sanctions (up to three years of imprisonment), with personal liability extending to company directors.
    
- **Carbon Pricing (Dual-Track):** A pilot Carbon Tax on petroleum products was implemented in early 2025 at THB 200 per tCO2e. This operates alongside the Thailand Emissions Trading System (TH-ETS) cap-and-trade system and a Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) to prevent carbon leakage.
    
- **Climate Change Fund:** Reinvests ETS and carbon fee revenues directly into local climate mitigation and adaptation programs.
    

#### B. SEC Thailand Revised Disclosure Requirements (November 28, 2025)

To align corporate reporting with international standards, the SEC has mandated that listed companies apply ISSB (IFRS S1 and S2) standards within the revised 56-1 One Report-S disclosure templates. In the initial phase, a "climate-first" reporting approach focusing on Scope 1 and Scope 2 GHG emissions is being enforced with a phased timeline:

- **SET50 Index:** Companies classified as constituents of the SET50 in the December 2026 Index Review must apply the disclosures.
    
- **SET100 Index:** Companies classified as constituents of the SET100 in the December 2027 Index Review.
    
- All reported emissions must undergo standardized assurance from providers registered with the Thailand Greenhouse Gas Management Organization (TGO) or international verifiers.
    

#### C. Bank of Thailand (BOT) Directional Paper and Taxonomy

The BOT issued a Policy Statement expecting financial institutions to systematically assess environmental and climate risks across four pillars: governance, strategy, risk management, and disclosure. Financial groups must adopt the "Thailand Taxonomy" Phase 1 (energy and transport) and Phase 2 (expanding to agriculture, construction, real estate, manufacturing, and waste management) to classify their investments into green (sustainable), amber (transitioning), or red (harmful) activities.

### 4. Thailand's Physical Climate Risk Profile & Localized Data Gaps

Thailand is highly vulnerable to the physical impacts of climate change, ranking 9th globally in the Global Climate Risk Index (2000–2019). Without decisive adaptation, the physical impacts could reduce Thailand's GDP by 7–14% by 2050. The primary physical hazard is riverine and coastal flooding, concentrated in the high-economic-exposure regions of Bangkok and the central plains.

- **The Historic Baseline:** The catastrophic 2011 floods affected 65 provinces, damaged roughly 1.5 million homes, caused USD 45–46.5 billion in economic losses, shut down major industrial estates in Ayutthaya and Pathum Thani, and dragged real GDP growth down by 1.1%.
    
- **Future Projections:** Extreme river floods are anticipated to affect over 2 million people by 2035–2044, and coastal flooding, exacerbated by rising sea levels and land subsidence in Bangkok, could affect another 2.4 million people by 2070–2100. Extreme heat is projected to rise by 1.8°C under high-emission scenarios, driving up cooling costs, while severe droughts threaten agricultural regions like the Northeast.
    

#### Core Challenges in Local Data Access

Despite these material risks, conducting physical risk assessments in Thailand is severely hindered by significant data gaps. Key challenges include:

1. **Fragmentation:** Climate, population, and asset data are siloed across multiple ministries.
    
2. **Lack of Standardization:** There are no unified data collection standards, definitions, or baseline data across government agencies.
    
3. **Access Limitations:** Almost half of the disaster risk reduction and climate change policy frameworks draft documents have limited public access.
    

#### Existing Local Portals & Platforms

While several public agencies maintain databases, they are generally designed for near-real-time disaster monitoring rather than long-term forward-looking risk modeling:

- **National Hydroinformatics Data Center (NHC):** Operated by the Hydro-Informatics Institute (HII), it integrates weather and water data from over 50 government agencies into the "ThaiWater" platform. It offers daily monitoring and flood warning APIs but lacks forward-looking climate projections or vulnerability assessment overlays.
    
- **GISTDA Portal:** Provides mapping of historical and 30-day active flooding extents, repeating flood areas, and land-use layers in GIS formats.
    
- **Thai Meteorological Department (TMD):** Operates 104+ stations and provides daily station observations on its Climate Information System (CIS). However, many stations suffer from high percentages of missing data (POM), and downloading historical raw data in standard network common data form (NetCDF) or structured formats can be procedurally challenging for private firms.
    
- **Thai People Map and Analytics Platform (TPMAP):** Integrates Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) data to map social vulnerability (e.g., elderly and child population deprivations), which is useful for social risk assessments but completely disconnected from financial and asset exposure tracking.
    

### 5. Avenues for Localized Empirical Modeling: Vulnerability & Damage Curves

A critical step in climate risk analytics is translating a physical hazard (such as a 100-year flood depth) into a financial loss metric. This requires a vulnerability curve (or stage-damage function), which relates hazard intensity to a fraction of the maximum asset value damaged. Applying generalized or foreign damage curves (e.g., applying US HAZUS or European JRC curves) often results in massive biases—up to an 8-fold error in loss estimation. To address this, localized empirical models must be utilized.

#### A. Building Structural Damage Functions (Lower Chao Phraya Basin)

Komolafe et al. (2018/2019) developed localized empirical stage-damage curves specifically for building structures in the Chao Phraya River basin, analyzing 118 field samples. They established logarithmic damage curves relating building damage ratio ($D_f$) to inundation depth ($x$, in meters):

$$D_f = C_1 \cdot \ln(x) + C$$

Where $C$ and $C_1$ are empirical damage coefficients representing the specific vulnerability of the structure. The models outputted maximum predicted vulnerability index values (where 1.0 represents total structural destruction) as follows:

- **Wooden Buildings:** Max index of 0.36 (highest vulnerability).
    
- **Concrete Frame with Unreinforced Masonry:** Max index of 0.30.
    
- **Reinforced Concrete Moment Frame:** Max index of 0.10 (highest structural resilience).
    
- **Key Covariates:** The studies proved that beyond water depth, building age, floor area, and plinth height are critical variables driving structural damage.
    

#### B. Agricultural Crop Damage Functions (Rice Farming)

Agriculture employs roughly one-third of Thailand's workforce, making crop-specific modeling vital. A 2026 Copernicus NHESS study developed localized deterministic and probabilistic stage-damage functions (SDFs) for rice crop risk assessments in Thailand. The univariable deterministic stage-damage function models relative yield loss purely as a function of water depth using ordinary least-squares:

$$\text{Relative Yield Loss} = \alpha + \beta\sqrt{\text{water depth}} + \epsilon$$

Where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are parameters and $\epsilon$ represents model uncertainty.

- **The Localization Factor:** The study proved that localized models significantly outperform transferred models. For instance, a simple localized univariable SDF out-performed complex machine-learning models (like Random Forests) that were trained on foreign data (e.g., from Myanmar) and transferred to Thailand. Transferred models systematically underpredict losses for shallow, short-duration floods in Thai agricultural ecosystems.
    

#### C. Non-Financial Vulnerability Overlay (Urban Health Impacts)

Physical hazard modeling can also be linked to public health outcomes. In Hat Yai Municipality (Southern Thailand), researchers established localized relationships showing a strong linear correlation between seasonal flood depths and waterborne disease outbreaks (specifically leptospirosis cases), achieving an $R^2$ of 0.85:

$$R^2 = 0.85$$

This allows public and private organizations to evaluate localized heat/flood hazards in terms of labor productivity, worker absenteeism, and public health costs.

### 6. Market Gap Analysis & Strategic Entry Strategy for a Thai Venture

#### Why Global Climate Risk Tools Leave a Gap in Thailand

Large-scale global platforms (such as Jupiter or Climanomics) are built for multinational financial institutions and operate at a high-cost enterprise pricing tier. These platforms fail to serve local organizations in Thailand for several reasons:

1. **Coarse Global Resolution:** They rely on global elevation models and generalized satellite data, which fail to capture localized hydrological variations in complex Thai catchments like the lower Chao Phraya basin.
    
2. **Lack of Thai Asset & Crop Sensitivity:** They use generalized damage curves and cannot differentiate between local building styles (e.g., wooden on stilts vs. low-plinth masonry) or local agricultural cycles (e.g., specific planting timelines for jasmine rice).
    
3. **Regulatory Disconnect:** Global platforms are not mapped to Thailand's specific compliance timelines (e.g., the SEC Form 56-1 One Report-S or the Bank of Thailand's Taxonomy Phase 2 rules).

#### The Emerging Venture Business Model: "Thai Climate Risk Data Clearinghouse"

To successfully enter this market, an emerging company does not need to build an expensive, end-to-end global climate model. Instead, the venture should act as a **specialized climate data localization engine and compliance helper**. The venture can monetize three distinct modular offerings:

##### Offering 1: The Localized Hazard & Exposure Catalog

- **The Service:** Source, clean, and format fragmented government climate data into an easily accessible digital catalog or API.
    
- **The Process:** Process TMD station observations to resolve missing data using statistical imputation, extract repeating flood boundaries from GISTDA, and pull real-time watershed capacities from HII's NHC. Combine these with social deprivation layers from TPMAP.
    
- **Value Proposition:** Provide local consulting firms and corporates with a "one-stop-shop" to map their physical asset locations (coordinates) against highly localized historical and forward-looking hazard layers.
    

##### Offering 2: Thai-Specific Vulnerability Curve Database

- **The Service:** License and deliver specific vulnerability curves (such as Komolafe's logarithmic curves for building types and the 2026 Copernicus rice crop damage curves) as an off-the-shelf database or Excel-based toolkit (similar to the RECCC framework).
    
- **Value Proposition:** Allow commercial banks to run stress tests on their housing mortgage portfolios and agricultural loan portfolios without having to build internal modeling teams. Banks can easily compute expected annual financial losses using local empirical data.
    

##### Offering 3: SEC and Taxonomy Template Builder

- **The Service:** A software-as-a-service (SaaS) template builder that takes asset exposure data and formats it directly into the required reporting outputs for the SEC Form 56-1 One Report-S and the Bank of Thailand's Taxonomy Phase 2 classification.
    
- **Value Proposition:** Target mid-cap listed companies entering the mandatory disclosure timelines in 2026/2027, drastically reducing their consulting fees and assurance prep times.
    

### 7. Key Takeaways and Strategic Entry Playbook

1. **Geographic Focus First:** Prioritize the **lower Chao Phraya River basin** (including the Bangkok metropolitan area, Pathum Thani, and Ayutthaya). This area covers only a third of Thailand's landmass but accounts for 40% of its population, 66% of its GDP, and represents the highest concentration of bank and industrial asset exposure.
    
2. **Target the Compliance Catalyst:** The immediate revenue generator is not long-term adaptation planning, but short-term regulatory pressure. Target mid-market companies who must disclose climate-first indicators to meet the **December 2026 and December 2027 SEC timelines**.
    
3. **Build Strategic Partnerships for Data Legitimacy:** Collaborate with the **Thailand Greenhouse Gas Management Organization (TGO)**, local universities (which conduct field surveys), and registered audit firms. Having your localized vulnerability curves pre-vetted or recognized by TGO-registered verifiers makes your data "audit-ready" and defensible for listed corporate clients.
    
4. **The "SaaS to Adaptation Advisory" Roadmap:** Use the low-cost data catalog and template builder (Modules 1 & 3) as a low-friction entry point to acquire customers. As these clients mature under regulatory pressure, expand the relationship by advising them on local physical adaptation ROI—helping them transition from basic exposure screening to choosing localized adaptation investments like expanding green spaces, wetland restoration, and retention reservoirs.
