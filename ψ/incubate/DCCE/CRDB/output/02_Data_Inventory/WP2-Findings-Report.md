# Thailand's Climate Data Landscape: What Exists, What It Serves, and Where the Gaps Are

## Purpose

Thailand's Department of Climate Change and Environment (DCCE) already holds, or has access to, a large and varied body of climate and disaster-related data — climate model projections, hazard maps, disaster records, socio-economic vulnerability statistics, and more, spread across dozens of government agencies. As DCCE moves to consolidate this landscape into a coherent national data platform, two concrete near-term obligations give a natural starting point for the question "which of this data actually matters, and for what": Thailand's upcoming Biennial Transparency Report (BTR) submission to the UNFCCC, and the country's ongoing need for authoritative national disaster loss-and-damage statistics.

This report asks three questions against that lens: which existing datasets and data domains are genuinely important to support these two obligations; how the existing DCCE climate risk map fits into this picture; and what data these obligations require that simply does not exist yet, anywhere in the current landscape. The findings are grounded in a systematic reading of the BTR's own text (all 379 of its underlying reporting requirements) and Thailand's national data catalogue (over 260 datasets across more than 40 agencies), matched against each other item by item — not a general impression, but a documented, evidence-cited comparison.

## What the data actually needs to support

Two demands anchor this analysis. The first is Thailand's BTR — the country's formal report to the UNFCCC on its climate adaptation progress, which draws on national circumstances, hazard trends, sector-by-sector risk, institutional monitoring systems, and disaster impact statistics. The second is the standing need for reliable, comprehensive national statistics on climate-related loss and damage — a service DCCE has to be able to produce more or less continuously, not just for one report.

Reading the BTR's text closely surfaces something worth stating plainly: most of what it asks for is not exotic. It wants basic, well-defined things — how many people are affected by floods each year, what the country's temperature and rainfall trends look like, which provinces face the highest drought risk, how much economic damage disasters cause. The demand side of this exercise is, in other words, fairly mundane and well-precedented. The interesting part is the supply side: how much of that demand is actually met by data Thailand already has, in a form fit for the purpose.

## The datasets and data domains that matter most

Several clusters of existing data stand out as genuinely important once matched against what the BTR and the loss-and-damage service actually require.

**National disaster and property-damage records** are the single most consequential finding of this exercise. Thailand's Department of Disaster Prevention and Mitigation maintains ten years of historical disaster-occurrence records and a parallel record of reported property damage (roads, livestock, temples, and similar assets) tied to local-government relief payments. These two records are, structurally, the closest thing the country has to a standing national disaster-loss-statistics dataset — they are the right *shape* of data for both the BTR's own disaster-impact table and the broader loss-and-damage service. But this is also where the clearest institutional gap in the whole landscape shows up: the department's own documentation openly states that the data flows one way, from local authorities up to the province, with no central fact-checking; that it lacks a standard damage-classification taxonomy aligned with the UN's disaster-risk-reduction terminology; and that local officials often enter data late or miscategorized, to the point that automated cleanup tooling has been identified as a need. In other words, Thailand does not lack a disaster-loss dataset — it lacks a *trustworthy, standardized* one, built on top of data that already exists but needs real investment to become authoritative.

**Thailand's national climate observation and projection data**, maintained by DCCE itself through its Climate Data Services platform, is extensive and well-matched to the BTR's climate-science content: historical and future-projected temperature, rainfall, and humidity, generated from multiple global climate models under internationally standard future-scenario pathways, spanning from the 1960s through to the end of the century. This is one of the more mature and complete parts of the landscape — the BTR's climate narrative (temperature records, rainfall trends, drought and flood projections) is well-supported by data DCCE already produces and maintains.

**The Thai Meteorological Department's climate-extreme indices** — measures like the number of unusually hot days per year, the length of warm spells, and the intensity of the heaviest rainfall events — provide direct, ready-made support for the specific extreme-weather statistics the BTR cites. These are strong, well-defined matches, though currently held as internal project data rather than published openly.

**Hazard-specific spatial datasets** — flood-prone area maps, drought-risk indices, landslide-susceptibility maps, wildfire burn-area records, coastal erosion tracking, and sea-level observation data, held variously by the Geo-Informatics and Space Technology Development Agency, the Department of Mineral Resources, the Department of Marine and Coastal Resources, the Royal Forest Department, and others — cover the basic geography of each hazard type reasonably well. What they consistently do not cover is the *derived, computed* layer the BTR actually reports: return periods for major floods, probability estimates for landslides under different climate scenarios, or the specific attribution of an event's severity to climate change. The raw hazard geography exists; the risk analytics built on top of it, for the most part, do not.

**Population, vulnerability, and demographic statistics** — from the Department of Provincial Administration, the National Statistical Office, the National Economic and Social Development Council, and other social-welfare-focused agencies — provide solid support for the BTR's discussion of who is most exposed to climate risk: the elderly, children, people with disabilities, low-income households. One practical limitation is worth flagging: the national census, the richest source for this kind of data, is only updated once a decade, whereas the BTR increasingly needs current-year figures.

**Environmental impact records** — coral bleaching monitoring by the Department of Marine and Coastal Resources, and forest-cover tracking by the Royal Forest Department — directly and specifically support the BTR's ecosystem-impact narrative, with recorded dates that line up well with the events the report actually cites.

## The existing DCCE climate risk map

DCCE already operates a national spatial climate risk map, publicly accessible through its Climate Change Information Center, and it is worth understanding precisely and honestly, because it plays a real but specific role in this landscape rather than being a general-purpose solution.

The risk map is explicitly named inside the BTR text itself, cited as completed national infrastructure, which makes it one of the best-corroborated products in the entire data landscape. Its methodology, confirmed directly from the underlying technical report, follows the international climate-science convention set out by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change: risk is calculated as the mathematical product of a hazard index, an exposure index, and a vulnerability index, each built from dozens of underlying social and economic variables — population density, income inequality, irrigation coverage, healthcare access, and many others — drawn from more than a dozen different government agencies. Every input variable is treated as equally important (no variable is weighted more heavily than another) and all values are mathematically rescaled onto a common 0.1-to-0.9 range before being combined, a design choice made specifically so that the multiplication doesn't collapse to zero if any single input happens to be zero.

This design has a direct and important consequence for how the risk map should be used. Because the underlying variables are compressed together — multiplied and averaged down to a single risk score per province, per sector, per hazard type — that compression cannot be undone. There is no way to work backward from the published risk score to recover the specific underlying values that produced it. This makes the risk map genuinely valuable as a policy-communication and prioritization tool: it is exactly the kind of clear, defensible, single-number summary that helps a province understand its relative risk standing and helps national planners direct attention and budget. But it is not, and structurally cannot be, a reusable building block for other data products or analyses — the granular, disaggregated inputs that actually carry the useful detail live in the various source agencies' own data, not in the composite score itself.

That distinction matters for how the underlying data should be treated going forward: the dozens of source datasets that feed the risk map — population statistics, land-use data, water-resource records, health indicators, agricultural statistics, and more, spread across the same agencies already discussed above — are the real long-term asset for building further analytical products. The composite risk map itself should be understood, and used, as a finished output for public communication and high-level prioritization, not as raw material.

## Where the real gaps are

Several things the BTR and the loss-and-damage service genuinely need simply do not exist yet, anywhere in the current data landscape, and are worth naming plainly rather than glossing over:

- A soil-moisture-retention or water-absorption-capacity dataset — the single most frequently requested variable across the underlying service-demand analysis that fed this work, and one with no equivalent anywhere in the national catalogue.
- Any measure of the day-to-day temperature swing (the gap between daily high and low temperatures) as a standalone, accessible dataset.
- Thailand's standing in the internationally published Global Climate Risk Index — an external ranking product, not something the country produces itself, but one the BTR needs to cite.
- A national economic-loss-as-a-share-of-GDP time series, or a macroeconomic projection of climate-driven GDP impact.
- Mortality data specifically attributable to extreme heat, broken down by demographic group.
- Nutritional-status trend data (rates of childhood stunting and wasting) connected to climate stress.
- Any dataset tracking the rollout or coverage of Thailand's cell-broadcast emergency-alert system.
- Participation counts for community-based disaster-risk-management programmes.
- A standing implementation of the internationally recognized post-disaster assessment methodologies (rapid damage assessment, post-disaster needs assessment, and detailed damage-and-loss assessment) as an actual operating system, rather than a one-off exercise.
- A loss-and-damage dataset specifically structured for the UN's Sendai Framework disaster-reporting requirements, or for financial-sector stress-testing purposes.
- Confirmation of whether two specific named analytical products referenced elsewhere in Thailand's adaptation reporting — an annual "Vulnerability Index" and a multi-year "Climate Resilience Index" — are distinct products that exist somewhere, or whether they were never actually built as standing datasets. This remains genuinely unresolved and is worth a direct inquiry to DCCE.

None of these gaps are exotic or unreasonable asks — each is the kind of statistic a mature national climate-reporting system would be expected to produce. Their absence is not a failure of imagination; it is a concrete, addressable list of what needs to be built, and by whom.

## Closing assessment

Put together, the picture is neither "Thailand lacks the data it needs" nor "Thailand's data landscape is already complete" — it is more specific and more useful than either. The country has strong, mature climate-observation infrastructure and a genuinely well-designed risk-communication tool in its climate risk map. It has reasonable, if scattered, coverage of hazard geography and social vulnerability. Its weakest point, and also its highest-value opportunity, is the disaster-loss-and-damage records held by the Department of Disaster Prevention and Mitigation: the right dataset already exists, in the wrong condition, and fixing it — building a proper data pipeline to clean, standardize, and centrally verify these records — would do more to close Thailand's reporting gap than almost any other single investment identified in this review. Beyond that, a short, well-defined list of genuinely missing datasets — mostly around derived risk analytics, health and demographic outcomes, and internationally standardized methodologies — represents real, fundable next steps rather than an open-ended research problem.
