# Service Business Narratives

**Status**: Draft, pending review.

This document lays out, service by service, why each of the nine proposed climate-information services exists, who actually needs it, what it could grow into, and where it genuinely stands today. Two of these — the historical loss-and-damage service and the national reporting pipeline — are priority services that will later get a full, detailed specification; what follows here is their conceptual foundation, not a substitute for that work.

---

## Service 1 — Certified Climate Data Repository & Official Data Endorsement

Fragmented data and the absence of any standard certification are the obstacle stakeholders raise most often. Government agencies and private firms alike report losing time searching for data and worrying about verifying its source whenever they need to use it for a decision with legal or financial weight. This service exists to fix that directly: a central place to find and access datasets that DCCE has certified to a recognized standard, acting as a single trusted source with consistent, Thailand-standard descriptions, so a user can search, download, and use data quickly and with confidence — without a formal request letter to the source agency and without redoing the quality check themselves. It's meant to be foundational: the other climate information services depend on it existing alongside them, not after them.

That foundational framing isn't rhetorical. In an internal reflection on its own data governance, DCCE staff acknowledged that even inside the department itself, there is no consolidated picture of what data products and services currently exist — no central catalog, even for DCCE's own holdings. If the certifying body's own inventory isn't consolidated yet, this service is not an optional enhancement sitting alongside the others; it's the precondition several of them are quietly assuming already exists.

One concrete shape this could take: a "broker" model, where DCCE doesn't try to host every dataset itself, only a directory that certifies and routes users to the real source, following international metadata-discovery conventions rather than an ad-hoc scheme. A first success could be as narrow as one "Official Data Badge" issued for a single core hazard dataset — rainfall, say — naming its source, update cycle, and terms of use. Separately, this certification function ties naturally to a real external demand lever: Thailand's securities regulator and central bank are both moving toward requiring climate-related financial disclosure, which gives certified DCCE data a concrete audience beyond government itself — the idea of DCCE as the authoritative trust layer underneath everyone else's climate reporting, not a data host. Two open risks are worth naming rather than glossing over: agencies can simply decline to share data with no real recourse, and a certification-first design risks excluding small businesses and the informal economy who can't produce the paperwork a certification process would ask for. None of this is committed — it's the range of what this service could become.

The website doesn't have a dedicated home for this service yet. The closest thing to it is the site's own data-catalog page — DCCE already runs a searchable system for datasets, so that page is treated as basically covered — but what's been supplied toward it so far is only a starting seed; the catalog's real scope and content still need to be worked out properly. The deeper problem sits underneath almost everything else on the platform: every dataset examined so far is still formally a draft, unverified, and has no named person or office responsible for keeping it current. That has to be resolved before any of it can be shown as authoritative — which is, in effect, exactly the gap this service exists to close. It isn't a single-page problem that building the website will fix on its own; it needs to become a real certification workflow, run across the whole catalog.

## Service 2 — High-Resolution Spatial Risk Analytics

Agencies working at the level of an actual project — social welfare planners, financial institutions, local governments, sector specialists — consistently report that province-level risk data isn't fine enough to plan against. They need risk information down to the subdistrict, municipality, household, or even individual land-plot level. This service exists to take future climate-hazard risk analysis down to that grain, so agencies can plan hazard response, reduce risk, and assess likely impact with real precision: pre-positioning support for bedridden or otherwise vulnerable residents before a hazard hits, rather than reacting after. The demand behind it spans urban risk analysis, risk to biodiversity, the economic resilience of households dependent on farming or manufacturing income, identifying the point at which a neighborhood becomes genuinely unlivable and needs long-term relocation planning, proactive welfare-program design for people with disabilities, matching risk areas and vulnerable groups to real municipal boundaries for resource and budget allocation, mapping urban heat risk among the urban poor, and plot-level agricultural monitoring to support both recovery policy and carbon-credit programs.

Interview evidence from Thailand's banking sector sharpens this well beyond a general statement of need. Major banks report directly that province-level data simply isn't useful to them; they need the probability of flooding at a specific asset's exact location, coverage of multiple flood types together — rainfall, river, and coastal — and how deep and how long the water sits, not just an index score. They explicitly prefer probability-based maps over simple index maps, and at least one bank already relies on an outside research institute's flood-zone map in the absence of anything better from DCCE. This is concrete, current demand, not a hypothetical.

One design direction worth exploring: a real resolution target — roughly the size of a city block for flood risk, finer than what's used today for most other hazards — layered with existing national poverty and social-vulnerability data, so hazard and poverty can be seen together rather than separately. A first success could be a detailed risk profile for a single pilot province, naming the specific small areas at highest seasonal flood risk. A workable version of this service would include a spatial risk layer, a map of where vulnerable populations sit relative to that risk, and a local dashboard — tested against a real question: could a local health officer actually use it to move resources to a specific at-risk household before a flood hits.

This service already has a place on the website, with much of its content ready to build. But the sharpest current problem sits underneath it: DCCE's existing risk index can't actually be broken down below the province level — the data behind it was never collected finely enough to disaggregate, so what's already built cannot deliver the precision this service promises. That isn't a display problem, it's a data problem. Two ways to fix it have been identified — pulling structured local detail out of the 77 provincial climate and disaster plans that already exist as documents, and reworking the underlying risk data to align with actual municipal boundaries instead of the administrative-district approximations used today — but both are still waiting on a decision to actually resource them.

## Service 3 — Finance & Budget Decision Support

The obstacle project-owning agencies report most is a budget-approval problem: when infrastructure is designed to withstand a changing future climate, it costs more than the historical price for similar past projects, and administrators face real risk in justifying that gap to the bodies that approve and audit public spending. This service exists as a legitimacy mechanism — a way to calculate the value of losses avoided, weigh costs against benefits, and marshal other economic evidence — so administrators have something concrete to show government auditors and budget authorities: that paying more now protects public finances and reduces the recovery bill later. The demand behind it includes financial-sector loss modeling, real economic-loss estimation to inform emergency budget decisions, risk-adjusted return-on-investment certification for large infrastructure loans, communicating the financial case for climate-resilient transport infrastructure that costs more than historical benchmarks, comparing the returns of nature-based approaches against conventional grey infrastructure, and evidence packages for local governments preparing annual budgets or requesting to draw on reserve funds.

A workable version of this already has a shape worth committing to: a usable output separates at least three distinct loss categories, states plainly what cannot yet be estimated and why rather than hiding the gap, and its test of success is operational, not abstract — a real agency names a point of contact, agrees to review one pilot output, supplies one dataset, and asks for the package ahead of an actual reporting deadline.

One possible technical core: build on empirical damage relationships specific to Thailand rather than imported formulas, and quantify the actual return on three concrete adaptation measures — irrigation infrastructure, climate-adapted crop varieties, and adjusted planting schedules. A first success could be a single evidence brief for one pilot project — nature-based drainage, say — showing avoided losses against a conventional alternative, tested against a real question: would the national budget office actually accept "avoided loss" reasoning as justification for a materially higher project cost. All of this stays a direction to explore, not a commitment.

This service already has a place on the website, but it sits underneath the single largest cluster of unresolved questions found anywhere on the platform. The blocker is the same across nearly all of them: no cost-benefit method exists yet, no standard for tagging budget as climate spending exists, no accepted way to calculate avoided losses exists, no library of damage relationships exists to calculate them from — a methodology problem before it's a platform problem. One pattern repeats throughout: every request for evidence of technology transfer or capacity-building support currently turns up records of money moving only — a grant disbursed, a proposal funded — with nothing on what capability or technology actually resulted. That's one absence showing up everywhere, not several unrelated ones. Given all this, this service can't honestly be scoped as ready to build next. What this document captures is the demand — real and well evidenced — not a specification ready for implementation. The missing methodology is the genuine prerequisite, and inventing one here wouldn't be credible.

## Service 4 — Historical Loss & Damage Assessment

Planning agencies and the financial sector both report the same limitation: government's own historical relief and recovery spending doesn't give a true, long-term picture of economic loss by sector — the kind of data needed to make the case for investing ahead of worsening disasters. This service exists to build a real evidence base of disaster impacts from the past through the present: comprehensive statistics on both economic loss and losses that don't show up in a balance sheet, built on a transparent method, serving as evidence for future recovery and relief budget planning and as a baseline for testing economic-impact models across sectors. The demand behind it includes improving the accuracy of national disaster loss assessment, macro-level sectoral economic loss reporting, loss disclosure aligned with international disaster-reporting frameworks, a method for financial-sector stress testing, and tourism-sector impact assessment to prioritize recovery.

This is a genuinely important gap to close: right now, nobody has written the actual methodology for how to calculate loss and damage, and several pieces of planned website content are effectively waiting on it before they can be built responsibly. The same acceptance test set out for Service 3 applies here as well: a usable output separates at least three loss categories, states plainly what cannot yet be estimated and why, and its test of success is operational — a real agency names a point of contact, agrees to review one pilot output, supplies one dataset, and asks for the package ahead of a real reporting deadline.

Unusually, a real methodology sketch already exists for this exact service and is worth treating seriously as a starting point. It proposes a bridge in terminology — from the raw source, government emergency relief payments, to the service's real goal, an estimate of true economic damage. It sketches a weighting formula combining disaster deaths, people affected, and relief spending relative to the size of the provincial economy, normalized so provinces can genuinely be compared to each other, piloted first at the province level with a path toward much finer geography later. The proposed path forward: fix the terminology first, cross-walk the relief-payment categories against the sectors they actually affected, apply damage ratios drawn from established international research to estimate the real economic cost, then check the result against known, independently-documented disaster events from the past. None of this is settled — it's a strong, citable starting point for the real methodology, not the methodology itself.

A separate, already-planned piece of website content shows historical disaster event counts and trends — not their economic value — and draws on the very same underlying disaster records as this service. That means some of the work of gathering and cleaning that data can genuinely serve both efforts, but the loss-and-damage valuation method itself remains a gap only this service's own work can close; the other content being ready to build does not mean this one is. Two questions stay genuinely open: records that name individuals or households will need a privacy review before they can be published, and nobody has yet said how long it actually takes to get restricted disaster records released — that needs a direct answer from the disaster agency that holds them, not a guess.

## Service 5 — Engineering Design Variables

Engineers and infrastructure managers report a specific technical problem: current design standards are based on historical statistics that no longer match today's more volatile climate, leaving infrastructure genuinely at risk of damage and, over time, at risk to the safety of the people who use it. This service exists as the bridge between climate science and engineering practice — converting future climate projections into concrete design guidance, so engineers can calculate and design drainage systems, roads, and ports with solid evidence for future conditions rather than past ones. The demand behind it includes converting rainfall intensity, peak flow, and temperature extremes into the specific numbers used to design road drainage; supplying risk, hazard, and land-suitability data under future scenarios for sustainable urban planning; updating standard rainfall-intensity curves from decades-old averages to curves based on future projections for urban drainage design; folding landslide and soil-stability risk into the building-permit process; and assessing risk to ports and other marine infrastructure to plan maintenance and upgrades against future storms and rising seas.

Real institutional traction for this already exists, not just a hypothetical need. The national agency responsible for public works and town planning is currently updating its wind-load design standard to reflect changing wind speeds, already runs a nature-based urban heat-reduction program, and already operates under a 2019 planning law that legally embeds climate resilience into zoning decisions. A working group already covers all 76 provinces, producing area-level risk and adaptation plans. None of this was invented for this service — it's existing institutional machinery this service could plug directly into.

One possible direction: convert global climate projections into the specific runoff and peak-flow figures engineers actually use, with a focus on localized rainfall-intensity curves. A first success could be a single design-standard update for one system — Bangkok's urban drainage network, say — stating new recommended safety margins for a mid-century scenario, tested against a real question: would the national highways authority actually adopt those figures in its next major procurement round.

This work is explicitly being deferred, not attempted in the current phase. The blocker is concrete: producing real, defensible rainfall-intensity curves requires statistics that don't exist anywhere in DCCE's current holdings, at any level of detail — a substantial undertaking in its own right. It would be easy to assume this falls out of other visualization work for free; it doesn't — it's real, separately-timed work needing its own data. One clarification worth stating plainly: this cannot be built by repurposing DCCE's existing risk index — that index serves the general hazard map, not plot-level engineering curves, and the two should not be conflated even though they sound adjacent. What this document captures is the demand and the genuine institutional hooks — the wind-load standard update, the 76-province working group — worth connecting to whenever this service is properly taken on.

## Service 6 — Multi-Hazard Impact-Based Early Warning

Field practitioners and business operators frame the core problem the same way: the gap isn't knowing the weather, it's knowing the *impact* — being able to act in time, specifically in places with real economic activity or vulnerable residents. This service exists to convert weather data into a picture of impact on life and property, predicting the chain effects of multiple hazards together and issuing alerts that factor in local vulnerability — how well a specific area's soil absorbs water, for instance — so the person receiving the alert understands the real severity where they actually are, not a generic weather statistic. The demand behind it includes managing industrial drought impact on production, forecasting heat-related health risk for outdoor workers and older residents to trigger cooling-shelter activation, estimating the financial cost of business interruption for small businesses, marine ecosystem surveillance such as early coral-bleaching warnings to close conservation areas in time, and setting localized flash-flood thresholds using each area's actual capacity to absorb water.

Two pieces of named evidence sharpen this well beyond a general statement of need. Thailand's industrial-sector association already runs a systemic water-management program and operates its own carbon-credit trading platform under real international climate-disclosure pressure, giving this service a concrete, named private-sector system it could interface with rather than a hypothetical audience. Separately, the banking sector has flagged real concern about cascading and supply-chain effects and wants numeric, sector-specific pathways for how transition risk actually plays out — directly relevant to the multi-hazard, chain-effect idea this service is built around.

One possible direction: set localized flood-warning thresholds based on each area's actual soil absorption, and add a health layer that links flood depth to waterborne-disease risk, triggering health warnings automatically. A first success could be a single well-designed alert for one hazard — extreme heat, say — carrying a specific operational instruction for outdoor workers rather than a bare temperature figure, tested against a real question: would local industrial estates actually trigger their business-continuity plans based on alerts like this.

This service has no home anywhere on the current website — not a content gap, a structural one. The only remotely related thing that already exists anywhere on the site is a static diagram explaining how hazards cascade into impacts — useful as an explainer, but not the same as delivering a live, localized alert, and it shouldn't be mistaken for one. Surfacing a service like this requires a decision about the site's own structure, not just a content review — there's genuinely no existing DCCE platform anywhere to build from, so this is a wholly new service, not an underdeveloped one. What this document captures is the demand; the fact that it has no home on the site yet is itself a real signal that it sits at a lower priority tier right now, not an oversight waiting to be quietly corrected.

## Service 7 — Adaptation Policy Monitoring & Evaluation

Policymakers and funding bodies need a tool that clearly shows what's working and what isn't, to avoid wasting limited budget and resources, and to demonstrate the country's progress concretely on the international stage. This service exists as the central function evaluating how effective adaptation measures actually are, connecting national and local targets, and tracking both progress and where the country's technical capability still falls short, so policy execution has real direction under national climate law. The demand behind it includes tracking the country's technology readiness to direct research funding at closing real gaps, adapting local-government performance indicators to align with national adaptation goals, supporting funders who want to finance spatial planning that prevents future disaster impact, and compiling the data Thailand needs for its international adaptation reporting.

This service needs to be understood relative to something that already exists, not written as if the space were empty. A separately built monitoring platform is already live — a substantial budget, collecting manually-entered data from eighteen agencies across the national plan's six sectors, explicitly built to feed the country's official climate reporting. A documented internal critique of that same platform is on record: real "big data" branding sitting on only a couple hundred rows of actual data a year, no automated data pipelines anywhere — the whole thing runs on people typing numbers into forms — and a real risk of being locked into one vendor once the original funding runs out. The verdict on file is blunt: paying for the policy alignment work, but getting a portal. The platform's real value is judged to be the work of getting agencies to agree on shared indicators, not the software itself. The genuine opportunity for this service may be less about building a new tracker from scratch and more about deciding whether and how that existing alignment work gets carried forward, or rebuilt properly, rather than duplicated.

One possible direction: align national indicators with the timelines corporate and financial regulators are already imposing for climate disclosure, and add a way to track how mature the country's adaptation technologies actually are. A first success could be a single scorecard rolling up provincial results into one format usable for international reporting, tested against a real question: would the national policy committee actually use a scorecard like this to help decide where to direct the national climate fund.

This service already has a well-covered home on the website. The link to the country's international adaptation-reporting goal and the national progress tracker are both confirmed and already tied into Thailand's official climate reporting; a library of successful project case studies is unusually well covered too. One item — a framework for tracking technology readiness — is currently assumed covered because it falls inside DCCE's existing monitoring platform, but nobody has actually confirmed that specific content exists; worth a direct check before relying on it. A related but genuinely different need sits next to this one, not inside it: tracking the status of individual adaptation projects and holding a repository of local and private-sector plans. That's blocked for a different reason — that information currently lives with DCCE's own programme staff and outside organizations, findable only by asking directly, not through any existing system. It needs regular internal reporting put in place before any system could hold it, which is separate work from this service.

## Service 8 — Uncertainty Governance & Institutional Shield

Agencies planning under future climate scenarios need to be able to design policy that stays flexible and resilient as conditions change, and to communicate the real uncertainty in risk data honestly rather than glossing over it. Deep feedback from the financial sector, national infrastructure planners, and other agencies with real adaptation-project experience converges on one point: the biggest limitation isn't a shortage of data, it's concern about legal liability and the credibility of interpretation when high-uncertainty data has to support a decision with real budget and financial-stability consequences. This service is designed as an institutional shield — a single national-standard way of managing and communicating climate-data uncertainty, so that worry turns into a decision grounded in due diligence and legally defensible evidence, not paralysis. Its use cases give this concrete institutional shape: confirming budget legitimacy for transport and local-government planning bodies, using an official allowance standard as a reference when justifying a project's value for money to auditors; realistic financial risk analysis for banks, where analysts use standardized guidance to calculate expected losses that reflect reality rather than over-rejecting credit; data selection for urban planners, who can rely on properly-graded data to set zoning with lower future litigation risk; and confidence management in DCCE's own national climate communication, so government, business, and the public share a common understanding.

Field evidence backs this precisely. Banks separately confirm that they currently treat a probability-based flood map as if it were a certainty map — applying a "hundred-year flood" boundary to loan exposure without accounting for what that actually means statistically — and say directly that their analysts need training and a standardized way of handling uncertainty. That's not a hypothetical concern; it's the same pain point reported independently in the field.

Three concrete mechanisms are already sketched, worth carrying forward as directions to explore rather than settled design: a tiered certification system — separating data ready for budget decisions from data only suitable for strategic planning or still-experimental research — stamped onto the data catalog; an official climate allowance standard, approved by the relevant policy body, so auditors and the budget office can accept higher construction costs as a standing rule rather than a one-off argument each time; and safe-use guidance for probability-based analysis, aimed specifically at preventing overstated risk in financial stress testing so credit access isn't blocked by a misread map. A parallel version of the same idea: a clear "fit for use" label on every dataset, a discipline of testing assumptions against multiple future scenarios before relying on them, and a short confidence note attached to every high-stakes output. None of this is locked.

This service has no home anywhere on the current website either — the same kind of structural gap as the early-warning service above, not a content gap. What already exists only thinly represents it: a page calling for a climate-scenario usage guide and a national uncertainty-management standard, where nothing yet exists for either — no guide, no standard — and the real blocker is developing the methodology and reaching institutional agreement on it, not a data or software problem. It's also explicitly the kind of gap that touches every other service on the platform, not just this one. The standing recommendation, reached independently more than once, is that this be built as a capability folded into every other service rather than stood up as its own separate product — worth stating plainly as the realistic path forward rather than implying a dedicated page is coming. One connection worth naming directly: the finding that DCCE's existing risk index can't be broken down below the province level is itself a concrete instance of exactly the opacity problem this service exists to solve. If this service gets built, it's the natural home for resolving that kind of problem generally, not just for the spatial risk service.

## Thailand's Biennial Transparency Report Pipeline

The institutional pain point here is well documented in the country's own reporting material, not asserted by this project. Thailand's national adaptation monitoring framework exists but is only partially operational, held back by fragmented data systems, inconsistent indicators, and limited coordination between agencies. Disaster mortality data is collected but can't currently be attributed to specific adaptation measures; losses avoided through adaptation aren't yet systematically quantified at the national level. Monitoring findings "cannot yet comprehensively demonstrate concrete progress," and the feedback loop from that monitoring back into policy isn't yet fully built into how government works. Technical expertise, data management capability, and analytical capacity vary significantly from one agency to the next. Current reporting, in the source material's own words, "mainly reflects activities undertaken and cannot yet adequately answer strategic questions about resilience strengthening." The real institutions involved are named too: the national climate change committee and cabinet for oversight, DCCE for coordination and monitoring, sector focal points and provincial working groups for execution on the ground.

The sharpest everyday version of this problem: a national reporting deadline arrives, and an unstructured data request goes out to dozens of agencies; each one manually searches its own isolated systems and pastes results into a spreadsheet; the results don't line up spatially — one agency uses province names, another uses postal codes — and don't mean the same thing to different people — "drought severity" isn't defined consistently across agencies; compiling the eventual national report takes months of manual work, and the final published numbers can't be traced back to where they came from. Thailand's own reporting material names four structural barriers behind this: gaps in data and knowledge (risk data isn't fine or timely enough, there's no standardized way to assess loss and damage, no standardized outcome indicators, and databases are fragmented with no central point of integration); institutional and coordination gaps (staffing and tooling constraints, policy-to-operations integration that's still incomplete, and inconsistent definitions that make aligning national data to international formats genuinely hard); financial and resource constraints (accessing international climate finance is procedurally complex, and there's no clear way to classify or track spending that's specifically adaptation-related, since it sits embedded inside broader development budgets); and gaps in implementation, reporting, and monitoring (reporting emphasizes activities and budget use rather than actual resilience outcomes, indicator interpretation is inconsistent, and quality-assurance is weak). The same reporting material's own forward-looking section makes the point from the other direction: it expects monitoring systems to support the country's international transparency reporting and enable evidence-based policy adjustment, while admitting in the same breath that measuring adaptation outcomes remains genuinely difficult and that data availability and consistency are major constraints — the aspiration and the honest limitation are both on the record, not just the aspiration.

No enrichment work exists yet for this specific pipeline, so what follows should be read the same way as the suggestions above: a possibility, not a settled design. One proposed solution: a structured data pipeline where raw submissions from each agency pass through data-quality checks, get standardized and spatially aligned to a common geography and a shared glossary of terms, then get aggregated into a single structure that feeds an automated national report — rather than the current spreadsheet-by-spreadsheet approach. Separately, a review of how this would fit into DCCE's broader data model proposes new structures derived directly from the country's own reporting requirements — including a way to track adaptation outcomes rather than just activities completed, fixing a longstanding bias in how this gets measured, and a way to tag budget specifically as adaptation spending. That review is itself explicit that it's raising open design questions, not closed answers.

This has to be reconciled against the same existing platform noted for the policy-monitoring service above — its dashboards are already explicitly built to output the charts Thailand's transparency reporting requires and are aligned to the same international framework. That's a real, adjacent, partially-overlapping system, not empty space this pipeline would be filling from nothing. On the positive side, the detailed review of national reporting requirements already carried out for this project has a concrete destination: hundreds of specific requirements have already been checked against the website's planned structure and shown to connect to a meaningful share of its content, so it's clear which parts of the site this pipeline's output is meant to feed, even before the pipeline itself is properly specified. One honest caveat on that same review, though: it's missing a data dictionary and a clear description of its own workflow, and there are open questions about exactly how its underlying evidence links back to each requirement — worth resolving before treating it as a finished foundation to build on.
