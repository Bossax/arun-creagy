# Service Business Narratives

**Status**: Draft, pending review.

This document lays out, service by service, why each of the eight proposed climate-information services exists, who actually needs it, what's blocking it, and what the smallest next step would be. One service — Historical Loss & Damage Assessment — and one enabling use case — the national reporting pipeline supporting Adaptation Monitoring & Evaluation — were selected as priorities for later detailed specification; what follows here is their conceptual foundation, not a substitute for that work.

Each service is written as a business case: **problem & evidence** (who has this, sourced), **value if solved**, **blocker today** (the real one, not a guess), and **the ask** (the smallest concrete next step). Design ideas, adjacent concepts, and other material that doesn't directly serve that case are kept in a separate **Side Notes** block per service — useful context for whoever specifies or builds these services next, but not part of the funding case itself.

---

## Service 1 — Climate Risk and Adaptation Data Catalog: คลังข้อมูลการประเมินความเสี่ยงและวางแผนการปรับตัว

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Fragmented data and inconsistent descriptions are the obstacles stakeholders raise most often — government agencies and private firms alike report losing time searching for climate-risk and adaptation data, establishing where it came from, checking whether it is current, and determining whether they are allowed to use it. The problem reaches inside DCCE itself: an internal reflection on its own data governance found no consolidated picture of what data products and services currently exist, not even for DCCE's own holdings.

**Value if solved.** A trusted central catalog for climate-risk assessment and adaptation planning, with consistent descriptions, sources, update cycles, access conditions, and terms of use. Users can discover the right data quickly, understand its status, and know how to obtain and use it without reconstructing that information themselves. It is foundational: the other climate-information services depend on these catalog and governance functions. There is a real audience beyond government too — Thailand's securities regulator and central bank are both moving toward mandatory climate-related financial disclosure, which gives well-described, authoritative climate data a concrete external user base.

**Blocker today.** The platform's own catalog — a deliberately separate build from DCCE's CKAN open-data system, since CKAN's metadata granularity doesn't serve product-development use — currently has only a starting seed, with real scope and content still undecided. Every dataset examined so far is still formally a draft and unverified, with no named person or office responsible for keeping it current. The catalog therefore cannot yet distinguish reliably between a dataset that merely appears in the inventory and one that is current, accessible, fit for a stated use, and maintained by an accountable owner. One real risk worth naming: agencies can decline to share data, with no recourse today.

**The ask.** One complete, usable catalog record for a single core dataset — rainfall, say — naming its source, owner, update cycle, access classification, terms of use, and intended applications, with a working route to obtain the data. This is the smallest version of trusted discovery working end to end.

### Side Notes

- A "broker" model is one possible shape: DCCE maintains the trusted catalog and routes users to the real source rather than hosting everything itself, following international metadata-discovery conventions (e.g. STAC) rather than an ad-hoc scheme.
- Official endorsement can be added as a governance feature for datasets that pass an agreed review, but it does not replace the catalog's core discovery, description, access, and maintenance functions.

## Service 2 — High-Resolution Spatial Risk Analytics

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Agencies working at the level of an actual project — social welfare planners, financial institutions, local governments, sector specialists — consistently report that province-level risk data isn't fine enough to plan against. They need risk information down to the subdistrict, municipality, household, or even individual land-plot level. Interview evidence from Thailand's banking sector sharpens this well beyond a general statement of need: major banks report directly that province-level data simply isn't useful to them; they need the probability of flooding at a specific asset's exact location, coverage of multiple flood types together — rainfall, river, and coastal — and how deep and how long the water sits, not just an index score. They explicitly prefer probability-based maps over simple index maps, and at least one bank already relies on an outside research institute's flood-zone map in the absence of anything better from DCCE.

**Value if solved.** Agencies can plan hazard response, reduce risk, and assess likely impact with real precision — pre-positioning support for bedridden or otherwise vulnerable residents before a hazard hits, rather than reacting after. The demand spans urban risk analysis, risk to biodiversity, the economic resilience of households dependent on farming or manufacturing income, identifying when a neighborhood becomes genuinely unlivable and needs relocation planning, proactive welfare-program design for people with disabilities, matching risk and vulnerable groups to real municipal boundaries for budget allocation, urban heat risk among the urban poor, and plot-level agricultural monitoring for both recovery policy and carbon-credit programs.

**Blocker today.** DCCE's existing risk index fixes province as its unit of analysis before the calculation ever runs — inputs from different line agencies at different granularities are harmonized up to province level first, not disaggregated down from something finer that already exists. On top of that, the index's own math makes the compression irreversible: hazard, exposure, and vulnerability sub-indices are multiplied and normalized into a single score, with no way to work backward to the underlying line-agency values. That's two independent structural reasons, not a display problem. Two fixes have been identified — pulling structured local detail out of the 77 provincial climate and disaster plans that already exist as documents, and reworking the underlying risk data to align with actual municipal boundaries instead of today's administrative-district approximations — but both are still waiting on a decision to resource them.

**The ask.** A detailed risk profile for a single pilot province, naming the specific small areas at highest seasonal flood risk — a spatial risk layer, a map of where vulnerable populations sit relative to that risk, and a local dashboard, tested against a real question: could a local health officer actually use it to move resources to a specific at-risk household before a flood hits.

### Side Notes

- A real resolution target — roughly city-block size for flood risk, finer than most other hazards today — layered with existing national poverty and social-vulnerability data, so hazard and poverty can be seen together.
- One bootstrap route: connect with Thailand's meteorological department for access to the Urban Act Project's outputs, where a handful of municipalities were already assessed at fine scale. Scaling that nationally is genuinely hard — it needs granular elevation and drainage-routing data that isn't consistently available outside those pilots. A workaround that scales further: a vulnerability/exposure index built from socioeconomic and environmental data, which is comparatively easy to obtain even where simulation isn't.
- Thailand's own biennial transparency reporting names a "Climate Resilience Index" concept, synthesized from sectoral trend assessments — but no built dataset behind it has been confirmed to exist anywhere, and it isn't the same thing as this service's risk index or the separate CRI project. A vulnerability/exposure index built for this service could be one possible first concrete version of that unbuilt concept — one possibility among several, not a design direction in its own right.

## Service 3 — Finance & Budget Decision Support

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** The obstacle project-owning agencies report most is a budget-approval problem: when infrastructure is designed to withstand a changing future climate, it costs more than the historical price for similar past projects, and administrators face real risk in justifying that gap to the bodies that approve and audit public spending. The demand behind it includes financial-sector loss modeling, real economic-loss estimation to inform emergency budget decisions, risk-adjusted return-on-investment certification for large infrastructure loans, communicating the financial case for climate-resilient transport infrastructure, comparing nature-based approaches against conventional grey infrastructure, and evidence packages for local governments preparing annual budgets or requesting reserve funds.

**Value if solved.** A legitimacy mechanism — a way to calculate the value of losses avoided, weigh costs against benefits, and marshal other economic evidence — so administrators have something concrete to show auditors and budget authorities: that paying more now protects public finances and reduces the recovery bill later.

**Blocker today.** This is the single largest cluster of unresolved questions found anywhere on the platform, and the blocker is the same across nearly all of them: no cost-benefit method exists yet, no standard for tagging budget as climate spending exists, no accepted way to calculate avoided losses exists, no library of damage relationships exists to calculate them from — a methodology problem before it's a platform problem. One pattern repeats throughout: every request for evidence of technology transfer or capacity-building support currently turns up records of money moving only — a grant disbursed, a proposal funded — with nothing on what capability or technology actually resulted. Given this, the service can't honestly be scoped as ready to build next; what's captured here is real, well-evidenced demand, not a specification ready for implementation. The methodology gap isn't the only prerequisite, either: this service needs real user research before it can be specified, because the metrics that would actually move a budget decision aren't established yet, and most users may not know which metrics matter until asked directly. That research has to run in parallel with methodology development, not after — a technically correct methodology the budgeting body doesn't recognize is worth nothing to the agencies this service is meant to serve, so the policy and advocacy work needed to get a methodology accepted is as much a prerequisite as the methodology itself.

**The ask.** A single financial evidence brief for one real adaptation investment — nature-based drainage, say — showing its costs, expected avoided losses, material uncertainties, and comparison with a conventional alternative. Its test of success is whether a project-owning agency and the relevant budget or audit authority agree that the evidence is usable in an actual approval process.

### Side Notes

- One possible technical core: build on empirical damage relationships specific to Thailand rather than imported formulas, and quantify the actual return on three concrete adaptation measures — irrigation infrastructure, climate-adapted crop varieties, and adjusted planting schedules.
- A first success could be a single evidence brief for one pilot project — nature-based drainage, say — showing avoided losses against a conventional alternative, tested against a real question: would the national budget office actually accept "avoided loss" reasoning as justification for a materially higher project cost.

## Service 4 — Historical Loss & Damage Assessment: การประเมินความสูญเสียและความเสียหายในอดีต

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Planning agencies and the financial sector both report the same limitation: government's own historical relief and recovery spending doesn't give a true, long-term picture of economic loss by sector — the kind of data needed to make the case for investing ahead of worsening disasters. The demand behind it includes improving the accuracy of national disaster loss assessment, macro-level sectoral economic loss reporting, loss disclosure aligned with international disaster-reporting frameworks, a method for financial-sector stress testing, and tourism-sector impact assessment to prioritize recovery.

**Value if solved.** A real evidence base of disaster impacts from the past through the present — comprehensive statistics on both economic loss and losses that don't show up in a balance sheet, built on a transparent method — serving as evidence for future recovery and relief budget planning and as a baseline for testing economic-impact models across sectors. In addition, the historical record shows which people, assets, sectors, and places have repeatedly borne the greatest consequences, allowing future risk-reduction plans and investments to target those patterns before the next disaster occurs. This matters beyond this section: the platform's own design specification is waiting on it directly. Four requirements there — a national macroeconomic loss-and-damage database, a historical loss dashboard, a non-economic loss record, and a standard national manual for risk, impact, and loss calculation — are recorded as blocked for one explicit reason: this service's own methodology hadn't been written yet, and the design specification says whoever eventually writes it should start from those four.

**Blocker today.** The methodology gap is closing, not open. NESDC has commissioned a researcher from Chulalongkorn University to build a true economic loss-and-damage framework, aligned to international standards — World Bank DaLA/ECLAC, FAO's agricultural damage-and-loss approach, and UNDRR's Sendai Framework — with the current phase running through June 2026 and focused first on direct agricultural losses. Sector-by-sector damage and loss formulas already exist in the commissioned work, along with a worked national estimate: cumulative disaster loss of roughly ฿1.62 trillion from 2006 to 2024, split 61.8% damage and 38.2% loss. CRDB's own review has already begun aligning this service's data model to it. Whether it also satisfies the fourth blocked requirement — the calculation manual — is a real possibility worth confirming rather than assuming: the design specification's own accounting treats that manual as still unmet, with an impact-chain manual on file that's explicitly not written for this purpose. NESDC's framework reads as a stronger candidate than anything currently on record, but that's a connection for someone to verify against the finished methodology, not a settled fact.

With a real methodology now in hand, the blocker has moved from method to data: the national disaster agency's records give affected people, households, deaths, and relief payments, but no monetary damage series, no sector-level split of provincial economic output, and no way to tell whether a zero in the record means nothing happened or means the data was never collected — that has to be resolved before the NESDC-commissioned methodology can actually be run against real numbers. Two questions stay genuinely open: records naming individuals or households will need a privacy review before they can be published, and nobody has yet said how long it actually takes to get restricted disaster records released — that needs a direct answer from the disaster agency that holds them.

A separate, already-planned piece of website content — the disaster-statistics product covering historical event counts and extreme-weather trends — isn't really a separate product sharing source data; it's the groundwork this service is built on. There's no L&D analytics without basic disaster statistics first, and the platform's own design specification already flags gathering that data once for both rather than running two separate compilation efforts — this should be viewed as one service with multiple access points on the website, not two adjacent ones. That same specification also notes the macroeconomic loss-and-damage database asked for here can likely be delivered the same lightweight way: as a rollup view built on top of the loss dashboard's own data, not as an independent build — worth carrying into how this service's build gets sequenced.

**The ask.** A usable output separates loss categories, states plainly what cannot yet be estimated and why, and its test of success is operational — a real agency names a point of contact, agrees to review one pilot output, supplies one dataset, and asks for the package ahead of a real reporting deadline.

### Side Notes

- A separate methodology sketch exists in adjacent project work — a distinct, DCCE-commissioned index-development effort, unconnected to NESDC's work — proposing a narrower proxy: a weighting formula combining disaster deaths, people affected, and relief spending relative to provincial economy size, built because that project didn't have monetary damage data to work with. It measures the scale of government relief response, not true economic damage, and isn't an early version of this service's methodology.
- Non-economic loss: the platform's design specification asks for three categories here — mental-health impact, biodiversity loss, and cultural-heritage loss — and today only biodiversity has any material behind it. The NESDC-commissioned framework is economic-loss only and doesn't touch this. Stakeholders did genuinely raise non-economic loss as a need; the gap is real but currently unaddressable for two of the three categories. A credible Functional Spec for this service will need to either scope non-economic loss down to biodiversity or name mental-health and cultural-heritage loss as explicitly out of scope for now.

## Service 5 — Climate Design Parameters: ตัวแปรสำหรับการออกแบบทางวิศวกรรมและสถาปัตยกรรม

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Engineers, architects, designers, and infrastructure managers report a specific technical problem: current design standards are based on historical statistics that no longer match today's more volatile climate, leaving buildings and infrastructure at risk of damage and, over time, putting the safety of the people who use them at risk. The demand behind it includes converting future rainfall intensity, peak flow, and maximum and minimum temperature ranges into the specific parameters used in design; supplying risk, hazard, and land-suitability data under future scenarios for sustainable urban planning; updating standard rainfall-intensity curves from decades-old averages to future-projection curves; folding landslide and soil-stability risk into the building-permit process; and assessing risk to ports and marine infrastructure to plan maintenance against future storms and rising seas.

**Value if solved.** The bridge between climate science and design practice — converting future climate projections into parameters that engineers, architects, and other designers can use directly, so buildings, drainage systems, roads, ports, and public spaces are designed for future conditions rather than past ones. Real institutional traction already exists to plug into: the national public-works/town-planning agency is updating its wind-load design standard, already runs a nature-based urban heat-reduction program, and already operates under a 2019 planning law that legally embeds climate resilience into zoning decisions; a working group covering all 76 provinces already produces area-level risk and adaptation plans. It also surfaces a beneficiary who doesn't otherwise appear in this document: local administrative organization staff, who plan and approve local infrastructure without in-house engineering capacity and would be direct users of design guidance produced this way.

**Blocker today.** This work is explicitly being deferred, not attempted in the current phase. Some underlying climate observations and projections exist, but the defensible, locally calibrated design parameters and curves derived from them are not available in DCCE's current holdings. Rainfall-intensity curves are the clearest example, but the same gap applies to runoff coefficients, peak flow, maximum and minimum temperature ranges, wind gust, and the other environmental parameters that engineering and architectural design actually use. This can't be built by repurposing DCCE's existing risk index, which serves the general hazard map, not plot-level design parameters — the two shouldn't be conflated even though they sound adjacent. It also can't be built by DCCE alone: it requires long-term partnership with subject-matter experts across each design discipline it touches, not a one-time data handoff.

**The ask.** A single design-standard update for one system — Bangkok's urban drainage network, say — stating new recommended safety margins for a mid-century scenario, tested against a real question: would the national highways authority actually adopt those figures in its next major procurement round.

### Side Notes

- One possible technical direction: convert global climate projections into the specific runoff and peak-flow figures engineers actually use, with a focus on localized rainfall-intensity curves.
- A "climate allowance" concept: a stated safety margin — such as a percentage add-on to a rainfall design value — that communicates an engineering parameter together with its uncertainty rather than presenting a single number as exact. It connects forward to Service 8's work on communicating uncertainty more broadly.

## Service 6 — Multi-Hazard Impact-Based Early Warning: การเตือนผลกระทบล่วงหน้าแบบหลายภัยพิบัติ

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Field practitioners and business operators frame the core problem the same way: the gap isn't knowing the weather, it's knowing the *impact* — being able to act in time, specifically in places with real economic activity or vulnerable residents. The demand behind it includes managing industrial drought impact on production, forecasting heat-related health risk for outdoor workers and older residents to trigger cooling-shelter activation, estimating the financial cost of business interruption for small businesses, marine ecosystem surveillance such as early coral-bleaching warnings, and setting localized flash-flood thresholds using each area's actual capacity to absorb water. Two pieces of named evidence sharpen this beyond a general statement of need: Thailand's industrial-sector association already runs a systemic water-management program and operates its own carbon-credit trading platform under real international climate-disclosure pressure — a concrete, named private-sector system to interface with, not a hypothetical audience. Separately, the banking sector has flagged real concern about cascading and supply-chain effects and wants numeric, sector-specific pathways for how transition risk actually plays out.

**Value if solved.** Converting observed and forecast weather conditions into the impacts likely to occur to lives, property, and vulnerable groups, then issuing locally relevant warnings that tell recipients what action to take rather than presenting a generic weather statistic. Multi-hazard and cascading-impact analysis strengthens this operational chain by showing how one hazard can trigger further consequences and how several hazards can compound across sectors and systems; it supports the service's central observation-to-impact-to-action function rather than replacing it.

**Blocker today.** This service isn't on the sitemap yet because the product itself hasn't been built — there's genuinely no existing DCCE platform to build it from, so it's a wholly new service, not an underdeveloped one. The only remotely related thing that already exists anywhere on the site is a static diagram explaining how hazards cascade into impacts — useful as an explainer, but not the same as delivering a live, localized alert, and it shouldn't be mistaken for one. One placement assumption is also worth correcting: this is an operational tool that delivers impact, not just hazard information, so it doesn't belong filed under the site's hazard pages the way informational content does — where it should actually sit is still an open question.

**The ask.** A first operational component of the multi-hazard service: one well-designed alert pathway for extreme heat, carrying a specific local instruction for outdoor workers and vulnerable residents rather than a bare temperature figure, tested against a real question: would local authorities and industrial estates actually trigger their response plans based on an alert like this.

### Side Notes

- One idea sketched in prior internal project ideation, not sourced to stakeholder evidence: localized flood-warning thresholds set by each area's soil-absorption capacity, paired with a health layer linking flood depth to waterborne-disease risk to trigger automatic health warnings. Illustrative of the kind of localized, impact-linked alert this service could produce — not itself a confirmed demand signal.

## Service 7 — Adaptation Monitoring & Evaluation: การติดตามและประเมินผลการปรับตัวต่อสภาพภูมิอากาศ

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Agencies implementing adaptation measures, policymakers, and funding bodies need a way to show what is being done, what results it produces, and what is or is not working across projects, places, sectors, and levels of government. Without that evidence, limited budget and resources cannot be redirected confidently, and the country cannot demonstrate concrete progress. Four sourced demand signals span this: tracking the country's technology-readiness status to direct research funding at closing real gaps; adapting local-government performance indicators to align with national adaptation targets; funders who want to finance spatial planning that prevents future disaster impact; and the data Thailand needs to compile for its Global Goal on Adaptation reporting.

**Value if solved.** A shared monitoring and evaluation function that measures the implementation and outcomes of adaptation measures, connects project and local results to national targets, and tracks both progress and where technical capability still falls short. National policy, funding decisions, and international reporting are important uses of that evidence, but they do not define the full boundary of the service.

**Blocker today.** A separately built monitoring platform is already live — collecting manually-entered data from eighteen agencies across the national plan's six sectors, feeding the country's official climate reporting. The genuine opportunity here may be less about building a new tracker from scratch and more about deciding whether and how that existing platform's alignment work gets carried forward, or rebuilt properly, rather than duplicated. This service already has a well-covered home on the website: the link to the country's international adaptation-reporting goal and the national progress tracker are both confirmed and already tied into Thailand's official climate reporting, and a library of successful project case studies is unusually well covered too. Two things remain unconfirmed: whether the site's technology-readiness framework item is actually built inside the existing monitoring platform (currently assumed, not verified — worth a direct check), and tracking the status of individual adaptation projects plus a repository of local and private-sector plans, which currently lives only with DCCE's own programme staff and outside organizations, findable by asking directly rather than through any existing system — that needs regular internal reporting in place before any system could hold it, separate work from this service.

**The ask.** A single scorecard for one adaptation programme that connects activities and spending to measurable outputs and outcomes, then shows how local or provincial results roll up to national targets and international reporting. Its test is whether implementing agencies can use it to improve delivery and whether funding and policy bodies can use the same evidence to redirect support.

### Side Notes

- A documented internal critique of the existing monitoring platform is on record: real "big data" branding sitting on only a couple hundred rows of actual data a year, no automated data pipelines anywhere — the whole thing runs on people typing numbers into forms — and a real risk of being locked into one vendor once the original funding runs out. The verdict on file is blunt: paying for the policy-alignment work, but getting a portal. The platform's real value is judged to be the work of getting agencies to agree on shared indicators, not the software itself.
- One possible direction: align national indicators with the timelines corporate and financial regulators are already imposing for climate disclosure, and add a way to track how mature the country's adaptation technologies actually are.

## Service 8 — Uncertainty Governance: ธรรมาภิบาลความไม่แน่นอน

### Business Case

**Problem & evidence.** Agencies planning under future climate scenarios need consistent rules for identifying, assessing, documenting, interpreting, and communicating uncertainty so that policy and design can remain flexible as conditions change. Deep feedback from the financial sector, national infrastructure planners, and other agencies with real adaptation-project experience shows why this governance is needed: users worry about the credibility and consequences of interpreting high-uncertainty data for decisions with real budget and financial-stability implications. Field evidence backs this precisely: banks confirm they currently treat a probability-based flood map as if it were a certainty map — applying a "hundred-year flood" boundary to loan exposure without accounting for what that actually means statistically — and say directly that their analysts need training and a standardized way of handling uncertainty.

**Value if solved.** A shared national approach to governing climate-data uncertainty: common methods, roles, documentation requirements, interpretation rules, and communication practices that allow users to understand what evidence can and cannot support. This enables more credible budget decisions, realistic financial-risk analysis, more adaptive urban and infrastructure planning, and clearer national climate communication. Legal defensibility and institutional confidence are benefits of sound uncertainty governance, not substitutes for the service itself.

**Blocker today.** This is a methodology-and-governance effort in its own right: the country still needs an agreed taxonomy of uncertainty, clear institutional responsibilities, standard documentation and communication practices, and rules for matching evidence to appropriate uses. Its home on the site is the adaptation-planning pages, but it isn't built there yet, and the reason is readiness rather than structure: uncertainty governance is still unfamiliar to many of the stakeholders this service would serve. The principal blocker is therefore methodological and institutional agreement, not software. One connection worth naming: the finding that DCCE's existing risk index can't be broken down below the province level is itself a concrete instance of the transparency and interpretation problem this service exists to address.

**The ask.** A first uncertainty-governance guideline defining the main uncertainty categories, who must document and review them, how fitness for use is determined, and how uncertainty must be communicated in a decision product. Plain-language examples — why a "hundred-year flood" boundary isn't a certainty line, or why scenario spread exists — would demonstrate the standard rather than stand in for it.

### Side Notes

- Three concrete mechanisms are already sketched, as directions to explore rather than settled design: a tiered certification system separating data ready for budget decisions from data suitable only for strategic planning or still-experimental research, stamped onto the data catalog; an official standard for stating engineering safety margins with their uncertainty attached, approved by the relevant policy body (Service 5's climate-allowance work is the concrete instance of this); and safe-use guidance for probability-based analysis, aimed at preventing overstated risk in financial stress testing. A parallel version of the same idea: a "fit for use" label on every dataset, testing assumptions against multiple future scenarios before relying on them, and a short confidence note attached to every high-stakes output.

## Enabling Pipeline for Service 7 — Thailand's Biennial Transparency Report

### Enabling Use Case

**Problem & evidence.** The institutional pain point here is well documented in the country's own reporting material, not asserted by this project. Thailand's national adaptation monitoring framework exists but is only partially operational, held back by fragmented data systems, inconsistent indicators, and limited coordination between agencies. Disaster mortality data is collected but can't currently be attributed to specific adaptation measures; losses avoided through adaptation aren't yet systematically quantified at the national level. Monitoring findings "cannot yet comprehensively demonstrate concrete progress," and the feedback loop from that monitoring back into policy isn't yet fully built into how government works. Current reporting, in the source material's own words, "mainly reflects activities undertaken and cannot yet adequately answer strategic questions about resilience strengthening." The real institutions involved are named too: the national climate change committee and cabinet for oversight, DCCE for coordination and monitoring, sector focal points and provincial working groups for execution on the ground.

Beyond the reporting material itself, the everyday process compounds this: the lead author of Thailand's Biennial Transparency Report also has to collect, read, and extract useful information from research papers to update the country's current climate-situation picture, plus other insight — on what data the country has and what projects are ongoing — discovered and collected through workshops and interviews. All of that lives in the author's own personal document archive, and only some of it ever makes its way into the published report.

The sharpest everyday version of this problem: a national reporting deadline arrives, and an unstructured data request goes out to dozens of agencies; each one manually searches its own isolated systems and pastes results into a spreadsheet; the results don't line up spatially — one agency uses province names, another uses postal codes — and don't mean the same thing to different people — "drought severity" isn't defined consistently across agencies; compiling the eventual national report takes months of manual work, and the final published numbers can't be traced back to where they came from. Thailand's own reporting material names four structural barriers behind this: gaps in data and knowledge (risk data isn't fine or timely enough, there's no standardized way to assess loss and damage, no standardized outcome indicators, and databases are fragmented with no central point of integration); institutional and coordination gaps (staffing and tooling constraints, policy-to-operations integration that's still incomplete, and inconsistent definitions that make aligning national data to international formats genuinely hard); financial and resource constraints (accessing international climate finance is procedurally complex, and there's no clear way to classify or track spending that's specifically adaptation-related, since it sits embedded inside broader development budgets); and gaps in implementation, reporting, and monitoring (reporting emphasizes activities and budget use rather than actual resilience outcomes, indicator interpretation is inconsistent, and quality-assurance is weak).

**Value if solved.** As an enabling pipeline for Adaptation Monitoring & Evaluation, a repeatable BTR process would turn evidence already needed for international transparency reporting into reusable inputs for domestic learning and policy adjustment. The same reporting material's own forward-looking section expects monitoring systems to perform both functions — while admitting that measuring adaptation outcomes remains genuinely difficult and that data availability and consistency are major constraints. The aspiration and the honest limitation are both on the record, not just the aspiration.

**Blocker today.** No enrichment work exists yet for this specific pipeline. This has to be reconciled against the same existing platform noted for the policy-monitoring service above — its dashboards are already explicitly built to output the charts Thailand's transparency reporting requires and are aligned to the same international framework. That's a real, adjacent, partially-overlapping system, not empty space this pipeline would be filling from nothing. On the positive side, a detailed review of national reporting requirements already carried out for this project has a concrete destination: hundreds of specific requirements have already been checked against the website's planned structure and shown to connect to a meaningful share of its content, so it's clear which parts of the site this pipeline's output is meant to feed, even before the pipeline itself is properly specified. One honest caveat on that same review: it's missing a data dictionary and a clear description of its own workflow, and there are open questions about exactly how its underlying evidence links back to each requirement — worth resolving before treating it as a finished foundation to build on.

**The ask / scope boundary.** The reporting itself won't get a dedicated page — producing the biennial transparency report is DCCE's own mandate, not something the general public has reason to seek out on the platform. The data pipelines built to support it are a different matter — those can and should surface across other pages wherever their outputs are relevant, since the underlying data has value well beyond the report it was assembled for.

### Side Notes

- One proposed solution: a structured data pipeline where raw submissions from each agency pass through data-quality checks, get standardized and spatially aligned to a common geography and a shared glossary of terms, then get aggregated into a single structure that feeds an automated national report — rather than the current spreadsheet-by-spreadsheet approach. Separately, a review of how this would fit into DCCE's broader data model proposes new structures derived directly from the country's own reporting requirements — including a way to track adaptation outcomes rather than just activities completed, and a way to tag budget specifically as adaptation spending. That review is itself explicit that it's raising open design questions, not closed answers.
