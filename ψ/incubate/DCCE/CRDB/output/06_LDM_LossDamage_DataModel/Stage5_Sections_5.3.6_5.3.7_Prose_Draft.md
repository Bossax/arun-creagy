# Draft Report Sections 5.3.6 and 5.3.7 — CRDB Loss and Damage Workflow

## 5.3.6 Draft Minimum Viable Dataset (MVD) and Loss and Damage Reporting Form

The proposed Minimum Viable Dataset (MVD) and draft Loss and Damage Reporting Form are designed to respond to a practical gap between current disaster reporting practice and the broader information structure required for a more systematic loss-and-damage database. The available evidence shows that the Department of Disaster Prevention and Mitigation (DDPM) currently relies on a single urgent disaster reporting form that follows a hierarchical administrative chain from local government to district, then to province, before being consolidated in the central disaster information system. This reporting flow is operationally important because it reflects how data are actually collected and transmitted during an emergency. It also indicates that any proposed reporting form must be compatible with staged submission, central consolidation, and uneven information availability across the first reporting cycles.

At the same time, current DDPM practice does not yet constitute a PDNA-based loss-and-damage assessment system. The existing reporting process is primarily used to support emergency assistance and reimbursement under Ministry of Finance rules, rather than to estimate total disaster damages and losses in a comprehensive analytical sense. Reported monetary figures therefore serve an administrative compensation function and may differ from actual total damages. Evidence also indicates that DDPM does not currently assess aggregate macroeconomic impacts such as GDP-level losses. These constraints are important because they define the realistic lower boundary of the MVD. A workable minimum dataset cannot assume that full valuation data, sector-level production losses, or macroeconomic effects are available at the time of urgent reporting.

For that reason, the proposed MVD starts from actual collectability rather than from an idealized international template. Its minimum core should remain close to the types of information that can plausibly be gathered in near-real time through DDPM-style rapid reporting. This includes event identification, date and location, hazard type, management level, affected people, casualties, housing impacts, utility disruption, urgent needs, and the reporting source. These are the fields most consistently aligned with DDPM’s existing rapid reporting practice and with the initial assessment logic described in the PDNA-related materials. In practical terms, the draft reporting form should therefore preserve a concise event-level reporting core that can be completed quickly and transmitted through the current administrative chain without requiring extensive sectoral verification at the outset.

However, retaining an operationally realistic minimum core does not mean the system should remain confined to reimbursement-oriented reporting. The international standards review indicates that more structured disaster-loss systems typically include standardized event metadata, spatial hierarchy, temporal markers, human impact variables, housing and infrastructure fields, and workflow controls such as status, source, and revision metadata. The same review also shows that meaningful loss-and-damage recording usually requires repeated sector- or asset-level observations rather than a single flat event form. This distinction is critical. A disaster event must be recorded once as the top-level occurrence, but the actual damage and loss information often emerges through multiple observations across sectors, facilities, assets, services, and livelihoods. A single undifferentiated form would not adequately support this structure.

The national signal from the NESDC material reinforces this design direction, but in a bounded way. NESDC highlights the absence of a standardized loss-and-damage database in Thailand and points to the need for more consistent sectoral structure. It also suggests sector groupings such as agriculture, production, housing, public utilities, and cultural heritage, and shows that valuation exercises often combine observed DDPM physical counts with separate unit-cost or unit-value references. This is useful for database design because it confirms that observed counts and valuation inputs should not be treated as the same kind of data. Nevertheless, the NESDC material should inform the database structure rather than dominate the entire reporting concept. Its main contribution here is to confirm that the MVD should be able to connect operational event reporting with later sectoral and valuation enrichment, while keeping those later layers analytically distinct from the rapid intake core.

The most suitable design response is therefore a PDNA-centered staged reporting structure. The PDNA evidence does not support the assumption that all relevant loss-and-damage information can be captured in one moment through one fully completed form. Instead, it distinguishes between preparedness baseline collection before an event, rapid post-event assessment immediately after impact, and more detailed follow-up and recovery-oriented assessment in later phases. The preparedness phase contains baseline reference information on population, infrastructure, economic activities, hydrology, topography, and historical disaster context. The rapid assessment phase focuses on immediately observable disaster characteristics and urgent needs. The later PDNA-oriented phase introduces detailed damage, loss, verification, and recovery planning logic. This phased structure implies that the reporting form should not be conceived as a one-time static questionnaire, but as one logical reporting envelope with staged completeness.

On this basis, the proposed draft form uses one integrated logical model with different levels of completion over time. The first part is an event header that records the disaster occurrence itself. This event-level structure should capture the minimum required-now fields needed for identification, routing, and immediate situational awareness. The second part is a linked set of repeated loss-and-damage observations that can be added as more detailed information becomes available from sectoral teams, follow-up assessments, or later verification. In the technical specification, these two layers are represented by the distinction between [`DISASTER_RECORD`](ψ/incubate/DCCE/CRDB/output/06_LDM_LossDamage_DataModel/Stage3_LossDamage_DataModel_Technical_Specification_Draft.md:37) and [`LOSS_DAMAGE_RECORD`](ψ/incubate/DCCE/CRDB/output/06_LDM_LossDamage_DataModel/Stage3_LossDamage_DataModel_Technical_Specification_Draft.md:43). This structure allows the reporting form to remain manageable during the rapid phase while still preserving a path for later enrichment.

An important implication of this design is that required-now fields must be clearly separated from later-completion fields. Fields that are essential for identifying the event and describing immediate impacts should be mandatory at intake. By contrast, fields that depend on baseline reconstruction, specialist validation, unit-cost references, or economic estimation should be completed only in later phases. This includes repair cost estimates, replacement cost estimates, direct damage estimates, economic loss estimates, and other valuation-heavy sector details. Administrative compensation values should also be stored separately from broader damage and loss estimates, because they are generated for different purposes and cannot be assumed to be analytically equivalent. The same principle applies to macroeconomic indicators, which should remain outside the minimum operational core.

To make this staged model usable in practice, provenance and revision controls must be treated as central design features rather than technical add-ons. Each record should preserve information on the source system, source document type, reporting organization, collection phase, record status, and revision sequence. These fields are necessary because the same event may begin as a rapid operational report, then be updated through follow-up assessment, and later be verified or supplemented with valuation-oriented detail. Without clear provenance and revision logic, the draft form would risk conflating preliminary observations, administrative compensation figures, and later analytical estimates.

In summary, the proposed MVD and reporting form should be understood as a conservative but extensible design. It begins with DDPM’s actual data collection reality, uses international standards and NESDC signals to define a stronger logical structure, and adopts PDNA’s phased assessment logic as the mechanism for staged completeness. The result is not a full national loss-estimation engine, nor an attempt to impose comprehensive PDNA valuation at the first reporting stage. Rather, it is a practical reporting architecture built around an operational event-level core, linked modular loss-and-damage observations, and explicit pathways for later verification and enrichment. This design provides the necessary bridge between current administrative reporting practice and a more standardized loss-and-damage database that can support future analytical use.

## 5.3.7 Methodology for Applying the Draft MVD to Real Events

The draft MVD should be tested through a bounded application methodology that uses real disaster events to assess field practicality, data availability, and the suitability of the staged reporting logic. The purpose of this exercise is not to reconstruct complete disaster histories or to populate a definitive historical database. Instead, the purpose is to determine whether the proposed minimum core can be populated from realistically available event materials, whether later-completion fields are appropriately assigned, and where the main evidence gaps appear when the model is applied to actual reporting situations.

The first step in this methodology is the selection of a small number of representative disaster events. Event selection should be sufficient to expose the MVD to different hazard or reporting conditions, but limited enough to preserve the scope of this exercise as a methodological test rather than a data excavation exercise. Preference should be given to events for which enough documentary material exists to support structured field mapping, such as urgent reports, follow-up summaries, or related sectoral assessment materials, without requiring a full reconstruction of all impacts. The selected set should also be adequate to show whether the required-now fields in the draft model are consistently collectible across different event contexts.

Once events are selected, the available documents for each event should be assembled into an application packet. These materials may include rapid disaster reports, central summaries, follow-up assessment forms, or other event-specific records already available through the relevant reporting chain. The purpose at this stage is not to standardize the source documents themselves, but to establish the evidentiary basis against which the MVD fields will be tested. Each source should be treated as evidence for field mapping, and the application process should preserve the distinction between what is directly observed in the source and what would require later derivation or verification.

The core of the methodology is a structured field-mapping procedure. For each selected event, information from the available documents should first be mapped to the event-level fields of the draft MVD. This includes event identity, hazard type, timing, location, management level, affected population, casualties, housing impact, utility disruption, urgent needs, and source metadata. Where the source material contains more detailed sectoral information, those details should then be mapped to linked loss-and-damage observation records rather than forced into the event header. This preserves the intended separation between the disaster occurrence record and the repeated sector or asset impact records.

Each mapped field should then be scored using the staged completeness categories defined in the draft model: available now, derivable later, or not currently available. A field should be marked as available now when it can be populated directly from the available event materials without additional reconstruction. A field should be marked as derivable later when it cannot yet be entered confidently at the rapid stage but could plausibly be completed through follow-up assessment, baseline linkage, sector verification, or valuation references. A field should be marked as not currently available when the available event materials provide no credible basis for populating it within the current reporting workflow. This scoring logic is essential because it converts the testing exercise from a binary “present or absent” review into a more useful assessment of when and how different kinds of data become available.

During mapping, provenance should be recorded for every populated field or grouped set of fields. At minimum, the source document, reporting organization, collection phase, and any relevant note on interpretation or uncertainty should be captured. Where multiple documents provide conflicting values, the application process should not force premature harmonization. Instead, the discrepancy should be documented so that the testing exercise can reveal where the model will require stronger verification rules or revision control. This is particularly important for figures that may represent administrative compensation values rather than full damage or loss estimates.

The methodology should also distinguish clearly between minimum-core testing and later-module testing. The primary question for the minimum core is whether the required-now fields can be populated with reasonable consistency across the selected events. If they can, the core is operationally realistic. If they cannot, the model may still be too ambitious for actual reporting conditions and should be revised. The question for later-completion fields is different. These fields are not expected to be consistently available at the rapid stage. Instead, the testing exercise should determine whether they are appropriately positioned as follow-up fields and whether the documents and workflows associated with real events provide plausible pathways for later completion.

Interpretation of the testing results should therefore follow a calibration logic. Fields that are consistently available across multiple events support retention as mandatory elements of the MVD core. Fields that recur only in later documents, sectoral assessments, or valuation exercises support their classification as later-completion modules. Fields that remain consistently absent may indicate one of two things: either the field is unrealistic for the current scope and should be deprioritized, or it represents a genuine system-improvement requirement that cannot yet be satisfied under present reporting practice. In either case, the testing exercise should inform refinement of field priority, provenance rules, and phase assignments, rather than being treated as evidence that the historical record itself has been completed.

In methodological terms, this approach allows the draft MVD to be applied to real events without breaking the scope boundary of the assignment. It tests the model against actual reporting conditions, shows whether the proposed separation between rapid core fields and later enrichment fields is credible, and reveals where the main implementation frictions are likely to occur. Most importantly, it keeps the exercise focused on validating the design of the dataset and reporting form. The outcome of the testing process should therefore be a refined view of which fields belong in the minimum operational core, which should remain staged for later completion, and which may require future institutional or data-system development before they can be used reliably.
