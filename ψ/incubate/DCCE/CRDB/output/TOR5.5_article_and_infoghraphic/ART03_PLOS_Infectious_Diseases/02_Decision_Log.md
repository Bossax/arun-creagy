## 📖 Executive Summary & Scientific Narrative

This study models the long-term excess risks of seven major infectious diseases in Thailand—dengue, influenza, malaria, pneumonia, leptospirosis, melioidosis, and Japanese encephalitis (JEV)—under future extreme weather events. Using Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) and CMIP6 climate scenarios (SSP126 to SSP585), the research maps projected disease risks through the end of the century at a highly granular, province-by-province level.

The core takeaway is that climate change does not uniformly amplify all infectious diseases across the country. Instead, the projected risks are highly heterogeneous. While diseases like pneumonia and melioidosis will see increased burdens under high-emission scenarios (SSP585), other diseases like dengue and influenza exhibit complex, localized trajectories—sometimes declining nationally but surging sharply in specific regions (like Northern and Central Thailand) during specific decades. This spatial and temporal variability stresses the need for "precision public health" and localized adaptation strategies rather than blanket national policies.

## 🔬 Key Scientific & Policy Insights (How Thailand is Impacted)

### 1. The Complex, Localized Trajectory of Dengue and Influenza
*   **The Science**: Contrary to the simplistic assumption that a warmer climate means more disease everywhere, ==dengue is projected to decline nationwide by 24.9% (2061-2080) under certain models==. However, under the moderate SSP245 scenario, extreme heat drives a localized surge in dengue risk in Northern and Central Thailand between 2021 and 2060. Similarly, influenza sees localized spikes, such as a projected 36.8% risk increase in Nakhon Ratchasima (2021-2040).
* %% highlight uncertainty in the disease dispersion and evolution %%
*   **The Cause**: Changes in extreme heat and extreme dry weather alter vector breeding cycles, viral incubation periods, and human behavioral patterns in geographically specific ways that do not average out neatly at the national level.
*   **Hazards Implication**: Broad national early-warning systems are less effective than localized, province-specific public health alerts targeting vulnerable regions during high-risk decades.

### 2. The High-Emissions Penalty: Pneumonia and Melioidosis
*   **The Science**: Under the most severe climate scenario (SSP585), the excess risk of pneumonia and melioidosis is expected to increase significantly during periods of extreme weather.
*   **The Cause**: Severe environmental stress—particularly extreme wet events and intense heat—compounds respiratory vulnerabilities in the population and increases exposure to the soil- and water-borne bacteria responsible for melioidosis.
*   **Hazards Implication**: If global emissions are not curbed, Thailand's healthcare infrastructure must prepare for heightened burdens of these specific diseases, requiring expanded diagnostic, hospital, and treatment capacities.

### 3. Declining Risks and Outliers: Leptospirosis, JEV, and Malaria
*   **The Science**: Interestingly, leptospirosis risk is projected to decline across all time periods and scenarios during extreme weather. JEV risk also generally decreases (except under SSP585). In contrast, malaria risk tends to increase during extreme weather across most scenarios.
*   **The Cause**: Changing precipitation patterns (SPI) and rising extreme heat may push certain pathogen/vector habitats (like those for leptospirosis) outside their viability thresholds, while expanding the environmental suitability for others (like malaria).
*   **Hazards Implication**: Climate adaptation in public health requires dynamic resource reallocation. Budgets and vector-control efforts can be shifted away from areas where environmental suitability is shrinking, and redirected toward newly emerging hotspots.

## Additional Information

The predictions of disease impacts are uncertain due to several methodological constraints, limitations in climate data, and unaccounted variables in the study's models:

**Climate Data Limitations**

- **Temporal Disaggregation:** Future monthly temperature projections were broken down into daily estimates to determine extreme heat days. This approach may miss true daily variability, meaning the projected frequency of extreme heat events could differ from actual future conditions.
- **Smoothing of Extremes:** The climate projections use 20-year monthly averages, which smooths out year-to-year weather extremes. Because of this, extreme precipitation or drought (SPI) values appeared less frequently in the projections, potentially underestimating the true impact of extreme wet or dry weather on disease risk.
- **Lack of Bias Correction:** The researchers did not apply additional bias correction to the Global Climate Model (GCM) inputs, meaning future weather extremes may have been over-estimated or under-estimated.

**Unaccounted Environmental and Human Factors**

- **Missing Ecological Drivers:** The models did not account for variables that strongly drive disease transmission, such as future changes in land use, or proximity to amplifying hosts (which is particularly important for diseases like Japanese Encephalitis and malaria).
- **Demographic and Immunity Dynamics:** The models assumed the population would remain at historical levels because incorporating future population growth resulted in implausibly large and uncertain estimates. The models also could not incorporate changes in population immunity dynamics or future public health interventions.

**Statistical and Surveillance Constraints**

- **Reporting Rates:** The models did not account for potential changes in disease reporting rates over time. This creates a risk that some projected fluctuations in cases might reflect changes in reporting practices rather than true epidemiological shifts.
- **Model Structure Issues:** Researchers were unable to include an autocorrelation structure in their statistical model due to convergence issues caused by the combination of lagged variables and overdispersed case count data.



###  uncertainties in the predictions 
while many are currently inherited from modeling choices and data availability, the researchers emphasize that several of these uncertainties are **reducible** in future studies as data quality and methodologies improve.

Here is how these uncertainties break down:

**Reducible with Better Data and Future Research**

- **Climate Data Formats:** The issues with temporal disaggregation and the smoothing of extremes are inherited from the format of current Global Climate Model (GCM) outputs (e.g., using 20-year monthly averages). The authors note that this is reducible: future research should focus on developing GCM outputs that provide continuous monthly data, which would allow for a more accurate representation of true interannual variability and extreme events.
- **Missing Variables:** The inability to account for changes in land use, proximity to amplifying hosts, population immunity dynamics, and future public health interventions is due to a lack of projected future data for these specific variables. The authors explicitly state that their predictions can be refined "in the future when such data is available".

**Inherited from Modeling Choices and Constraints**

- **Statistical Constraints:** The absence of an autocorrelation structure is inherited directly from computational limitations. The researchers were unable to include it because the combination of lagged covariates and overdispersed case count data caused convergence issues in their statistical models.
- **Population Projections:** The models assumed the population would remain at historical levels because the modeling choices could not handle population projections that exceeded current observations; attempting to incorporate future population growth led to implausibly large and highly uncertain estimates.
- **Bias Correction:** The lack of bias correction applied to the GCM inputs was a methodological choice made during the study, which leaves the possibility that future weather extremes were under- or over-estimated.

**Inherited from Surveillance Data Quality**

- **Reporting Practices:** The risk that case fluctuations might reflect changes in reporting practices rather than true disease spread is an inherent flaw in real-world surveillance data. However, the authors suggest this uncertainty might be minor, as short-term disruptions to reporting likely have a minimal impact on the long-term trends observed over the nearly two-decade study period.

### recommendations
The paper recommends integrating climate resilience into local public health responses, emphasizing that interventions must be **targeted at a finer administrative level** due to the heterogeneous (varied) changes in disease risk across different provinces.

Specifically, to tackle the evolving impacts of diseases under the most likely climate scenario (SSP245), the authors provide the following actionable recommendations:

- **For Dengue:** High-risk provinces in northern and central Thailand—such as Bangkok, Chiang Rai, Ratchaburi, Nakhon Ratchasima, and Si Sa Ket—should prioritize **vector control and hospital preparedness** during heatwaves that are accompanied by normal rainfall.
- **For Influenza:** High-risk provinces—including Nakhon Ratchasima, Buri Ram, Ubon Ratchathani, and Chiang Mai—need to strengthen **surveillance, vaccination campaigns, and healthcare surge capacity** during heatwaves coupled with extended dry periods, as these conditions are linked to higher transmission risks.
- **Vigilance and Preparedness:** Even for infectious diseases where risk is projected to decrease during extreme weather, the authors stress the need to remain vigilant against the broader impacts of extreme heat, drought, and heavy rainfall on human health. The operational triggers identified in the study should inform region-specific preparedness and response planning.
- **Future Research and Adaptation:** The researchers recommend using their methodological framework to evaluate disease risks in other climatic zones (e.g., temperate climates) and in communities with different health infrastructures and economic resilience. This will help inform broader, region-specific adaptation strategies globally.
