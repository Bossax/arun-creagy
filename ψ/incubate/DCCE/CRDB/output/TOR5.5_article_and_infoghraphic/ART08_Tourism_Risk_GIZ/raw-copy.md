Response:
The "Climate Change Risk Assessment for Thailand's Tourism Sector" document details distinct climate risks, specific hot spots, and vulnerability metrics across different regions of Thailand.

**Macro-Regional Climate Risks and Indicators**

- **North and Northeast Thailand:**
    - **Temperature & Heat:** These regions are projected to experience relatively greater rates of warming (1.7 to 3.4°C by late-century) compared to coastal areas. A key metric is the **shortening of the "cold period"** (days where the temperature is <16°C), which is projected to decrease from the current 2–2.5 months to just 1–2.5 months by mid-century. This threatens the destination competitiveness of provinces like Chiang Rai, which currently relies on its cool winter climate (averaging 21°C daytime and 8°C nighttime) to attract tourists.
    - **Precipitation & Flooding:** Extreme rainfall events are becoming more likely as rain falls in fewer, more intense bursts, leading to a high risk of landslides and extensive flooding.
    - **Air Pollution:** Hotter temperatures exacerbate severe air quality issues (e.g., high PM10 levels) caused by agricultural "slash and burn" practices in nine northern provinces, which negatively impacts tourist demand and safety.
- **Central Thailand (including Bangkok):**
    - **Flooding:** The low-lying Chao Phraya River Basin is highly exposed to extreme rain and flooding, which has previously damaged ancient archaeological remains in Ayutthaya.
    - **Bangkok Specifics:** Often referred to as the "Venice of the East" due to its low elevation (1.5 m above sea level) and reduced drainage from lost canals, Bangkok faces severe risks. Projections indicate that by mid-century, **1-in-10 and 1-in-30-year flood events will inundate 30% more of the city** with "nuisance flooding".
- **South Thailand & Coastal Destinations:**
    - **Sea Level Rise (SLR) & Coastal Erosion:** The Gulf of Thailand has experienced SLR of **3 to 5 mm per year** (1993–2008), a rate 3 to 5 times the global average. National projections estimate a severe beach loss rate between **45.8% (low emission scenario) and 71.8% (high emission scenario)** by the end of the century, meaning that between 8 and 23 of Thailand's 51 sandy beach zones could disappear entirely.
    - **Marine Ecosystems:** These areas face a high risk of coral bleaching and ocean acidification due to rising sea surface temperatures.
    - **Storms:** There is an anticipated increase in higher intensity tropical storms (Categories 4 and 5), threatening coastal infrastructure.

**Specific Hot Spots and Localized Risks**

- **Koh Phi Phi (Andaman Coast):** This 12.25 km² island is highly exposed due to its unusual topography, where nearly all tourism infrastructure is concentrated on a narrow, flat sandy strip. This extreme exposure was demonstrated during the 2004 Tsunami, where **6-meter waves destroyed roughly 70% of the island's guest rooms**. Despite this, development has doubled to accommodate an estimated 10,000 visitors per day, drastically increasing its exposure to climate hazards and straining limited freshwater reserves.
- **Koh Tao (Gulf of Thailand):** Conversely, Koh Tao (21 km²) features granitic hills up to 379m above sea level, making its infrastructure slightly less exposed to direct coastal erosion. However, because its economy heavily relies on dive tourism, it is intensely vulnerable to marine hazards like coral bleaching and ocean acidification. Like Koh Phi Phi, it is highly exposed to drought due to limited freshwater reserves.
- **Koh Samui:** Extreme weather has caused major disruptions here. A 2016 drought led to severe water shortages and government-ordered water rationing. In January 2019, Tropical Storm Pabuk forced the evacuation of **30,000 to 50,000 tourists** from Koh Samui, Koh Tao, and Koh Phangan during the peak holiday season.
- **Samut Prakan:** In the Ban Khun Samut Chin community, coastal erosion and storm surges have directly damaged historical buildings (temples). In Bang Kra Jao, saltwater intrusion has damaged agricultural areas vital to agri-ecotourism, such as coconut palm sugar factories.
