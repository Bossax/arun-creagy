## 📖 Executive Summary & Scientific Narrative

This study presents a national-level **Climate Change Risk Assessment (CCRA)** for Thailand's tourism sector, aligned with the **IPCC AR5 framework** (Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability). It evaluates how past climate variability and future projections threaten key "Values at Risk" (economic revenue, visitor safety, comfort, asset protection, and natural resources). 

Tourism is a macro-economic driver for Thailand, representing over **15% of national GDP**. However, because **88% of arrivals are leisure holidaymakers** (who are highly flexible and sensitive to conditions), the country's economic dependence on tourism represents a significant climate exposure. The study combines global index ratings (where Thailand sits in the highest risk quintile) with localized stakeholder workshops and a detailed survey of **193 accommodation businesses** on Koh Tao and Koh Phi Phi to map climate impacts from the national scale down to specific island destinations.

## 🔬 Key Scientific Insights (How Thailand's Tourism is Impacted)

### 1. Dual Island Vulnerability Profiles (Koh Phi Phi vs. Koh Tao)
*   **The Science**: Climate exposure is heavily shaped by local topography and resource availability. 
*   **Koh Phi Phi (Physical Exposure)**: A 12.25 km² island where nearly all resort infrastructure is concentrated on a low, narrow sandy isthmus. This was demonstrated during the 2004 Tsunami when 6-meter waves destroyed 70% of guest rooms. Despite this risk, infrastructure has doubled to support up to 10,000 visitors daily, vastly increasing physical exposure to storm surges and sea-level rise.
*   **Koh Tao (Resource & Economic Exposure)**: A 21 km² granitic island rising to 379 meters. While its elevated topography shields hotels from direct coastal flooding, its economy is intensely vulnerable due to a near-total dependence on marine dive tourism (highly exposed to coral bleaching and ocean acidification) and chronic freshwater shortages.

### 2. Disappearing Beaches & Accelerated Sea Level Rise
*   **The Science**: Sea level rise (SLR) in the Gulf of Thailand was recorded at **3 to 5 mm per year** (1993–2008), which is 3 to 5 times the global average rate.
*   **The Impact**: By 2100, Thailand's sandy coastlines are projected to lose between **45.8% (low-emission, RCP2.6)** and **71.8% (high-emission, RCP8.5)** of their beach areas. This threat implies that between **8 and 23 of the country's 51 sandy beach zones could disappear completely**, eroding the primary asset of Thailand's coastal tourism economy.

### 3. Immediate Stresses on Island Accommodations (The 193-Hotel Survey)
*   **The Science**: The survey of 193 accommodation suppliers across Koh Tao and Koh Phi Phi shows that climate change is already a current business constraint, not a distant threat.
*   **Key Business Impacts**:
    *   **81.3%** report negative impacts from a perceived reduction of natural beauty.
    *   **78.7%** report impacts from changing weather seasons.
    *   **75.6%** have suffered storm or wind damage to their premises.
    *   **69.9%** experience chronic freshwater shortages.
    *   **60.6%** suffer from floods/extreme rain events.
    *   **58.6%** report operational/demand impacts from coral bleaching.

### 4. Northern/Central Mainland Extremes: Access and Activity Blockages
*   **Extreme Heat (>42°C)**: Historical destinations in Sukhothai, Tak, and Phitsanulok face temperatures exceeding 42°C, making daytime outdoor activities uncomfortable for visitors and degrading destination competitiveness.
*   **Decline in Cold Period**: Northern provinces face a projected decline in the "cold period" (days <16°C) from 2–2.5 months to just 1–2.5 months by mid-century, directly impacting winter peak tourism in areas like Chiang Rai.
*   **Access Blockages (Landslides)**: Intense rainfall is triggering landslides that block roads in Mae Hong Son, Uttaradit, and Phetchabun, completely cutting off tourist access. A landslide at Phu Chi Fa (Chiang Rai) damaged resort infrastructure and decreased visitor confidence.

### 1. Introduction: The Network Economy of Thai Tourism

- **System Characteristics:** Define tourism as a network economy—interdependent nodes of transport, accommodation, and ecosystems.
    
- **Natural Capital Dependency:** The "Product" is the environment. Tourists seek relaxation in nature, not artificial environments. This creates a reliance on **natural capital** rather than synthetic infrastructure.
    

### 2. The Twin Pathways of Climate Hardship

- **Pathway 1: The Direct Human Impact (Comfort & Safety)**
    
    - _The Driver:_ Extreme heat and environmental degradation (e.g., bleached coral reefs).
        
    - _The Impact:_ These don't just "harm nature"—they degrade the tourist experience. When the "natural" product becomes uncomfortable (too hot) or unattractive (dead coral), the willingness to travel drops. Tourists are people, and when their comfort or safety is compromised, the demand shifts.
        
- **Pathway 2: The Eco-Economic Cascade (The "Hidden" Cost)**
    
    - _The Driver:_ Climate impacts on ecosystem health (e.g., erosion, water scarcity).
        
    - _The Impact:_ Economic disruption to local communities. Because the industry relies on nature, ecosystem decline directly reduces the ability of local people to earn a living. The complexity lies in the fact that we struggle to model these "domino effects" from a climate event to a local resident's bank account.
        

### 3. Case Studies: Where Economy Meets Vulnerability

- **Koh Phi Phi: Geography as an Economic Trap**
    
    - _The Issue:_ The island's narrow, low-lying sandy strip is a prime example of high-value tourism development in an extremely high-risk zone.
        
    - _The Driver:_ Economic forces drive development on land that is physically vulnerable to sea-level rise and storm surges, creating a "disaster-in-waiting" scenario where economic profit ignores geological reality.
        
- **Koh Tao: The Water Scarcity Conflict**
    
    - _The Issue:_ Chronic water shortages.
        
    - _The Driver:_ Infrastructure stress caused by sheer tourist volume. The number of visitors frequently outpaces the island's natural water capacity and waste management systems, turning a "nature destination" into a system under immense environmental stress.
        

### 4. Stakeholder Sentiment: Bridging the Gap

- **Local Perception (General Summary):** Based on research and surveys (e.g., in places like Phi Phi), there is a distinct recognition among local operators that climate change is a real and present threat to their business.
    
- **The Adaptation Paradox:** While businesses are aware of the risks and often implement _ad-hoc_ measures (like private water treatment or insurance), there is a frustration that these are just "band-aids." The underlying structural issues—like mass-tourism pressure and lack of unified sustainability planning—remain largely unaddressed, leaving the community vulnerable to the next major shock.

### Q: what is the background of this report and the research project?
This report is an output of the **"Mainstreaming Climate Change Adaptation into the Tourism Sector in Thailand"** initiative, which is part of a broader collaboration between the **Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ)** and Thailand's **Office of Natural Resources and Environmental Policy and Planning (ONEP)**. The research is financed by the International Climate Initiative (IKI) of the German Federal Ministry for the Environment, Nature Conservation and Nuclear Safety (BMU).

The project's background is rooted in the implementation of the **"Risk based National Adaptation Plan" (Risk-NAP)**. During the creation of the 2018 draft National Adaptation Plan (NAP), a nation-wide vulnerability analysis explicitly identified **tourism as one of six priority sectors** for climate change adaptation efforts in Thailand.

Because the tourism sector is vital to the country's economy, this specific Climate Change Risk Assessment (CCRA) was commissioned to achieve the following objectives:

- To systematically **identify potential and existing climate change-related risks** facing the Thai tourism sector.
- To understand the **availability of data and identify information gaps** regarding the quantification of these climate risks.
- To provide recommendations and next steps for the Thai tourism sector to properly address and manage these risks.

Ultimately, the research was designed to strengthen Thailand's overall adaptive capacity. The findings from this assessment are intended to **feed directly into Thailand’s broader climate change response strategies**, supporting the ongoing implementation of the NAP by helping decision-makers integrate climate risks into regional planning, sectoral plans, and policy instruments.

### Q: Does the source metion the solutions? in what sense
The source extensively discusses solutions, though they are primarily framed in the context of **"adaptation measures," "risk management strategies," and "policy recommendations"**.

In a broad sense, the document treats these solutions not as quick, isolated fixes, but as integral parts of **long-term planning, "good practice," and maintaining business competitiveness**. The solutions focus heavily on building the "adaptive capacity" of Thailand's tourism sector by shifting away from volume-driven tourism toward resilient, value-driven sustainability.

The solutions are discussed in two main senses: high-level strategic/policy recommendations and practical, on-the-ground actions.

#### 1. Strategic and Policy Solutions

The report outlines 10 key recommendations for tourism decision-makers to implement at a macro level. These strategic solutions include:

- **Improving Sustainability:** Rapidly improving the overall sustainability of tourism to reduce existing pressure on social and environmental systems.
- **Fostering Partnerships:** Working across government agencies to eliminate unsustainable practices and forming partnerships (between the public sector, private sector, and NGOs) to create win-win opportunities for climate risk management.
- **Financing Adaptation:** Exploring new finance mechanisms to fund climate change adaptation and investing in assessing the cost-benefit "business case" for these measures.
- **Data and Monitoring:** Investing in robust tourism statistics and directly surveying tourists regarding their perceptions of safety and comfort (e.g., regarding air pollution or heat) to determine critical thresholds where climate impacts destination competitiveness.
- **Localized Planning:** Developing detailed, destination-based risk assessments so that local planning and policy can incorporate projected changes in climate risk.

#### 2. Practical and "No-Regret" Solutions

The source also emphasizes that addressing non-climate risks is an important **"no-regret measure"**—meaning these actions provide immediate, tangible benefits to the environment and economy while simultaneously reducing vulnerability to future climate risks. Specific practical solutions mentioned to address key climate hazards include:

- **For flooding and landslides:** Improving land use management, such as limiting deforestation and explicitly considering floodplains during infrastructure development.
- **For extreme heat:** Working to reduce air pollution to moderate the compounded negative impacts of heatwaves on visitor health and comfort.
- **For coral bleaching:** Minimizing other environmental stressors, such as water pollution, to increase the resilience of marine ecosystems and give them a better chance to recover from warming oceans.
- **For water shortages:** Maximizing resource efficiency and eliminating water waste across tourism businesses.
- **For coastal erosion and sea-level rise:** Carefully managing and restricting the development of infrastructure in vulnerable coastal zones.
- **For rising energy costs:** Mainstreaming building designs (such as traditional Thai architecture) that provide better natural ventilation and cooling, thereby reducing reliance on air conditioning and lowering operational expenses.

Overall, the solutions are presented as a holistic, multi-dimensional effort that requires collaboration, financial investment, and a fundamental shift toward sustainable practices.
