## 📖 Executive Summary & Scientific Narrative

This study analyzes the individual-level determinants and cascading health outcomes of **urban heat stress** in the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration (BMA) and Pathumthani Province. Using a quota-sampled survey of **505 respondents** across four density zones, the research assesses how socioeconomic conditions, built-environment parameters, and health behaviors shape perceived heat stress across four daily activity domains: **sleeping, daily travel, work, and exercise**. It then examines how this heat stress cascades into three health and well-being outcomes: **life satisfaction (well-being), energy levels (physical health), and emotional problems (mental health)**.

The core takeaway is that urban heat stress is not merely a natural hazard but a socially and environmentally structured challenge. Individual socioeconomic vulnerabilities (low income, gender, education) and built-environment configurations (lack of workplace/home air-conditioning, high population density, and high Urban Heat Island intensity) compound heat stress, subsequently degrading physical health, mental health, and life satisfaction.

## 🔬 Key Scientific Insights (How Bangkok is Impacted)

### 1. The Low-Income Commuter and Worker Penalty
*   **The Science**: The study reveals a severe socioeconomic disparity in heat vulnerability. The lowest income group (<5,000 Baht/month) experiences massive odds of heat stress during daily travel (**OR = 5.333**) and work (**OR = 4.946**). 
*   **The Cause**: Low-income residents spend significantly more time outdoors, are exposed to intense heat during traffic congestion, and rely on non-air-conditioned public buses (the most affordable transport). Furthermore, they often work in settings without workplace air-conditioning (**OR = 3.391**).
*   **Hazards Implication**: Heat stress acts as a regressive penalty on the urban poor, directly compromising their occupational safety and economic productivity.

### 2. The Air-Conditioning Paradox
*   **The Science**: Standard individual adaptation relies heavily on installing air-conditioning (AC). However, the study shows AC has limited overall effectiveness. Having AC at home **only** mitigates heat stress during sleep (**OR = 2.154** if lacking) and has no protective effect during daily travel or work.
*   **The Feedback Loop**: Relying on AC as a primary adaptation strategy is counter-productive. Increased AC usage drives up domestic energy consumption and expels anthropogenic heat, which directly **exacerbates the Urban Heat Island (UHI) intensity** of the surrounding city.

### 3. Green Spaces as Protective Urban Shields
*   **The Science**: Living close to a park (<1 km) is an exceptionally strong protective factor, significantly reducing the odds of heat stress during sleep (**OR = 0.444**) and exercise (**OR = 0.363**). 
*   **Hazards Implication**: Urban green spaces act as physical cool islands that buffer local temperatures, validating green infrastructure planning (like rooftop gardens and public parks) over private AC reliance.

### 4. Cascading Health and Mental Toll
*   **The Science**: Heat stress is not just physical discomfort; it significantly increases the likelihood of emotional problems (mental health) and poor life satisfaction (well-being) across all four domains. The **strongest link to poor life satisfaction was found in the exercise domain (OR = 2.470)**, indicating that the inability to maintain active, healthy lifestyles in intense heat severely degrades urban well-being.
*   **Physical Health Limit**: Interestingly, physical energy depletion is strongly tied to travel, work, and exercise heat stress, but *not* sleep-related heat stress, suggesting that people's bodies still recover physical energy during sleep even if they experience subjective discomfort.
