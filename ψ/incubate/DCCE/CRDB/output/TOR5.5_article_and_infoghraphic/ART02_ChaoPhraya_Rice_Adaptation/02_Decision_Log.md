## 📖 Executive Summary & Scientific Narrative

This study investigates the future climate change impacts on dry-season rice production and irrigation water demand in the lower Chao Phraya River Basin. Utilizing the DSSAT-CERES-Rice model alongside CMIP6 climate projections (SSP126, SSP245, SSP585) across the early, mid, and late 21st century, the research moves beyond simple impact assessment by proactively testing two types of adaptation: management-based strategies and virtual genetic crop traits.

The core takeaway is that sustainable rice production in Thailand's agricultural heartland requires a highly tailored approach. The paper examines the popular RD57 cultivar under various cultivation methods (e.g., direct seeding, transplanting, continuous flooding, and alternate wetting and drying) and highlights how adjusting planting dates, fertilizer application, and irrigation thresholds—or shifting to heat-tolerant and water-efficient virtual cultivars—can mitigate the compounding pressures of rising temperatures and shifting rainfall patterns.

## 🔬 Key Scientific & Policy Insights (How the Chao Phraya Basin is Impacted)

### 1. Management-Based Adaptation as a First Line of Defense
*   **The Science**: The study evaluates structural changes in farm management, specifically analyzing how shifts in planting dates, fertilizer application timing and dosage, and irrigation water thresholds affect the final rice yield and water use.
*   **The Cause**: As average minimum and maximum temperatures rise and rainfall patterns become erratic, traditional cropping calendars fall out of sync with optimal growing conditions.
*   **Hazards Implication**: Adjusting management practices represents a highly accessible, low-capital adaptation strategy for local farmers. However, optimizing for yield without considering irrigation thresholds risks depleting water reserves in the Chao Phraya basin.

### 2. The Potential of Genetic Traits (Virtual Cultivars)
*   **The Science**: Beyond behavioral changes, the model tests "virtual cultivars" featuring hypothetical genetic traits designed for climate stress (e.g., enhanced thermal tolerance, better root water uptake). 
*   **The Cause**: Management changes alone may eventually hit a hard ceiling under the high-emissions SSP585 scenario by mid-to-late century, requiring structural biological resilience against intense heat and drought.
*   **Hazards Implication**: This provides a clear roadmap for national agricultural research (like the Rice Department), prioritizing the breeding of specific traits (heat tolerance and water efficiency) over generic high-yield varieties.

### 3. Water-Saving Cultivation Techniques
*   **The Science**: The research explicitly compares continuous flooding (CF) against alternate wetting and drying (AWD) at different thresholds (15cm and 30cm), alongside different seeding methods.
*   **The Cause**: Dry-season rice is exceptionally water-intensive. Future climate volatility threatens the water supply reliability from major dams into the Chao Phraya irrigation network.
*   **Hazards Implication**: Transitioning to AWD is critical not just for saving water, but potentially for maintaining yields when water allocations are inevitably reduced during severe drought years.
