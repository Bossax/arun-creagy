Here are the key qualitative and policy findings from the study on coastal erosion and flood coping mechanisms in Southern Thailand:

**1. Key differences in vulnerability between Krabi and Nakhon Si Thammarat**

- **Krabi (Andaman Sea):** Vulnerability is heavily driven by **anthropogenic pressures from eco-tourism**, with approximately 100 boats operating daily,. The frequent boat traffic generates continuous water waves that cause severe coastal erosion and boat fuel destroys aquatic life,. Economic reliance on tourism also compromises preparedness; for instance, **tsunami early warning systems were historically not tested during tourist seasons** for fear of scaring visitors away. Furthermore, local fishermen face docking restrictions in areas reserved for tourists, which penalizes the local community.
- **Nakhon Si Thammarat (Gulf of Thailand):** Vulnerability is heavily tied to **illegal fishing practices and storms**. Communities formed conservation groups to conduct night patrols against illegal fishermen using dragging tools that destroy habitats, utilizing bamboo barriers to keep larger boats out. The region also experienced severe damage from tropical storms, such as Tropical Storm Pabuk (2019), which was exacerbated by mudslides caused by illegal forestry activities,.
- **Corruption and Infrastructure:** In Nakhon Si Thammarat, corruption among contractors was reported to be rampant, resulting in **substandard hard construction** of coastal defenses that vary in material and height, leaving communities exposed.

**2. Local coping mechanisms and challenges (Hard vs. Soft Defenses)**

- **Soft Defenses (Natural Mechanisms):** Communities use forest plantations, coconut leaves to collect clay, sea grass, and bamboo to protect shorelines and fish habitats,,.
    - _Challenges:_ Bamboo was found to increase sand on the shoreline but **offered little protection against actual coastal erosion** and caused injuries. Sea grass helps slow down boat waves but is ineffective against storm waves. Additionally, conservation budgets for these projects are severely limited. Ecologically, certain species like the red mangrove (_Rhizophora mucronata_) were found to produce large amounts of wastewater and are recommended to be avoided.
- **Hard Defenses:** The government prefers hard construction, utilizing concrete block ways, 45-degree concrete blocks, and sandbags to protect infrastructure, ports, and tourist beaches,.
    - _Challenges:_ Communities oppose hard defenses because **if erosion shifts to unprotected areas, the resulting damage becomes far too expensive**. Furthermore, artificial coral reefs made from concrete and coal ash sparked community protests and legal battles because the **coal ash contained mercury, which contaminated local fish**,.

**3. Socio-economic, legal, and institutional barriers**

- **Land Licenses and Taxation Penalties:** Most coastal residents lack title deeds because the beaches fall under the Marine Office's jurisdiction. Consequently, **locals have no incentive to build self-protection structures against boat waves, as doing so attracts taxation penalties** from the Marine Office.
- **Compensation Inequities:** Following disasters, financial compensation was distributed unevenly (ranging from nothing to 11,000 THB), with some absentee landlords in Bangkok receiving funds while vulnerable locals got nothing. Furthermore, because **fishermen lacked land licenses and a formal government database**, they were excluded from agricultural compensation schemes.
- **Evacuation Reluctance:** Human behavior limits emergency response; boat owners often refuse to evacuate to protect their families and valuables, while disabled individuals are frequently reluctant to leave their homes.
- **Siloed Government Agencies:** Different government agencies operate under distinct regulations depending on their funding source, causing delayed responses and disjointed disaster management.

**4. Standard Operating Procedures, EOCs, and Early Warning Systems**

- **SOPs and EOCs:** Strictly speaking, there are established guidelines rather than formal SOPs at the central/regional levels. **Emergency Operation Centers (EOCs) are successfully set up** in each province, with the Ministry of Interior taking a leadership role to coordinate information sharing across organizations. An Incident Command System (ICS) operates effectively using a top-down approach to issue evacuation orders.
- **Early Warning Systems:** Early warning systems exist but have flaws. Warning posters are strategically placed, but **tourists often understand them better than the local Thai residents**. News is mainly disseminated via radio, TV, and social media (Facebook, LINE), but not all locals have compatible mobile devices.
- **Surveillance Functionality:** Surveillance capabilities vary by region. In Krabi, NGOs lacked their own disaster surveillance systems and relied purely on external info. Conversely, in Nakhon Si Thammarat, **a strong surveillance network run by a drug control center** successfully used mobile equipment and scuba suits to assist during floods. The health sector also successfully uses Emergency Medical Services (EMS) linked with community leaders to monitor water levels locally.

**1. Qualitative Research Methods, Validation, and Content Analysis Techniques**

- **Methods:** The study utilized **focus group discussions (FGDs)** using a semi-structured guide that covered the impacts of coastal hazards, control measures, mitigation measures, and emergency preparedness and response.
- **Content Analysis:** The researchers applied a **summative content analysis** approach to explore specific aspects and gain insights into the usage of certain words to better interpret the underlying context of the FGD findings,.
- **Validation:** The authors noted a methodological challenge regarding validation, stating that "it is challenging to demonstrate data rigidity since individual viewpoints frequently served as the basis of data for the study",. (The study's protocol was ethically validated and approved by the Mahidol University Central Institutional Review Board).

**2. Specific Limitations and Data Gaps**

- **Reliance on Perspectives/Recollections:** The study is fundamentally based on the "long-term recollections and experiences of the communities". The authors explicitly identify this as a limitation, noting that the qualitative strategy "solely considers perspectives; as a result, it does not give a statistical representation",.
- **Data and Budget Gaps:** Local administrations are noted to be "limited in terms of sufficient data and budget" when trying to address the huge needs of vulnerable populations facing hydrometeorological disasters.
- **Lack of Title Deeds:** A major socio-legal limitation identified is that the majority of coastal residents do not have land licenses (title deeds) because the land falls under the Marine Office's jurisdiction. Consequently, locals have no incentive to construct protective structures against waves, as doing so requires permission and attracts taxation penalties from the Marine Office.

**3. Author Warnings and Long-Term Planning Recommendations**

- **Ecological Warning:** The authors explicitly warn that the Royal Forestry Department should "desist from planting a certain type of mangrove forest (_Rhizophora mucronate_, or red mangrove) because it produces huge amounts of wastewater".
- **Long-term Planning (5–20 Year Roadmaps):** The authors recommend that each government agency create a **roadmap covering 5–20 years**. This roadmap should detail specific initiatives and projects for particular areas to "better forecast resources and identify opportunities for integrating projects to achieve efficiency". Furthermore, they note that the Public Works and Town & Country Planning Office in Krabi province already utilizes a 5- to 10-year roadmap detailing their disaster preparedness and response plans.
