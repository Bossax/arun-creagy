## 📖 Executive Summary & Scientific Narrative

This qualitative study investigates the vulnerability (exposure, sensitivity, effects, and responses) and local coping mechanisms of coastal communities in Southern Thailand, comparing **Krabi (Andaman Sea coast)** and **Nakhon Si Thammarat (Gulf of Thailand coast)**. 

The study moves beyond simple engineering metrics to look at the **socio-economic, legal, and operational realities** that dictate how local communities survive chronic coastal erosion and sudden flooding. 

---

## 🔬 Key Scientific & Policy Insights (How Southern Thailand is Impacted)

### 1. Andaman (Krabi) vs. Gulf (Nakhon Si Thammarat) Vulnerability Profiles
*   **Andaman Coast (Krabi)**: Vulnerability is heavily driven by **tourism-induced waves**. Approximately **100 boats operate daily** in study subdistricts, creating constant wake waves that accelerate sandy beach erosion. Economic interests compromise warning systems—tsunami warning sirens are historically not tested during tourist peak periods to prevent visitor panic.
*   **Gulf Coast (Nakhon Si Thammarat)**: Vulnerability is driven by **severe weather (e.g., Tropical Storm Pabuk in 2019) and illegal trawling**. Local networks conduct night patrols and construct bamboo fences to keep large commercial drag-nets out of the local marine habitat.

### 2. The Hard vs. Soft Protection Battleground
*   **Hard Construction (Concrete Walls / 45° Blocks)**: Preferred by government agencies but highly contested by communities. Hard structures displace wave energy, causing **accelerated erosion in adjacent unprotected areas** (which becomes too expensive to manage). Furthermore, artificial reefs constructed from concrete and coal ash sparked major protests due to **mercury contamination** of fish.
*   **Soft Construction (Bamboo / Mangroves)**: Preferred by locals. Nakhon Si Thammarat received a **200,000 THB** budget for natural bamboo fences. However, bamboo only helps trap sand; it **fails to stop severe storm waves** and can cause physical injuries. 
*   **The Ecological Warning**: The study warns against planting red mangroves (*Rhizophora mucronata*) in restoration projects because they produce high levels of wastewater.

### 3. Legal and Institutional Barriers (The Land Title Deed Trap)
*   **The Problem**: The majority of coastal residents lack land title deeds because the coastal strip is under Marine Office jurisdiction. 
*   **The Consequence**: Because they do not own the land, **locals are prohibited from building self-protection structures and face tax penalties from the Marine Office if they try**.
*   **Compensation Inequities**: Post-storm compensation (ranging from **6,000 to 11,000 THB**) is distributed unevenly. Absentee landlords in Bangkok frequently receive payouts, while vulnerable local residents and landless fishermen (who are excluded from government registry databases) receive nothing.

### 4. Strategic Financial Recommendations
*   **The Advice**: Focus group participants recommend investing **100 million THB** in comprehensive coastal studies to map local erosion factors, arguing this would prevent far more damage than spending **10 million THB** to build temporary replacement housing after disasters occur.
*   **The Roadmap**: Government agencies should integrate and align their efforts using **5–20 year roadmaps** to forecast resources, rather than running disconnected, short-term projects.
