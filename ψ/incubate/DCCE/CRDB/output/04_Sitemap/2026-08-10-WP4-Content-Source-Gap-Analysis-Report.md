# Do We Already Have What We Need to Build the National Climate Adaptation Portal?

**Date:** 10 August 2026

## Executive Summary

The page structure for Thailand's planned national climate adaptation information portal has already been designed and agreed. The open question this report answers is more practical: for each page in that design, does the Department of Climate Change and Environment (DCCE) already own the material needed to build it, or does that material still need to be created?

The short answer: **close to 30% of the portal's planned content is genuinely ready to build from today.** About a third has *some* real, usable source material behind it, but is missing a specific piece the page promises to show — and for a meaningful slice of that third, the "real material" is raw, access-restricted, or unverified data that still needs work before it's usable, not something ready to plug in. Just under 40% has nothing at all — no dataset, no report, no existing web page that speaks to it.

This is not a criticism of the page design, which is sound. It is a stocktake of the raw material behind it, page by page, so that whoever builds the portal next knows exactly where to start, where to patch a gap, and where new information genuinely needs to be produced from scratch.

A first attempt at this stocktake, done back in July, only checked whether a page had *any* related material at all — a coarse yes/no. That approach turned out to be too generous: several pages that were marked "covered" actually only had part of what they promised. A page about recording losses that aren't purely financial — like harm to mental health, biodiversity, or cultural heritage — was marked "covered" because one document happened to discuss biodiversity. Mental health and cultural heritage were never actually addressed. This report corrects that by checking coverage at the level of what a page's content specifically promises, not just whether the page's general topic is touched anywhere.

## Why This Was Needed

The portal's page-by-page design already exists and has been reviewed and approved as the platform's structural blueprint — that part of the work is done and does not need repeating. What it does not tell anyone is whether the *content* each page promises already exists somewhere in DCCE's records, or has to be produced. An earlier pass at answering this, completed in July, tried to match pages against DCCE's known publications and systems, but never finished: the section meant to list genuine gaps was left blank, and the matching it did do was a simple yes/no per page rather than a check of whether every specific thing a page promises is actually present.

This report replaces that earlier, incomplete attempt with a finished one, grounded in DCCE's current, unified inventory of everything it owns digitally — publications, datasets, live web systems, and media — rather than an older, partial listing that had since fallen out of date with the page design it was meant to check against.

## How This Was Checked

The check ran in four steps, each building on the one before it.

**Step one — work out exactly what each page needs to show.** The page-design document describes, page by page, what content is expected to appear there — sometimes ==a single clear ask,== often several distinct things bundled into one bullet point (for example, a single line might ask for financial support tracking, technology transfer tracking, *and* capacity-building support tracking, all at once). Reading the design document directly, every one of these individual asks was pulled out separately, rather than treating a whole page as one lump requirement. This produced ==73 distinct, specific content requirements== spread across ==31 content-bearing pages== (a further set of pages in the design are pure section headers with no content of their own, and were excluded).

**Step two — check what DCCE already has for each of those 73 requirements.** DCCE maintains a single, current inventory of everything it publishes or operates digitally: ==**391 items in tota**==l, spanning formal publications, raw datasets, live web applications and dashboards, and media explainers (videos, infographics, short articles). Each of the 73 requirements was checked against this inventory for anything topically relevant. Where a real candidate existed, it was recorded by name along with a short note on why it's relevant. Where nothing relevant existed at all, that requirement was recorded as a genuine gap.

One caveat is important here: this check judged whether an item in DCCE's inventory is *about the right subject* — not whether its actual content, once opened, is ready to publish as-is or needs rewriting first. Judging that would mean reading all 391 items in full, which was out of scope for this pass. So "something exists" means "something relevant exists to start from," not "this is finished, ready-to-publish content."

**Step three — check for hidden partial coverage.** The second step, on its own, has a blind spot: if a content requirement bundles several distinct things together (as many do), the whole requirement gets marked "covered" the moment *any one* of those things finds a match — even if the rest are still missing. This was caught directly while sample-checking results: the page for recording climate-related losses that aren't measured in money — explicitly naming mental health impacts, biodiversity loss, and loss of cultural heritage as three separate examples — was marked "covered," but the only two items found both turned out to address biodiversity only. Mental health and cultural heritage were never actually covered by anything.

Once this pattern was spotted, all 39 requirements that had been marked "covered" in step two were individually re-checked against the exact wording of what they promised, to see whether every named element was genuinely accounted for, or whether the requirement was being credited with more coverage than it actually had. (The 34 requirements with no material at all were not re-checked — there is nothing partial about zero.)

**Step four — for pages that need real data, not just relevant documents, check the underlying dataset catalog too.** Steps two and three treated DCCE's 391-item inventory as a single pool — publications, media, systems, and raw datasets all counted equally as "coverage" once judged topically relevant. That's a reasonable bar for narrative pages, but it understates the risk for pages that need to be analytical, interactive, or data-driven (dashboards, maps, indices, visualization tools): a relevant *publication* about a topic doesn't mean real, structured, machine-readable *data* exists to power a chart or map. The 16 requirements of this kind were re-checked against DCCE's separate, dataset-only catalog — a stricter, 260-entry inventory built for a different work package, listing only actual datasets rather than documents, media, or web systems.

This cross-check moved several requirements. Five previously-gap items — a national risk-summary index, historical extreme-weather data, climatology variables, downscaled climate projections, and sea-level rise data — turned out to have real, if raw and access-restricted, datasets behind them, and were reclassified to partial coverage. Two already-partial items (the coastal erosion index and the loss-and-damage dashboard) turned out to have meaningfully stronger data backing than the narrative-document evidence originally cited for them.

One item's status was checked, questioned, and then confirmed after a second look: the national adaptation monitoring-and-evaluation tracker was initially flagged for downgrade because its cited inventory reference didn't appear in the narrower 260-item dataset catalog used for this cross-check. Checked directly against DCCE's own live data catalog instead, that reference turned out to be a real, current, actively maintained dataset — tied to Thailand's official climate reporting to the UNFCCC — that the 260-item extract simply hadn't captured. The page's original full-coverage rating stands. The lesson carried forward: absence from a curated subset of DCCE's holdings is not the same as absence from DCCE's holdings, and that distinction matters most exactly where this cross-check was most aggressive.

One thing this cross-check does *not* change: finding a dataset in the catalog is still not the same as having ready-to-use data. Almost everything that surfaced this way is flagged Restricted access, marked Baseline-Draft or Unverified, or delivered as raw grid/raster data that needs real processing before it could power a live dashboard or map. Every item reclassified by this step should be read as "real data exists to start from," not "ready to plug in" — the same caveat step two already applies to documents applies here too, just for a different kind of source material.

## What Was Found

Of the platform's 73 distinct content requirements:

- **21 (29%) are fully covered.** Real, relevant material exists for everything the requirement asks for.
- **24 (33%) are partially covered.** Real material exists, but a specific, named element of what the page promises is still missing — and for several of these, the "real material" is raw or access-restricted data, not finished content.
- **28 (38%) have nothing at all.** No dataset, publication, system, or media item in either of DCCE's inventories speaks to them.

>[!note] Conclusion
> So the honest starting point for anyone planning the build is that **close to 30% of the site's planned content is ready to source today**, a further third has real material that still needs work — sourcing, access negotiation, or data processing — before it's usable, and the remaining 38% has nothing at all.

### What kind of material is actually doing the work

Across everything found relevant in DCCE's 391-item document/publication inventory, the vast majority is narrative or explanatory material — formal publications, guides, and short media pieces — rather than structured, machine-readable data. The follow-up check against DCCE's separate 260-item dataset catalog found more real structured data behind the analytical/interactive pages than the document inventory alone suggested: provincial risk indices, historical climate grids, sea-level records, coastal erosion extents, and per-hazard loss records all exist there. But nearly all of it shares the same limitation — Restricted access rights, Baseline-Draft or Unverified status, or raw grid/raster format rather than a finished, dashboard-ready product. A live web application showing DCCE's climate risk map remains the only working interactive tool being reused across multiple pages.

This matters for planning purposes: pages that are essentially explanatory content (background, policy summaries, case studies) are, on the whole, in reasonable shape. Pages that are supposed to be live, data-driven features — dashboards, indices, interactive tools — have almost nothing structured behind them yet, even where the page is nominally "covered" by a document that discusses the topic in prose.

### A pattern that repeats

One gap shows up more than once in different parts of the site: wherever a page asks for **financial support, technology transfer, and capacity-building support** together, DCCE's inventory reliably has the financial piece — publications about the Green Climate Fund, the Adaptation Fund, and the Global Environment Facility all exist and are genuinely useful — but technology transfer and capacity-building tracking are consistently absent. This happens independently on the page about adaptation finance and budget tracking and again on the page about the country's adaptation roadmap. It's not a one-off; it's a structural blind spot in what DCCE currently tracks and publishes.

Similarly, wherever a page asks for **raw climate science data** — weather station records, satellite-based observations, climate projections, downscaled future scenarios — coverage looked like a total blank when checked only against the 391-item document inventory. The follow-up check against the 260-item dataset catalog found that picture was too pessimistic in one respect and confirmed it in another: real historical climate grids (1981–2023) and downscaled climate projection datasets (through 2099) do exist, moving several of these requirements from gap to partial — but they are Restricted-access, national-grid-resolution, raw data, not the ready-to-use trend statistics or projections the pages actually promise. Meanwhile land subsidence and coastal salinity intrusion data remain a confirmed, total gap in both inventories — nothing speaks to them at all, and a dedicated damage-functions library for sector-level impact modeling is equally absent everywhere.

## A Closer Look: Three Worked Examples

To make the method verifiable rather than just asserted, here are three pages examined in full.

### The Loss and Damage page

This page needs three things: an explanation of the international framework for climate-related loss and damage, a dashboard summarizing economic and physical losses from past climate events, and a record of non-economic losses such as mental health impacts, biodiversity loss, and cultural heritage loss.

What actually exists: a web page describing an international fund (established after the 27th UN climate conference) that helps developing countries respond to loss and damage, and a full assessment report DCCE commissioned — via international development cooperation — evaluating the country's climate loss and damage situation. For non-economic losses specifically, there is a media piece on how rising temperatures are expected to affect biodiversity, and an environmental-education manual that also touches on biodiversity.

All three of this page's requirements are technically "covered" in the sense that something relevant exists — ==**but none of them are fully satisfied**==. The fund description page is about financing, not really a framework explainer. The assessment report is a static document, not the kind of structured data a dashboard needs. And the non-economic losses material covers biodiversity only — mental health and cultural heritage, both explicitly asked for, have nothing behind them at all. This page is a clear example of a "covered" page that a developer would be wrong to treat as finished.

### The adaptation finance and budget tracking page

This page needs, among other things, a directory of funding sources with a cost-benefit guide for budget justification purposes, and tracking of financial aid, technology transfer, and technical assistance received from major international climate funds.

What exists: a DCCE-authored guide to climate finance sources, and individual publications describing the Adaptation Fund, the Green Climate Fund, and the Global Environment Facility, plus DCCE's own internal process documentation for endorsing Green Climate Fund project proposals. This is solid, genuinely reusable material for the *financial* half of this page.

What's missing: nothing in DCCE's inventory functions as a cost-benefit analysis guide aimed at budget justification, and — repeating the pattern noted above — nothing tracks technology transfer or technical assistance separately from money. The page will need new material specifically for those two pieces; the financial tracking content can be built from what already exists.

### The data catalog page

This page needs a searchable system for raw datasets, analytical data products, and their descriptions, meeting appropriate data-security standards.

This one is fully covered: DCCE already operates a live data catalog system, has a publication describing it, and has a data governance manual covering metadata and security standards. This is one of the few pages where the underlying infrastructure genuinely already exists and simply needs to be linked into the new portal, not rebuilt.

## What This Means for Planning the Build

For whoever plans the next phase of work, the practical takeaway is this: don't treat "some material was found" as "this page is done." The difference between full and partial coverage in this report is not academic — it's the difference between a page that just needs linking to existing content, and a page that needs a developer to write, source, or commission something new for a specific, nameable piece of it, while reusing what already exists for the rest.

The pages with nothing at all are still the clearest planning signal — but after the dataset-catalog cross-check, this list is narrower and more precisely defined than it first looked: land subsidence data, coastal salinity intrusion data, a sector-level damage-functions library, and a handful of methodology/framework documents have no source anywhere in DCCE's current holdings — neither the document inventory nor the dataset catalog. These aren't small content-writing tasks — they represent real data-collection or data-partnership work that needs to be scoped and resourced as its own effort, separate from ordinary content production.

A second category needs a different kind of planning attention: raw climate observation data, downscaled climate projections, historical extreme-weather statistics, and sea-level rise data do exist as real datasets — but they're Restricted access, Baseline-Draft/Unverified, or raw grid data with no processing pipeline into a usable form. Getting these onto the portal isn't a data-collection problem, it's a data-access-and-processing problem: negotiating access to Restricted datasets, and building the pipeline to turn raw grids into the trend statistics, indices, or dashboard values the pages actually promise.

The partially-covered pages are where careful, item-by-item planning pays off most: each one comes with a specific, named list of exactly what's still missing, rather than a vague sense that "something's not quite right." That specificity is what should drive cost and time estimates for the build phase, and it's also a natural input for deciding what DCCE should prioritize collecting or producing next, beyond just this website project.

## What This Report Does Not Do

A few limits are worth stating plainly, so the findings aren't overtrusted:

- **This checks for topical relevance, not publish-readiness.** An item being "found" for a page means it's about the right subject — not that its actual content, once opened, is written in a form ready to put on a public website. Some of what's marked "fully covered" may still need editing, translation, or reformatting before it's usable. For items surfaced by the dataset-catalog cross-check specifically, "found" often means Restricted-access or raw grid data — a further step below "ready to publish."
- **The 28 fully-missing requirements were not re-examined for partial sub-coverage**, since there is nothing to subdivide when nothing was found in the first place.
- **The dataset cross-check itself needed a correction mid-exercise**: one full-coverage item was initially flagged for downgrade because its cited asset wasn't in the narrower 260-item catalog used for that check, then confirmed correct after verifying directly against DCCE's own live data catalog. The other reclassifications from that cross-check were not re-audited against the live catalog the same way — this is a caveat on the cross-check's own reliability, not just on the underlying inventories.
- **One remaining requirement (a technology-readiness-level framework for adaptation innovation) was marked covered on a different basis than the rest of this report**: rather than matching it to a specific document, it was assumed covered as part of DCCE's confirmed, actively maintained Adaptation M&E platform, on the reasoning that anything genuinely part of that platform's scope should be assumed in scope for this page. That's a judgment call, not a verified document match — worth a spot-check before treating this item as build-ready in the same sense as the other full-coverage items.
- **The dataset-catalog cross-check only covered the 16 requirements judged analytical, interactive, or data-driven** — the remaining 57 requirements were checked only against the 391-item document/publication inventory. If any of those also turn out to need real structured data rather than narrative content, they haven't been checked against the dataset catalog and could carry the same blind spot this cross-check was built to catch.
- **A data-quality issue was found in DCCE's inventory** during this work and is worth flagging separately: at least one item's internal identifier suggests it's a raw dataset, but its actual content is a procedural guidance document, not structured data. Anyone relying on identifier patterns alone to judge what's genuinely structured data in DCCE's inventory should be aware this labeling isn't fully consistent.
- **This is a snapshot**, checked against DCCE's inventory and the page design as they stood in August 2026. Both will change as the project progresses, and this stocktake will need to be refreshed if either does.

## Appendix: Where the Underlying Data Lives

This report is a narrative summary. The full, page-by-page working data — including every specific item name, the reasoning for each match, and the internal reference codes used to trace each entry back to DCCE's source inventory — is available in the accompanying working files for anyone who needs to verify or build directly from them:

- The full list of 73 individual content requirements, extracted directly from the page design document.
- The full matching results, including every item found (or not found) for each requirement, the reasoning for each match, and the full/partial/missing coverage verdict described in this report.

Both are plain data files (not narrative) intended for technical use during the build phase, and are kept in the same location as this report.
