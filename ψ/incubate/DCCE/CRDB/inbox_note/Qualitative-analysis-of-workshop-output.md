## Relevant case studies and projects

1. **Discourse analysis of climate‑services “emergence”**
    
    - A study on the institutionalization of climate services uses discourse analysis on policy and scientific texts to show how actors frame “why climate services are needed,” more so than “what” they are or “how” they should be delivered.[sciencedirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959378016305258)
        
    - Results highlight gaps between stated needs (e.g., better decisions) and actual practice (focus on data products), which is analogous to what you want to uncover in workshop language.
        
2. **Discourse analysis for climate‑change communication**
    
    - Reviews of climate‑change communication show that discourse analysis is used to examine how different stakeholders (media, policymakers, NGOs) talk about climate risk, adaptation, and responsibility.[research.lancaster-university](https://research.lancaster-university.uk/en/publications/discourse-analysis-approaches-for-assessing-climate-change-commun/)
        
    - These studies often code narratives around “causality,” “urgency,” “responsibility,” and “agency,” which can be adapted to code workshop transcripts on climate‑service understanding.
        
3. **NLP‑based text analysis of climate‑related text**
    
    - Teaching and research projects now apply NLP (topic modelling, sentiment analysis, word clouds) to climate‑related datasets such as policy documents, news, and social‑media texts.linguistlist+1
        
    - These workflows can be reused on workshop transcripts to detect dominant themes, uncertainty language, and knowledge gaps.
        
4. **Co‑production and evaluation of climate services**
    
    - Projects such as UK Climate Resilience and EU‑funded climate‑services programmes (e.g., Climate‑Europe, ERA4CS, CONFER) use workshops and co‑evaluation to map needs and gaps in climate services.wires.onlinelibrary.wiley+2
        
    - They often combine qualitative coding of workshop outputs with structured evaluation frameworks (e.g., “readiness” of services, user trust, usability).[ukclimateresilience](https://www.ukclimateresilience.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Recommendations-UK-NFCS-AUG22.pdf)

## Common approaches and methods

You can blend these into your own light framework:

1. **Discourse and narrative analysis**
    
    - Focus on _how_ people talk about climate services:
        
        - Level of technicality (jargon vs. plain language)
            
        - Framing of risk (projected vs. lived experience)
            
        - Attribution of responsibility (who should provide/implement services).sciencedirect+1
            
    - Use techniques from critical discourse analysis (CDA) or interactional sociolinguistics to trace power, assumptions, and gaps in collective understanding.[research.lancaster-university](https://research.lancaster-university.uk/en/publications/discourse-analysis-approaches-for-assessing-climate-change-commun/)
        
2. **Thematic coding of workshop transcripts**
    
    - Start from a priori categories (e.g., “definition of climate service,” “expected use case,” “barriers,” “trust/trustworthiness”) and then allow emergent codes (e.g., “data dependency,” “local knowledge,” “institutional bottlenecks”).
        
    - The “five main stages of framework analysis” (familiarizing, coding, charting, mapping, interpreting) is widely used in policy and public‑health research and transfers well to workshop outputs.[ncbi.nlm.nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK286110/)
        
3. **NLP‑assisted pattern detection**
    
    - Once you transcribe and clean workshop notes, apply:
        
        - Topic modelling (e.g., LDA or BERT‑based models) to identify recurring themes.
            
        - Sentiment/uncertainty analysis to see where language is tentative, skeptical, or overly optimistic.
            
        - Word clouds or collocation analysis to reveal key terms and possible misalignments with “official” climate‑service definitions.eu-conexus+1
            
4. **Gap‑mapping via co‑production frameworks**
    
    - Several papers propose co‑production frameworks for climate services that separate:ukclimateresilience+1
        
        - **Knowledge** (what users say they understand)
            
        - **Function** (how they expect services to behave)
            
        - **Institutional context** (who should provide, finance, or regulate services)
            
    - You can code workshop language into these three dimensions and then visually map “strong” vs. “shallow” or absent understandings.

## Concrete workflow you can adopt

For your specific workshop outputs, you might:

1. **Transcribe and preprocess**
    
    - Convert facilitator notes, flip‑charts, and recording summaries into structured text (verbatim quotes where possible).
        
2. **Define a coding framework**
    
    - Base categories on:
        
        - _Conceptual understanding_ (What is a climate service to them?)
            
        - _Use context_ (Which sectors, time scales, decision arenas are mentioned?)wires.onlinelibrary.wiley+1
            
        - _Barriers and enablers_ (data access, trust, language/communication, institutional capacity).clearglobal+1
            
3. **Run mixed‑method analysis**
    
    - Manually code a subset of utterances per category and then
        
    - Use NLP tools (e.g., Python + scikit‑learn/transformers) to scale up topic or sentiment labelling on the rest.[eu-conexus](https://www.eu-conexus.eu/en/student-research-hub/natural-language-processing-nlp-for-climate-research-practical-text-analysis-workflows/)
        
4. **Visualize and diagnose gaps**
    
    - Create a simple matrix: rows = participant groups (e.g., policymakers, practitioners, scientists), columns = understanding dimensions (definition, use, trust, responsibility).
        
    - Flag cells where language is vague, inconsistent, or absent as “gaps to close” in your next round of capacity‑building or co‑design.ukclimateresilience+1
