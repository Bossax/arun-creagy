## 1. Background

### DCCE coordinates a system it has no authority to direct

The Department of Climate Change and Environment (DCCE) was established roughly three years ago under the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment. It is the national coordinating body for climate change adaptation, and the operative word is coordinating. For planning, it can supply tools, data, and guidelines that agencies are expected to take up in their own routines. For implementation, agencies are expected to incorporate principles into projects they own. Even risk and impact assessment could be carried out entirely by other agencies holding the relevant datasets.

What DCCE holds over the eighteen focal-point agencies spread across six sectors is a set of memoranda of understanding and the institutional standing it has accumulated over three years of work. It holds no role in those agencies' budget review or approval. It can invoke no statutory reporting duty. There is no legal instrument it can point to when an agency does not submit.

Its reach downward is similarly thin. DCCE has no dedicated provincial office. Where it needs presence outside Bangkok it relies on the Ministry's regional coordination structure, which carries several other agendas at the same time.

This matters more than it might appear, because the comparative literature is consistent on the point. Central climate coordinators without command authority succeed only when they attach climate reporting to a stronger authorising environment already operating inside government — cabinet-backed mainstreaming, statutory review, budget circulars, expenditure tagging, medium-term expenditure frameworks, or a mandatory national reporting process (Saito, 2013; Bellon & Massetti, 2022; Rogers et al., 2023). Authority to act and capacity to act interact. Without an authorising environment, technically sound indicators go unused; without staff, data systems, and reporting routines, mandates remain symbolic (Rogers et al., 2023).

### The platform exists, and the contract that built it has closed

Thailand is not starting from nothing. A national monitoring and evaluation platform was built in 2025 by Ditto (Thailand) PCL. That contract has since ended and the platform was formally handed over to DCCE, which means DCCE, not a contractor, is now the counterparty for any technical work on it.

The platform's design is more developed than its operation. Its Phase 2 Progress Report already contains indicator targets, baseline values, a weighted scoring arrangement converting achievement against target into a level, a composite index, and a dashboard. It separates a monitoring group of indicators from an evaluation group. It contains a section explicitly titled Dimension 4, covering monitoring, evaluation and learning, which describes the shift from audit-style monitoring toward learning-oriented practice supporting adaptive management, organised around whether resilience is actually increasing rather than how many projects were completed.

Against that design, the operating condition is thinner. The indicator set moved from roughly 150 to 160, up to 233 in the March 2026 report, then down to 137 by April 2026, at which point it was declared closed for at least two years. The platform remains at its first phase, is not yet connected by application programming interface (API) to source systems, and the underlying data is described by those close to it as scattered. Most indicators function as monitoring indicators in practice. The evaluation layer is aspirational.

One thing has moved. As part of platform development, DCCE gathered a first set of indicator values from the focal-point agencies. That is a single collection round rather than a repeating routine, but it means the step from design into practice has begun.

### International reporting is the driver, which shapes what gets built

The honest account of why this work is happening is compliance with international obligation. Thailand reports under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), and the adaptation reporting expectation runs through that channel. When reporting is the driver, effort concentrates on producing numbers that can be reported and on having a structure that looks complete from outside. A structure can satisfy that test while remaining hollow inside.

This is not a Thailand-specific failing. The same pattern appears across countries, and the literature records it plainly. Leiter's (2021) evidence-based stocktake found that fewer than forty per cent of countries that adopted a national adaptation plan actually monitor or evaluate its implementation, and that relying on countries' stated intentions rather than observed practice overestimates the number by a factor of three to four. Global indicators that count how many countries have a plan risk misleading policymakers into assuming adaptation is being handled (Leiter, 2021).

## 2. What the literature means by MEL in adaptation

### MEL, not MERL, is the construct the obligation is measured against

The UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience, adopted at the twenty-eighth Conference of the Parties in 2023, sets an explicit target: by 2030 all countries will have designed, established and operationalised a system for monitoring, evaluation and learning for their national adaptation efforts, and will have built the institutional capacity to run it. Three letters. That framework treats the development of a MEL system as an adaptation action in its own right, not merely as reporting overhead.

A direct check of the adaptation literature for "MERL" as a standard construct returned nothing. What the field organises around is M&E at project level, MEL at governance level, and monitoring, reporting and evaluation in global tracking contexts. The fourth letter is a local elaboration rather than the field's vocabulary. This is treated in detail in the earlier concept note and is not re-argued here.

### Monitoring, evaluation, and learning produce different things

Treating the three as one blended activity obscures what each is for. Considered separately, each produces distinct outputs.

**Monitoring** is the systematic and continuing tracking of implementation and performance, confirming whether activities are proceeding as planned and resources are being used as intended. It produces tracked indicator datasets, implementation status registers showing whether prioritised actions are on track or not, financial and compliance records, and baseline series for climate and hazard variables.

**Evaluation** uses monitoring data together with research results and external evidence to judge performance against stated goals. Where monitoring tracks implementation and trends, evaluation is a deeper periodic analysis against criteria such as relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, coherence, equity, and sustainability. It produces mid-term and end-of-term reports, contribution analyses explaining why results occurred, reviews of bottlenecks and maladaptation, and strategic recommendations.

**Learning** is defined in this literature as the collective and deliberate process of acquiring, assessing, and disseminating new knowledge that results in changed or reinforced knowledge, attitudes, and behaviours (adapted from Heikkila & Gerlak, 2013). The definition carries an important qualifier. Running the activities is not itself learning; the process must lead to a change in knowledge, attitude, or behaviour. Countries can perform monitoring and evaluation without learning, and can learn about adaptation outside monitoring and evaluation entirely, through research or training (Dekens & Harvey, 2024). Its outputs are convergent understanding across stakeholders, technical capacity held by sector staff, adjusted indicators and theories of change, and shifts at the level of assumptions or institutional rules.

### The three outputs enter the adaptation cycle at different points

Mapping those outputs onto the cycle of planning, implementation, tracking, review, and revision shows why the distinction is operational rather than academic.

Evaluation recommendations from a previous cycle, together with baseline risk information, shape the priorities of a new plan. Monitoring runs through implementation and populates the indicator framework. At review, monitoring reports establish what was done while evaluation investigates why targets were met or missed. At revision, evaluation findings justify shifts in allocation or strategic focus.

Learning sits differently from the other two. It is the only component that closes the loop back into planning. Monitoring and evaluation both terminate in information; learning is what converts information into a changed plan, a changed rule, or a changed practice. This is the single-loop and double-loop distinction in operation. Single-loop learning improves the action while holding the strategy fixed. Double-loop learning revisits the causal assumptions behind the strategy. Triple-loop learning reaches underlying norms and institutional rules (Butler et al., 2016; Gonzales-Iwanciw et al., 2020; Baird et al., 2014). A scoring dashboard, however well built, delivers only the first.

Learning also has to be designed rather than expected to happen. The literature is explicit that effective learning is deliberately structured and monitored, not left to incidental stakeholder contact (Sommerauer et al., 2026), and that it must address power, scale, and uncertainty or it becomes extractive and superficial (Tschakert & Dietrich, 2010; Fisher & Dodman, 2019; Goodwin & Olazabal, 2025). Whether learning is actually used depends on the surrounding administrative system — how coordination, accountability, and existing knowledge flows are arranged inside government (Lucy et al., 2022; Njuguna et al., 2023).

## 3. What a working national system looks like

### National systems become routine through five institutional stages

Across the comparative cases a consistent sequence appears. Countries move from adopting a strategy to running routine adaptation MEL through five stages, each triggered by a different mechanism.

The first stage is creating a national adaptation strategy and a coordinating body, triggered by political recognition or international commitment. The second is designing indicators and consulting on them, triggered by structured stakeholder consultation and technical assistance. The third is pilot or ad hoc reporting, triggered by an institutional champion or a first review cycle. The fourth is mainstreaming, where reporting becomes embedded in sector plans, budgets, and ordinary government reporting, triggered by fiscal or legal embedding into systems that already exist. The fifth is repeated cycles with recurrent learning and oversight, triggered by independent scrutiny, repetition, and accumulated administrative habit (Saito, 2013; Butler et al., 2016; Bellon & Massetti, 2022; Olazabal & Castán Broto, 2022).

The transition into the fourth stage is where most systems either become real or stall. National MEL systems rarely become operational through indicator design alone. They become routine when ministries report through systems that already bind them, when the coordinating body holds either legal review power or a fiscal entry point, and when early informal cycles have generated enough practice to justify formalising them.

### A parallel technical scale measures the machinery itself

Alongside the institutional pathway, Leiter (2021) classifies the technical maturity of a country's arrangements on an evidence-based scale. A country states an intention to undertake monitoring and evaluation. It moves to an early stage of tangible development, producing draft indicator lists, inventories of data sources, working groups, and draft institutional arrangements. It reaches an advanced stage where results frameworks, an elaborated indicator system, and final institutional arrangements exist. The system is then formally approved, which in practice requires data-sharing agreements between ministries. A progress report is published, which requires stable data flows and working focal points. Finally an evaluation is published, which requires either independent review capacity or commissioned external evaluators.

Leiter's scale also includes a category for systems whose development stalled and did not resume, which is a useful reminder that progression is not automatic. Technical design has to match a country's practical financial and technical capacity, or a mandate stays symbolic.

### Monitoring matures first, and evaluation and learning lag everywhere

A comparative study of nine countries found that all had initiated work and had at least partly functional MEL arrangements, but that operationalisation concentrated on monitoring and progress reporting while evaluation and learning remained less developed. Only France, the United Kingdom, and Vietnam had shifted attention toward evaluation and learning. The same study concludes there is no single approach that fits all countries, that MEL systems must fit national context and build on monitoring and evaluation systems that already exist, and that establishing one is an incremental process where components are added over time.

That finding is worth holding onto, because it means DCCE's current imbalance — monitoring comparatively developed, evaluation thin, learning absent — is the normal condition at this point in the sequence rather than evidence of unusual failure.

## 4. Where DCCE stands, and the constraints that follow

### DCCE is at stage two, moving weakly into stage three

Placed on the institutional pathway, DCCE sits at the second stage and is moving weakly into the third. A detailed MEL framework exists. Regular inter-ministry data submission has not become routine practice. There is no clear legal or budgetary mechanism compelling it.

The single collection round already completed means the third stage has genuinely begun rather than merely being designed for. It has not been repeated, so it does not yet constitute routine reporting.

The consequence is a scoping one. The achievable next move is not full national operation delivered inside one procurement cycle. It is the transition from the third stage to the fourth — run one or two real reporting cycles through a small number of sector agencies, then attach the reporting obligation to a budget or planning instrument those agencies already answer to, so that they report because their own annual and medium-term management requires it rather than because DCCE has asked.

What follows are the conditions that constrain how that is done.

### Nothing currently compels a line agency to report

This is the most consequential constraint. DCCE's authority rests on memoranda and accumulated standing, with no budget-review role and no statutory reporting duty available to invoke. The comparative finding that compliance requires a fiscal or legal mechanism, never persuasion alone, therefore has no existing instrument at DCCE to build on.

Thailand's public financial management system does operate budget-tagging categories. Climate change is not currently one of them. That distinction changes the shape of the problem considerably. The task is not to build tagging infrastructure from nothing but to have an adaptation category recognised inside a mechanism that already functions. The United Nations Development Programme is already engaged with Thailand on climate and gender budget tagging, which is a directly adjacent line of work rather than a cold start.

### No one in Thai government evaluates whether a project achieved its purpose

Thailand has general performance-monitoring capacity. The Office of the Public Sector Development Commission (OPDC) and the National Economic and Social Development Council (NESDC) set key performance indicators for line agencies and monitor the performance of bodies receiving budget. The Office of the Auditor General audits how budget was used. Those are real capacities, and they are not evaluation in the sense this system needs.

OPDC's arrangement checks whether an indicator met its target value. It carries no strategic review of a ministry's actual work. An agency that meets all its indicators is assessed favourably regardless of what those indicators measured or whether they meant anything for adaptation. The Auditor General audits budget-use efficiency rather than whether a project achieved what it was funded to achieve. Assessment of whether a project answered the need that justified it is done, where it is done at all, by the project owner. There is no third-party verification.

This is a harder starting position than most comparators had. Kenya could route climate monitoring through the National Integrated Monitoring and Evaluation System and draw on the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics, which already cross-checks agency submissions for anomalies and runs a sampling audit function. Indonesia had technical monitoring capacity in the National Agricultural Technology Assessment Agency and the Meteorology and Geophysics Agency. Vietnam had hydrometeorological and disaster databases managed under its Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment. DCCE building an evaluation function that judges effectiveness would be creating an institutional first rather than extending an existing practice.

There is also no convening authority yet established with the standing to resolve competing recommendations across divisions. The plausible route to creating one runs through the Climate Change Act, whose present scope is focused on mitigation.

### The plan being measured is not derived from risk

The National Adaptation Plan does not build on a Thailand-specific climate risk assessment. Which sectors count as adaptation sectors, and the strategy built on that choice, came from negotiated policy dialogue among agencies and stakeholders rather than from data or risk analysis. The action plan largely consists of asking line agencies which of their existing projects could be labelled adaptation, without a clear and consistent test for what qualifies.

The implication is uncomfortable and needs stating. If adaptation carries no shared operational definition and the plan is assembled from relabelled existing activity, then a MEL system built on top of it measures compliance labelling rather than effectiveness against climate risk. No amount of system design fixes this, because the object being measured is not stable.

### The six sectors do not share one organising logic

Adaptation reporting in Thailand is organised around the six sectors named in the National Adaptation Plan, and those sectors are the organising unit for everything — risk assessment, planning, implementation, monitoring and evaluation, and reporting all run through them. Every project's narrative is framed by which sector it belongs to.

The six are water resource management, food and agriculture, tourism and the economy, human settlement and security, public health, and natural resources. They do not share a single basis for grouping. Some are defined by a type of resource, some by an economic activity, and some by a type of vulnerability. Three different organising logics sit inside one list of six. This is the sector-level expression of the same fragmentation described above, and it means each sector likely maps to a different mixture of agencies rather than one agency to one sector.

### Two indicator systems are doing different jobs

What looks like one indicator problem is two parallel exercises with different natures.

The Global Goal on Adaptation indicators are an external reporting requirement. DCCE's task there is to map data that already exists onto a structure defined elsewhere. Alignment is partial and runs in one direction — available data determines which international indicators can be filled, rather than the framework determining what should be collected. Many of those indicators find no matching Thai reported data at all. This is the expected shape of a mapping exercise and not a design fault specific to DCCE.

The platform's own indicators are a separate set that DCCE developed, where real groundwork exists. These are mostly output indicators. There is no framework behind them capable of assessing sectoral resilience, even though that term appears in DCCE's own monitoring and evaluation guideline manual.

The practical consequence is that evaluation, when it starts, will have very little outcome-level data to work with. This is normal at this stage. Tonga's arrangement tracks 92 planned activities entirely through process indicators because baseline data for outcome indicators is lacking. Even the United Kingdom, far further along, still has about sixty per cent of its indicator list unpopulated because the data does not exist.

### The pattern to guard against is moving an untested method into operation

There is a recurring tendency at DCCE to take a methodology developed inside one project and move it straight into full operation and enforcement, without solid grounding, without testing, and without genuinely mainstreaming it first.

Each of the comparator sequencing arrangements was built to prevent exactly this. The United Kingdom ran a trial with 35 local authorities before considering a wider requirement. South Korea's legal requirement for local plans preceded the tool that made compliance possible by several years, and it was the tool that made the arrangement work. Peru gates mandatory subnational reporting on the prior publication of national pilot models and guidance.

## 5. The short-term plan

The short-term objective is one complete working example of the whole cycle, from collecting data through turning it into usable values through using those values in a real decision, achieved with the authority and institutions DCCE already holds. What follows sets out each design choice with the comparative basis for it and the alternatives that were considered and set aside.

### Start from the collection round DCCE has already run

The pilot repeats and formalises the collection of indicator values from focal-point agencies rather than designing a new exercise. The alternative — treating this as a first attempt and building fresh — was rejected because it discards work already done and mistakes DCCE's position on the pathway, which is early third stage rather than second.

### Work from the platform's own indicators rather than the international reporting list

Because the two indicator systems are doing different jobs, the pilot should build on the platform's own indicator groundwork. Mapping data to the Global Goal on Adaptation is retrofitted reporting and does not measure anything DCCE designed to track. Piloting against that list would test compliance labelling rather than testing whether the system produces usable management information.

### Take ten to fifteen indicators inside one sector

The literature's own analysis of Thailand's position proposes selecting ten to fifteen of the 137 indicators for a first operational cycle, on the reasoning that a smaller set reveals reporting bottlenecks, ownership problems, and data gaps before any wider rollout.

Those indicators should sit inside one sector rather than being distributed across several. Since the six sectors are how the entire system describes itself, a pilot organised by agency or by theme would cut against the structure everything else uses. Choosing which sector is a decision still open, and the criteria for it are drawn from three distinct entry patterns in the comparative record.

The first is entering through agriculture and natural resources, as Kenya, Vietnam, and Namibia did, where the sector's economic weight attracts donor funding and technical support and where extension services already generate local data. The second is entering through regulated infrastructure, as the United Kingdom did with water, energy, and transport organisations, which works where those bodies already hold risk-management systems and reporting habits — although in the United Kingdom's case a statutory reporting power sat behind it, which Thailand does not have. The third is Finland's low-burden route, where a coordinating ministry ran qualitative sectoral self-assessment against a simple policy-process indicator scored one to five, requiring no data infrastructure at all and no separate funding, with participating bodies covering their own costs.

Finland's case carries a warning alongside the encouragement. It shows an early operating cycle can run before any strong legal instrument exists, and it also shows such arrangements stay indicative and fragile when they depend on voluntary contribution and self-funded participation.

### Use only data agencies already report

The pilot should draw on data line agencies already submit rather than requesting new collection. Establishing new data streams requires budget to sustain them, and a pilot cannot commit DCCE to a recurring cost it has not secured. The comparative pattern points the same way, with the great majority of countries relying mainly on data already held in government records specifically to avoid asking stretched ministries for more than they can supply (`ψ/inbox/notebooklm_runs/2026-08-22_1410_round3-*`).

Because monitoring is already centralised at DCCE, evaluation should build on that same foundation, extended with contextual or supporting information DCCE can assemble itself to judge how the plan is working. Beyond budget tagging, the comparative record points to several ways existing national data can serve this purpose — embedding strategic indicators into periodic national socio-economic and ecological surveys so the official statistics process supports outcome assessment, drawing on existing hazard and exposure databases as baselines, and arranging data-sharing with the sectoral bodies that already hold operational figures such as water flow or crop yield (Njuguna et al., 2023).

### Keep the calculation simple enough to defend

Turning submitted data into indicator values should stay deliberately plain. Tonga scored 92 activities on a three-level scale of fully implemented, partially implemented, or not implemented. The comparative pattern is consistently a checklist score or straightforward count rather than a complex calculation, checked either by the body that collected it or by an existing national statistics office.

The alternative available inside the platform is the weighted zero-to-five scoring arrangement rolled into a composite index. That mechanism suits output and monitoring indicators and distorts outcome and evaluation indicators, which is a reason to keep the first cycle on a simpler basis and to establish where the data actually stands before adding weighting.

### Evaluate through a convened committee rather than an independent body

Three organisational arrangements for separating evaluation from monitoring appear in the comparative record. The United Kingdom and Ireland established independent statutory bodies, with the Climate Change Committee reporting to Parliament while the responsible department coordinates monitoring. Germany and Mexico keep monitoring with the coordinating agency and commission third-party evaluation, with Germany's Federal Environment Agency running the indicator-based monitoring while independent scientific investigators carry out strategic evaluation. France convenes an internal cross-agency committee, where the national observatory monitors annually and an Adaptation Plan Evaluation Committee drawing on civil servants, scientific representatives, and observers produces mid-term and end-of-term evaluation reports with recommendations.

The France arrangement is the appropriate model for DCCE. Thailand has no precedent for an independent third-party technical evaluator of government projects, so both the United Kingdom and German arrangements assume a kind of evaluator independence that does not exist here and would have to be created before it could be used. An independent evaluator would be more transparent in principle. A realistic starting arrangement has to fit what the bureaucracy already recognises, and a convened cross-agency committee is still a substantive improvement on evaluation performed solely by the project owner.

Two conditions attach. The committee's design has to account for the politics and power relations between participating agencies, which is a known failure point — Indonesia's arrangement stalled where authority was divided between the planning agency and the environment ministry, producing discrepancies in data, methods, and priorities (Apresian, 2024). And the method should be written so it can be handed to line agencies later, if evaluation moves toward a more distributed arrangement over time.

There is a further reason to keep the first evaluation centralised at DCCE. National systems almost never begin with monitoring and evaluation organisationally separated. They begin with one central unit performing both, typically the environment ministry or a central planning office, and formal separation appears only once the system is mature enough to have published evaluations. The three arrangements above describe what a national system grows into, not where it starts.

### Send learning to the point evaluation identifies

Learning should not be established as a standing national forum. The comparative record shows first learning activity almost never occurs at national scale when resources are constrained. Indonesia's ran in one province, testing approaches in specific districts before touching national planning, with a collaborative team acting as facilitator and knowledge broker (Butler et al., 2016). Work across Kenya, Uganda, and Ethiopia ran within the livestock sector alone. Finland's national-scale attempt was informal, unfunded, and self-assessed.

That raises an apparent tension with keeping evaluation central, and resolving it produces the operating arrangement. What DCCE measures is results, and the purpose of measuring results is to improve the processes that produced them. Those results run along a chain — national policy becomes activities, regulations, funding mechanisms, and funding criteria, and eventually determines what happens on the ground, which in turn either does or does not improve resilience.

DCCE's evaluation function is positioned to watch the aggregate signal and the middle of that chain, and to identify roughly where something is not converting into results. That is work a central body can do from data it already holds without visiting every project. Once a weak point is identified, a learning activity at the sector or project closest to that point establishes why. This matches the distributed diagnosis the literature describes, where root-cause work sits with sectoral technical units and existing review processes while central coordinators aggregate, flag, and convene rather than performing detailed causal investigation themselves (Klostermann et al., 2015; Njuguna et al., 2023).

What is learned there is then carried back by DCCE into the specific part of the chain that was identified — a funding criterion, a regulation, or how a programme is run. The transfer is a deliberate step performed by DCCE, not an automatic consequence of information existing. Indonesia's experience is explicit that local work created readiness for expansion into formal planning processes when conditions allowed, rather than flowing upward on its own (Butler et al., 2016).

For the first cycle this should stay small. One identified weak point, one sector or project-level learning activity, one specific change carried back.

A practical way to start the learning component costs almost nothing. Tonga added a single qualitative question to its existing reporting form, asking what lessons and opportunities could be drawn from the reporting period, and collated the answers. That is a functioning first learning mechanism attached to a routine that already exists.

### Run the cycle twice if the calendar allows

Tonga's most useful finding was that its quarterly reporting schedule was too frequent, and it changed to twice yearly. That finding came from running the cycle a second time, not from planning. Within 240 days two rounds is tight, so the interval between them is a real design question for the Terms of Reference rather than something to fix in advance.

### The pilot's second purpose is to identify the instrument for the fourth stage

Running one real cycle is how DCCE finds out which existing government routine could carry adaptation reporting afterward. The pilot should be scoped to produce that answer — where reporting actually stalls, who owns which gap, which data never arrives — rather than committing to a mainstreaming instrument before the evidence exists.

This turns an open question into part of the work. The literature's own assessment of Thailand's position names the same three questions as the priority research need, asking which existing Thai budget or planning instrument could credibly require annual submission of adaptation indicator data, whether current climate expenditure coding or transparency reporting could be extended to adaptation indicators at ministry level, and which ten to fifteen of the 137 indicators suit a first operating cycle.

### What the Terms of Reference should state plainly

Three things should be written into the document rather than left implicit.

That the National Adaptation Plan does not build on a Thailand-specific risk assessment, that adaptation carries no shared operational definition across sectors, and that the six sectors do not share one organising logic. A pilot that formalises reporting on relabelled existing activity without naming this would entrench the fragmentation rather than expose it.

That the pilot produces a tested method which is explicitly not ready for national enforcement. This is the guard against moving an untested method into operation.

That certain things are deliberately outside scope — full rollout across all six sectors and eighteen agencies, an independent evaluator, any new data collection, building local-level MEL as a new activity, and committing to a fourth-stage instrument before the pilot has shown which one could carry it.

## 6. The long-term plan

### Four instruments could carry reporting into the fourth stage

The transition to mainstreaming requires attaching adaptation reporting to something agencies already cannot skip. Four candidates exist, each with comparative support and known limits. The choice among them remains open and should be informed by what the pilot finds.

**Adding an adaptation category to the existing budget-tagging system.** The Philippines integrated Climate Change Expenditure Tagging into budget preparation, with the budget department issuing a circular requiring agencies to tag climate-responsive activities using specified codes. Agencies comply because tagging is a precondition of budget approval. Indonesia established budget-coded expenditure lines on similar logic (Mutiara et al., 2020; Pindiriri & Kwaramba, 2024). Since Thailand's tagging mechanism already functions and lacks only a climate category, this is the most concrete of the four. Two cautions attach. The Philippines' own audit office found in 2024 that its results-based monitoring and evaluation arrangement had yet to be institutionalised despite the tagging mechanism, and Indonesia's experience shows how divided authority between planning and environment ministries produces inconsistent data and methods (Apresian, 2024).

**Attaching to an existing mandatory planning cycle.** Tonga's climate secretariat, without a legal requirement and without a funded monitoring officer, worked with the National Planning Division to add climate questions to the mandatory quarterly corporate-planning questionnaire that agencies already had to complete. Agencies responded because the central planning authority already required the submission. This is the lowest-cost route and depends on finding a Thai equivalent, which the pilot is positioned to identify.

**Connecting to existing performance monitoring.** Kenya integrated climate monitoring into its National Integrated Monitoring and Evaluation System rather than building a parallel database, with sectoral climate change units funded from departmental budgets reporting through that channel. The Thai analogue would be OPDC or NESDC performance monitoring. The limitation identified above applies directly — OPDC checks whether targets were met without strategic review, so this route could carry the reporting flow without carrying any assessment of whether the reported activity accomplished anything.

**Routing through existing statistical infrastructure.** Thailand already operates climate-relevant statistical and accounting arrangements, including Green Gross Domestic Product accounting and sectoral emissions accounting. Using these means the capacity question is less about building data systems and more about establishing an enforceable reporting connection between agency budgets, existing datasets, and DCCE's framework.

A fifth possibility sits outside these four. Bangladesh tied reporting to fund disbursement, with its Climate Change Trust Fund requiring monthly progress reporting from implementing agencies as a condition of continued release. This works where a national climate fund exists and the coordinating body administers it, which is not currently DCCE's position.

### Local-level work is already under way and needs testing, not a starting signal

The original framing of this question assumed local-level MEL was something to begin after national arrangements matured. That is not DCCE's situation. DCCE already conducts substantial local activity through site visits and direct engagement with provinces and municipalities, running alongside national work rather than sequenced after it.

The comparative cases still matter, but for a different reason than sequencing. The United Kingdom operated its national arrangement for roughly fourteen years covering central government and large regulated organisations before extending to local authorities, and the extension was triggered by a formal recommendation in its review body's progress report, which then produced a trial with 35 local authorities before any wider requirement. South Korea's extension to seventeen metropolitan and provincial governments and 226 basic local governments was required by law, and what made it workable was the environment ministry completing a web-based implementation checking system in 2020, together with plan-establishment guidelines and a risk assessment tool. Peru ties mandatory subnational reporting to the prior publication of national pilot models and reporting guidance.

What these share is not a rule about when local work may begin. It is that national capability was in place before local reporting became an obligation. The corresponding task for DCCE is to make sure whatever local practice already exists is properly tested and grounded at whatever scale it is happening, before any of it is formalised into a national requirement.

### Evaluation can be distributed only after the method survives one cycle centrally

The long-term direction for evaluation is a more distributed arrangement, where line agencies carry out evaluation within their own domains and DCCE aggregates and convenes. That is what the distributed diagnosis model implies and what the comparative record shows mature systems doing.

The sequence matters. The method has to be developed and run centrally by DCCE first, then written in a form that line agencies can execute. Attempting distribution before the method has survived one complete cycle would hand agencies an untested instrument, which is the pattern this analysis has already identified as the main risk.

Vietnam illustrates both the ambition and the difficulty. Its framework synthesises indicators from project, provincial, and sectoral levels into a national assessment, with all 63 provincial People's Committees mandated to participate. Its first cycle found some indicators too complex or difficult to measure, with the intention of addressing this in subsequent cycles using feedback from ministries and subnational bodies.

### What would have to become true for a genuine forcing instrument to exist

Three conditions currently absent would each change what is possible.

A climate or adaptation category recognised within the existing budget-tagging mechanism would give DCCE a fiscal entry point where it now has none. The work the United Nations Development Programme is already doing on climate and gender tagging is the nearest existing thread to build from.

An adaptation reporting duty with legal standing would provide the alternative to a fiscal instrument. The available route runs through the Climate Change Act, whose current scope is focused on mitigation. Extending that scope to adaptation is a legislative question well beyond a consulting contract, but naming it as the eventual requirement is within reach of one.

A convening authority with the institutional weight to resolve competing recommendations across divisions would give the evaluation committee somewhere to send disputes. Without it, a cross-agency committee can produce recommendations but cannot settle disagreements between agencies that outrank each other's technical arguments.

None of these is deliverable inside a 240-day contract with a fixed budget of 3,000,000 baht. What is deliverable is one tested cycle, evidence about where reporting actually breaks, and a named answer to which instrument could carry the fourth stage. Naming who inherits the cycle after the contract ends, and on what recurring schedule, is the single deliverable that determines whether anything else survives.
