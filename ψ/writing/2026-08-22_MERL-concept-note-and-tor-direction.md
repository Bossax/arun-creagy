## Part 1 — What MERL actually is

### MEL is the real standard. MERL is not a recognized construct.

When COP28 adopted the UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience, it set an explicit global target — by 2030, every Party has "designed, established and operationalized a system for **monitoring, evaluation and learning** for their national adaptation efforts and have built the required institutional capacity to fully [operate it]" (target 10(d)) [NotebookLM, `2026-08-21_1615.../q2_learning.json`, source `0357bd94`]. That's MEL, three letters, and it is the actual thing Thailand's reporting obligations are measured against.

A direct check of the general adaptation-M&E literature against the term "MERL" came back clean: it isn't used as a standard construct anywhere in the sources queried. What the literature actually organizes around is M&E (the traditional project-level approach), MEL (the governance-level framework COP28 codified), and MRE — Monitoring, Reporting and Evaluation, a term used in global tracking contexts [NotebookLM, `q3_research_component.json`]. When DCCE encountered "MERL" and wondered what the fourth letter does, that instinct was correct — the acronym isn't the field's vocabulary. It's a local elaboration.

### What Monitoring and Evaluation actually separate on

The most rigorous empirical basis available — an OECD review of 106 adaptation project documents across six bilateral development agencies (OECD, *ENV/WKP(2011)8*) — draws the Monitoring/Evaluation line on more than "does a target exist." Monitoring is "the systematic collection of data on pre-defined project or programme indicators," an ongoing check on whether an initiative is on track. Evaluation "measures change over time, as well as the strengths and weaknesses of project or programme design" [NotebookLM, `q1_monitoring_vs_evaluation.json`, citations 1–2].

Six real dimensions separate them, per that review:

- **Attribution.** Evaluation has to confront the "attribution gap" — outcomes rarely trace to one intervention, so evaluation typically substitutes *contribution* analysis (theory-of-change reasoning plus mixed evidence) for causal proof, because rigorous attribution studies in adaptation are "negligible" [citations 4, 6].
- **Value judgment.** There is no field-wide consensus on what "successful adaptation" even means, so evaluators inevitably inject subjective judgment, especially against vague target concepts like "resilience" or "wellbeing" [citations 5, 7, 8].
- **Timing.** Monitoring runs continuously through a project's active life. Evaluation needs a much longer horizon, because "for many adaptation initiatives no discernible change may take place over the lifetime of the project" — climate outcomes lag, so credible evaluation often has to happen years after a project closes, not the standard 2–5 years used elsewhere in development evaluation [citations 3, 11, 13].
- **Methodology.** Monitoring runs on Results-Based Management and logframes. Evaluation needs surveys, focus groups, and direct consultation to assess ambiguous constructs [citations 9, 15, 16].
- **Who conducts it.** Internal staff bring contextual knowledge; external evaluators bring objectivity — a real trade-off, not a formality [citation 17].

None of this requires a target value to exist. What it requires is a **stated basis for judgment set in advance** — a target is one form of that; a recorded theory-of-change assumption ("we expect X to produce Y because we believe Z") is another, and in adaptation, often the more honest one, because targets set against a moving climate baseline can mislead in both directions — hiding good work behind a missed number, or hiding maladaptation behind a hit one.

### What Learning is, distinct from watching data

Learning shows up in the literature as a **reflexive, structural process** — not a synonym for evaluation, and not simply "look at the results." Its defining feature is that it changes the goals and instruments themselves, not just the effort applied to them: a genuine learning loop "allows for recalibration of goal/instrument configurations (that is, adding new instruments and changing goals)" [NotebookLM, `q2_learning.json`, citation 5]. The foundational GEF framing of this goes back further: an adaptation M&E system exists "to identify aspects that are working, those that are not working, and the reasons why, as well as providing mechanisms to adjust the adaptation process accordingly" [citation 7]. And because adaptation goals themselves should evolve as vulnerabilities and ambitions change, the literature treats "assessing progress in goals and targets" as itself evidence of "the reflexive nature of government MRE and collective learning" [citation 1].

This is the single-loop/double-loop distinction in practice. Single-loop is: missed the target, push harder, same strategy. Double-loop is: missed the target, and now the target, the strategy, or the underlying belief about what drives resilience gets revisited. A scoring dashboard, even a fully operational one, only ever delivers the first. The UAE Framework's own MEL indicators make the distinction visible institutionally — it tracks not just whether a MEL system was *designed*, but its *level of operationalization*, whether findings get *periodically published*, and critically, the "level of **integration** of monitoring, evaluation and learning system findings into national adaptation efforts" [citation 8]. Integration back into the plan is the whole point; a system that only reports numbers never reaches it.

### Where "Research" earns a distinct place — and it isn't retrospective literature review

Where the reviewed literature does isolate a distinct Research category (the OECD's own empirical coding of agency project portfolios includes it as a fifth activity type, alongside risk reduction, policy management, education/awareness, and coordination), its job is specific and forward-facing, not backward-facing:

- **Building baselines that account for a moving target.** Because "baseline climatic risks evolve under climate change," research is what produces climate predictions under different scenarios — projections, maps, vulnerability indices — so that evaluation isn't judged against a baseline that's already stale by the time results come in [NotebookLM, `q3_research_component.json`, citations 9–11].
- **Directing where effort should go, before the fact.** Research "supplies information that is necessary to understand where training, policy and risk reduction activities are most needed" — an ex-ante function evaluation, which is retrospective by definition, cannot perform [citation 12].
- **Closing the "rationale gap."** A large-sample study of 226 adaptation policies found that "risk and vulnerability assessments are rarely connected with adaptation decision-making" — meaning plans get written without the underlying risk research actually shaping them. This is described as a direct cause of weak M&E later on: "there is a general lack of knowledge regarding whether and how specific measures increase the capacity to adapt or reduce risks... and in which measure" [citation 14]. Research's job here is to make sure the plan and the evidence are actually talking to each other before implementation starts, not after.

The 2026 MERL TOR's own Research task (§5.1.3: review ≥10 existing studies on adaptation M&E methodology) points the opposite direction — backward, at methodology literature, rather than forward, at the specific climate-risk projections the sectors would need to set credible targets. Taking the field's own definition of what "R" is for, this is the one place the TOR's central innovation is aimed at the wrong target.

### What's already in DCCE's hands

None of this is being built from zero. The 2025 M&E Platform's Phase 2 Progress Report — written by DCCE's own prior contractor, Ditto (Thailand) PCL, a year before the MERL TOR was drafted — already contains an elaborate design:

- Indicator targets, stored as required data (*"ค่าตัวชี้วัดรายปี ค่าเป้าหมาย ค่าพื้นฐาน"* — annual indicator values, target values, baseline values) [NotebookLM, `2026-08-21_1600.../q1_targets.json`, citation 3].
- A weighted 0–5 scoring system converting percent-of-target achievement into a level, rolled up into a composite index and displayed on a traffic-light dashboard [same file, citations 4–6, 12–13, 16].
- A formal split between a **Monitoring group** of indicators (output-focused, sourced from sector agencies) and an **Evaluation group** (outcome-focused, meant to "answer the national and international climate strategy") [citation 10] — the same output/outcome distinction the general literature treats as foundational.
- Most importantly, an explicit **"Dimension 4: Monitoring, Evaluation and Learning (MEL)"** section that names the shift the 2026 TOR claims to be introducing: *"มิตินี้เปลี่ยนมุมมองจาก M&E แบบเดิมที่เน้นการตรวจสอบความรับผิดชอบ (Accountability/Audit) มาสู่ MEL ที่เน้นการเรียนรู้ (Learning-oriented MEL) เพื่อสนับสนุนการบริหารจัดการแบบปรับตัว (Adaptive Management)"* — this dimension moves the system from traditional audit-style M&E to learning-oriented MEL supporting adaptive management, organized around one question: *"เรามีความยืดหยุ่นเพิ่มขึ้นจริงหรือไม่?"* — are we actually becoming more resilient? — not how many projects got finished [citation 8].

Set against this, DCCE's operating reality a month later (an April 2026 launch-event transcript, Asst. Prof. Dr. Pawitr Chuenchoom of Chulalongkorn, already summarized in `ψ/writing/2026-08-21_MERL-TOR-prior-project-context.md`): the indicator set moved from roughly 150–160, to 233 in the March 2026 report, down to **137 by April, declared closed for at least two years** — *"เราไม่มีโอกาสแก้ตัวชี้วัดแล้ว"*. The platform is confirmed still "phase one," not yet API-connected, and data is described in his own words as scattered — *"การขาดแคลนข้อมูล... หรือมีมันก็กระจัดกระจายมาก."* Most indicators remain monitoring-only in practice; the evaluation-grade layer is aspirational.

### The actual gap: operationalization, not a missing concept

Put the design document next to the operating reality and the shape of the problem changes. DCCE doesn't lack a concept of evaluation, or a concept of learning, or even a concept of research-informed target-setting — Ditto's own Dimension 4 already states, almost word for word, what MERL claims to newly introduce: comparing results to stated hypotheses, understanding what factors drove change, adjusting policy accordingly. What DCCE lacks is **execution** — targets that were never actually entered by agencies, a scoring mechanism that's never actually run, and a learning loop that exists as a report chapter, not a standing institutional practice.

Framed this way, "MERL" isn't adding a missing letter to an incomplete acronym. It's DCCE noticing, correctly, that something about its M&E isn't working — and reaching for a new label rather than diagnosing why the system it already designed never turned on.

### Who should own root-cause analysis — and what makes agencies actually comply

Working through this in conversation, a distributed model was proposed: M&E's job is to flag deviation — evidence of what fell short — not to explain it. Root-cause investigation belongs to whichever of the other three adaptation-cycle functions (risk/vulnerability assessment, planning, implementation) has the domain standing to explain it, each working within their own scope. Findings pool centrally, and a decision-maker resolves competing accounts into one institutional conclusion — including, where warranted, revising targets or what "resilience" is even taken to mean. This model treats the political convening of that decision as itself the learning event, not a side effect of it.

This is not a hypothetical design — it matches how real coordinating bodies with no command authority over line ministries actually operate:

- **Germany.** UBA (the Federal Environment Agency) runs the DAS monitoring report but explicitly does not evaluate the effectiveness of specific measures, because implementation "falls under the responsibility" of the competent ministries; UBA works "in close cooperation with the responsible ministries" rather than auditing them [Perplexity `ask`, 2026-08-21_15-53-52]. What compels ministries to supply data is not a shared "adaptation cycle" — it's the 2024 Federal Climate Adaptation Act (KAnG), which creates a statutory duty to produce the report, largely by pulling from statistics ministries already collect for other reasons (health, transport, agriculture), moving toward automatic updating rather than new manual reporting [Perplexity `ask`, 2026-08-21_15-52-49].
- **United Kingdom.** The Climate Change Committee is a statutory external assessor, not an implementer — it flags weak progress and recommends government "coordinate relevant delivery and statistics agencies" to actually gather the evidence. Compliance is compelled by the Climate Change Act's Adaptation Reporting Power, which lets the Secretary of State legally direct named "reporting authorities" to report, with parliamentary accountability behind the CCC's biennial findings [same source].
- **Philippines.** The Climate Change Commission leads NCCAP's M&E but is explicitly not the root-cause investigator — that sits with implementing agencies and local government units, with the Commission compiling and reporting gaps [Perplexity `ask`, 2026-08-21_15-53-52]. What makes it work operationally is Climate Change Expenditure Tagging: tagging climate-relevant budget lines is a precondition of the annual budget process itself, embedded in the General Appropriations Act — agencies comply because non-compliance risks fund release, not because they've adopted a shared adaptation calendar [Perplexity `ask`, 2026-08-21_15-53-21].

Two things follow from this, and the second is a genuine caution rather than a clean success story. First: the distributed root-cause model isn't a departure from real practice, it's what real practice actually does — no coordinating body diagnoses causes itself, and DCCE shouldn't expect its own M&E division to either. Second: the Philippines' Commission on Audit found in 2024 that its NCCAP Results-Based M&E System "has yet to be institutionalized," citing missing KPIs, incomplete agency reporting, and weak expenditure tagging [Perplexity `ask`, 2026-08-21_15-53-21] — meaning even the country with the closest structural analog to DCCE's situation, and a real budget-linked forcing mechanism, is still fighting the same operationalization problem years in. Precedent shows the right shape of the solution; it doesn't prove the problem is easy.

### The missing piece is a forcing mechanism, not a fourth letter

None of the three countries got sector-ministry compliance by inventing a parallel institutional rhythm and asking ministries to adopt it. Each attached climate reporting to something ministries could not skip regardless of climate policy — a budget precondition, or a statutory duty backed by legal consequence. DCCE's current platform has neither. The 18 focal-point agencies are asked to log in and enter numbers, and nothing consequential follows if they don't. That absence — not a missing concept, not a missing acronym letter — is the actual, structural reason the Dimension 4 design sits unused a year after it was written.

## Part 2 — Redirecting the TOR: a direction, not a mandate

What follows is a story of where the TOR should point, not a clause-by-clause rewrite. The current TOR is already over-specified in some places (rigid deliverable counts, event headcounts) and under-specified in the places that actually matter (what the research is for, who owns the outcome, what "evaluation" produces). The fix isn't to tighten every line — a TOR this locked leaves a future contractor, or DCCE itself, no room to adjust once the actual condition of the platform becomes clearer through the work. The fix is to point the contract at the right target and leave room to discover the path.

**The target is activation, not invention.** The TOR's rationale currently reads as though DCCE is missing a concept — as though MEL needs a fourth letter to become adequate. The evidence says otherwise: DCCE has a MEL design, written by its own prior contractor, sitting unused. The honest premise for this contract is closer to: *find out why Dimension 4 never turned on, and turn it on.* That reframing changes what "Research" is for — not a literature review of ten M&E studies, but the forward-looking risk and scenario work needed to set targets that will still mean something as the climate baseline shifts, which is the one place the field's own literature says "R" does real work no other letter does.

**The qualifications should ask for the depth this actually requires.** A contract asking someone to diagnose why an evaluation system never activated, and to do genuine climate-risk research, needs people who've done that kind of work before — not just legal capacity to hold a government contract. This isn't a new lesson for DCCE to learn; it's the same lesson the CRDB TOR review already wrote down five months earlier for a different DCCE procurement, and it's worth reusing rather than rediscovering.

**The convening events already in the budget can carry more weight than they currently do.** Three forums and a workshop are funded regardless of anything else in this note. At least one of them could be the actual mechanism by which the 18 focal-point agencies enter real target values against the closed 137-indicator set — turning a presentation into the operational step the platform has been missing, at close to no additional cost, since the room and the people are already being paid for.

**On the site visit — it should stay, but its objective needs to change.**

This was worked through at length, and the reasoning is worth carrying forward rather than compressing into a single line. The first instinct was skepticism: a single visit, one session, six areas, cannot establish a before-and-after, and doubling it to two rounds ran straight into the contract's own payment schedule — Milestone 2 falls at day 150, only ninety days after the workplan is even approved, which rules out a second physical round spaced far enough apart to observe anything real. Pushing further, a sharper objection surfaced: if the actual problem is that eighteen agencies aren't feeding data into a platform, that problem lives inside those agencies' own offices and workflows — not in a flood-prone district or a drought-affected tourism site. A trip to a physical location doesn't test whether a contact person in an agency office logs into a dashboard.

But the TOR's own language in this section isn't actually asking that question. §5.2.3–5.2.4 asks for an analysis of gaps, constraints, and support needs *of the study area itself*, and for findings that link policy agencies, sector agencies, area-level agencies, and the study area together. That's genuinely place-bound — how a national plan lands, or fails to land, in a specific locality is not something any database or head-office interview can substitute for. So the visit is justified, but for a narrower and more concrete purpose than either "test the national platform" or "produce a resilience finding in five months": it should trace where each sector's indicator data would actually originate at that location — who holds the underlying number, in what form, on what cycle, and what it would take to get it flowing — which is exactly the question this session's discussion could not resolve from Bangkok, and which a single well-designed visit can answer.

That reframing carries three practical consequences. The visit's method should be specified rather than left as "a meeting or dialogue forum" — a structured interview with the office that actually holds the data, and a walkthrough of their records, alongside whatever consultation happens. Site selection should favor areas where a relevant project is already mid-implementation and the agency's own area office is the natural data source, so one visit can serve both the area-level diagnostic and the data-tracing purpose at once, rather than treating them as separate problems needing separate trips. And the two-round instinct doesn't have to be abandoned — it can be redirected into the otherwise-idle days 150–240 of the contract, as a *remote* verification: does the area keep submitting through whatever mechanism the visit helped design, once nobody from the consultant team is standing in the room. That tests the one thing a second physical visit would have tested, inside the calendar the contract actually has, without doubling the travel budget.

**The evaluation section should measure the system, not the audience.** Right now §5.6 asks what percentage of a target audience used the knowledge — a proxy for awareness, not for whether MEL turned on. The more honest measures are operational: how many of the 137 indicators have an actual target entered, how many sectors completed one full monitor-evaluate-adjust cycle, what the underlying data completeness rate is by the end of the contract. These are observable, and they're the ones that actually answer whether this project did what its own name claims.

**And someone has to own it afterward.** Every piece of this — the activation, the target-setting, the area-level tracing, the remote verification — depends on someone at DCCE continuing to run the cycle after day 240. Precedent from Germany, the UK, and the Philippines all point the same direction: without a forcing mechanism tied to something agencies can't skip, the loop stops the day the consultant leaves, the way Ditto's own Dimension 4 stopped a year ago. That forcing mechanism — a budget linkage or a legal reporting duty — is very likely bigger than what a 240-day, ฿3,000,000 consulting contract can build outright. But naming who inherits the loop, and on what recurring schedule, is well within this contract's reach, and it's the one deliverable that determines whether everything else in it outlives the contract at all.
