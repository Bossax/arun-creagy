### Slide 2 — What MEL means

**Heading:** What MEL means

**Subheading:** Monitoring, evaluation and learning are three connected functions, not one blended activity, and MEL is the actual global standard.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — MEL is monitoring, evaluation, and learning working together — three functions, not one activity.
- **L2** — Why MEL is the standard term. A one-line preview of the three functions, detail comes next slide.
- **L3** — The note on "MERL."

**On-slide text**

> **What MEL means**
>
> Monitoring, evaluation and learning are three connected functions, not one blended activity, and MEL is the actual global standard.
>
> **Monitoring** &mdash; Is it on track?
> **Evaluation** &mdash; Did it work?
> **Learning** &mdash; What changes next?
>
> The 2023 UAE Framework for Global Climate Resilience sets a 2030 target for every country to have a working MEL system in place. Three letters — MEL is the term the obligation is actually measured against.
>
> A note on "MERL": some of DCCE's own documents add a fourth letter, for Research. That is DCCE's own framing, not the global standard.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 2. This slide exists purely to establish shared vocabulary before anything else is said — keep it brief, a few sentences, not an argument. If the MERL point raises questions, note only that Research is not treated as a separate stage anywhere in the literature; the full reasoning is in the report, not this deck.

### Slide 3 — What M, E and L produce

**Heading:** What M, E and L produce

**Subheading:** Each of the three produces something different.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — Monitoring, evaluation and learning are three distinct functions with three distinct outputs.
- **L2** — The three rows.
- **L3** — The qualifier on learning.

**On-slide text**

> **What M, E and L produce**
>
> Each of the three produces something different.
>
> **Monitoring** — continuous tracking of whether implementation matches plan
> Produces: indicator data, status registers, financial records, baselines
>
> **Evaluation** — periodic judgment against relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, equity
> Produces: mid-term and end-term reports, contribution analysis, bottleneck reviews, recommendations
>
> **Learning** — deliberate collective process that changes knowledge, attitudes or behaviour
> Produces: shared understanding, sector capacity, revised indicators and assumptions, institutional change
>
> Running learning activities is not learning. The process has to produce a change.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 2. The closing line is the one to emphasise — a country can monitor and evaluate without learning at all, and this is the normal outcome, not the exception.

### Slide 4 — How the outputs move

**Heading:** How the outputs move

**Subheading:** Learning is the only component that closes the loop back into planning.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — Monitoring and evaluation both end in information. Only learning turns information into a changed plan.
- **L2** — Which component feeds which point in the cycle.
- **L3** — What a dashboard alone can and cannot deliver.

**On-slide text**

> **How the outputs move**
>
> Learning is the only component that closes the loop back into planning.
>
> Planning &#8594; Implementation &#8594; Indicator tracking &#8594; Review &#8594; Revision &#8594; back to Planning
>
> **Planning** draws on previous evaluation recommendations and baseline risk information.
> **Implementation and tracking** run on monitoring.
> **Review** uses monitoring reports to show what happened, and evaluation to show why.
> **Revision** uses evaluation findings and learning.
>
> A dashboard, however complete, only ever says push harder on the same strategy.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 2. This is the single-loop and double-loop distinction in plain words. Single-loop is missing the target and applying more effort to the same approach. Double-loop is missing the target and reopening the strategy or the assumption behind it.

### Slide 5 — How systems like this develop

**Heading:** How systems like this develop

**Subheading:** Countries build a working MEL system through five stages, and a different trigger moves each stage to the next.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — This is a known, repeated pattern across very different countries — not something unique to Thailand.
- **L2** — The five stages and their triggers.
- **L3** — None yet — this slide is deliberately generic, DCCE is not mentioned.

**On-slide text**

> **How systems like this develop**
>
> Countries build a working MEL system through five stages, and a different trigger moves each stage to the next.
>
> *[Five-stage bar, generic, unmarked]*
> **1** Strategy and coordinating body — triggered by political recognition or international commitment
> **2** Indicator design and consultation — triggered by stakeholder consultation and technical assistance
> **3** Pilot reporting — triggered by an institutional champion running a first cycle
> **4** Mainstreaming into budgets and plans — triggered by a fiscal or legal instrument
> **5** Repeated cycles with independent oversight — triggered by accumulated administrative habit
>
> This pattern shows up across very different countries. It is not unique to Thailand.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 3. This is the map the next slide places DCCE on. Deliver it as a neutral, generic pattern — no DCCE content yet — so the positioning on the following slide lands as "here is where a known map puts a known country," not as new information to absorb all at once.

### Slide 7 — Authority and instruments

**Heading:** Authority and instruments

**Subheading:** Nothing currently requires a line agency to report.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — DCCE has no instrument that compels reporting, and the comparative record says persuasion alone does not work.
- **L2** — Five specific gaps.
- **L3** — The tagging point, the one piece of good news here.

**On-slide text**

> **Authority and instruments**
>
> Nothing currently requires a line agency to report.
>
> Authority is memoranda and accumulated standing. No budget-review role, no statutory reporting duty.
> No independent evaluator of project effectiveness exists anywhere in Thai government.
> No convening authority with standing to resolve disputes between divisions.
> No dedicated provincial presence.
>
> **Partial** — Budget tagging categories already work. Climate is not one of them.
>
> Who does what today: the Office of the Public Sector Development Commission (OPDC) and the National Economic and Social Development Council (NESDC) check whether targets were met. The Office of the Auditor General audits budget use. Project owners assess themselves.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 4. The tagging point reframes the task from building infrastructure to having a category recognised inside something that already works. On the evaluator point, this is a harder starting position than the comparators had — Kenya could route through an existing national monitoring system with a statistics bureau that already checks agency submissions; we cannot.

### Slide 8 — What is being measured

**Heading:** What is being measured

**Subheading:** The plan being measured comes from policy dialogue rather than risk analysis.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — The object of measurement is not stable, so a MEL system built on it measures compliance labelling rather than effectiveness against climate risk.
- **L2** — Four findings.
- **L3** — The consequence line.

**On-slide text**

> **What is being measured**
>
> The plan being measured comes from policy dialogue rather than risk analysis.
>
> The National Adaptation Plan does not build on a Thailand-specific climate risk assessment.
> Sector scope and strategy were negotiated, not derived from data.
> The action plan is largely existing agency projects relabelled as adaptation, with no consistent test for what qualifies.
>
> **The six sectors follow three different logics**
> Resource type — water resource management, natural resources
> Economic activity — food and agriculture, tourism and the economy
> Vulnerability type — human settlement and security, public health
>
> A system built on this measures compliance labelling, not effectiveness against risk.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 4. Deliver plainly — this is the uncomfortable slide and the whole point of an internal deck is that we can say this here. The consequence is what matters: no system design fixes an unstable object of measurement. It has to be named in the Terms of Reference.

### Slide 9 — What other countries did

**Heading:** What other countries did

**Subheading:** Every country that made reporting routine attached it to something agencies already could not skip.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — Nobody invented a parallel climate reporting process and asked ministries to adopt it.
- **L2** — The six cases and what each attached to.
- **L3** — The Finland and Philippines caveats.

**On-slide text**

> **What other countries did**
>
> Every country that made reporting routine attached it to something agencies already could not skip.
>
> **Tonga** — five questions added to the planning office's existing mandatory quarterly report. No legal requirement, no funded post; agencies answered because the submission was already required.
> **Philippines** — budget circular making climate tagging a condition of budget approval. Compliance was structural; the audit office still found the monitoring arrangement not institutionalised.
> **Kenya** — integration into the existing national monitoring system. Worked because a statistics bureau already verified agency submissions.
> **Bangladesh** — reporting tied to disbursement from a national climate fund. Needs a fund the coordinating body administers.
> **Finland** — voluntary qualitative self-assessment, no separate funding. Ran before any legal instrument existed; stayed indicative and fragile.
> **France** — central body monitors, convened cross-agency committee evaluates. Works without an independent evaluator existing.
>
> None of them built a parallel process and asked ministries to adopt it.

**Speaker notes**

Report sections 5 and 6. Finland is the case closest to our authority position, and it cuts both ways — an operating cycle can run before legal force exists, and it also stays fragile without one. The Philippines caveat matters too: even a real budget-linked mechanism did not, by itself, institutionalise the monitoring system.

### Slide 10 — The operating model

**Heading:** The operating model

**Subheading:** Monitoring and evaluation stay national. Diagnosis and learning go to whichever sector or project the evidence points to.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — This is the target design in one picture — scale, data collection, processing, and use of results — before the pilot proposal that works toward it.
- **L2** — The chain, and the four-part breakdown.
- **L3** — The repeating-cycle line.

**On-slide text**

> **The operating model**
>
> Monitoring and evaluation stay national. Diagnosis and learning go to whichever sector or project the evidence points to.
>
> *[Chain diagram: Policy &#8594; Activities &#8594; Regulations &#8594; Funding mechanisms &#8594; Funding criteria &#8594; Ground-level results &#8594; Resilience outcome, with DCCE's watching span drawn across the whole chain, one arrow dropping down to a sector/project box, and an arrow carrying the answer back up into the chain]*
>
> **Scale** — monitoring and evaluation centralised at DCCE. Diagnosis and learning at whichever sector or project the evidence points to. Never a standing national forum.
> **Collection** — only what line agencies already report. No new data streams; those need budget the system does not have.
> **Processing** — a plain score or count, not a complex calculation. Checked by DCCE itself or an existing national statistics body.
> **Use** — carried back into the specific point in the chain that was flagged — a funding criterion, a regulation, how a programme is run. Never left as a report nobody acts on.
>
> This is a repeating cycle. Evaluation flags where to look. Learning finds out why. DCCE carries the answer back. The next cycle checks whether it worked.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 5, the "send learning to the point evaluation identifies" material, generalised into the target design. This slide states, once, the picture the rest of the deck has been building toward and that the pilot on the next slide is a first step into — not a separate idea. If asked why evaluation is central but learning is not: evaluation is the watching job, working from data DCCE already has. Learning is the finding-out-why job, which needs people close enough to a specific problem to do anything real. The two are different jobs, not different tiers of importance.

### Slide 11 — What to do first

**Heading:** What to do first

**Subheading:** One complete cycle, inside one sector, using only data agencies already report.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — Run one complete cycle end to end, with the authority DCCE already has, as the first turn of the operating model just described.
- **L2** — Seven design choices.
- **L3** — The rationale behind France, the learning handoff, and running twice.

**On-slide text**

> **What to do first**
>
> One complete cycle, inside one sector, using only data agencies already report.
>
> Collect &#8594; Score &#8594; Evaluate &#8594; Identify weak point &#8594; Learn &#8594; Carry back
>
> **Start** from the collection round already completed. Do not design fresh.
> **Indicators** — ten to fifteen, from the platform's own set, not the international reporting list.
> **Scope** — one sector, because sectors are how the whole system describes itself.
> **Data** — only what agencies already submit. New streams need budget to sustain.
> **Scoring** — plain three-level, not the weighted composite index.
> **Evaluation** — a convened cross-agency committee, following France.
> **Learning** — sent to whichever sector or project the evaluation identifies. Not a standing national forum.
>
> Run it twice if the calendar allows.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 5. This is the operating model from the previous slide, run once, at the smallest scale that produces a real answer. On evaluation, the United Kingdom and German arrangements both assume an evaluator that is independent or commissioned, and neither exists in Thailand — a convened cross-agency committee is still a real improvement on the project owner assessing itself. On running twice, Tonga only discovered its quarterly schedule was too frequent by running the cycle a second time.

### Slide 12 — Getting to stage four

**Heading:** Getting to stage four

**Subheading:** The first cycle is also how we find out which instrument could carry reporting afterward.

**Message hierarchy**
- **L1** — Leaving the instrument choice open is deliberate. Running one real cycle is how the answer gets found.
- **L2** — Four candidates with their limits.
- **L3** — The correction on local work. The sequence for distributing evaluation.

**On-slide text**

> **Getting to stage four**
>
> The first cycle is also how we find out which instrument could carry reporting afterward.
>
> **Add an adaptation category to existing budget tagging** — Philippines, Indonesia. The mechanism already runs. Watch for divided authority between planning and environment ministries.
> **Attach to an existing mandatory planning cycle** — Tonga. Lowest cost. Depends on finding the Thai equivalent.
> **Connect to OPDC or NESDC performance monitoring** — Kenya. Carries the reporting flow but not the assessment.
> **Route through existing statistical work** — Green GDP, sectoral emissions accounting, national surveys.
>
> What the pilot produces toward this — where reporting stalls, who owns which gap, which data never arrives.
>
> Local work is already happening. The task is testing what exists before formalising it, not waiting for a signal to begin.

**Speaker notes**

Report section 6. The literature's own analysis of Thailand names this exact question as the priority research need — running a real cycle is how it gets answered rather than guessed. On local work, correct the assumption directly if it comes up: DCCE already runs site visits and engages provinces and municipalities; the question was never when to start.
